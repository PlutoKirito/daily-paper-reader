ABSTRACT
Reinforcement learning (RL) for video generation usually assigns one scalar re-
ward to an entire sampled video. Yet a video is not uniformly flawed: some
visual tokens may already satisfy the prompt, whereas others require correction.
A scalar reward cannot localize errors, causing optimization to perturb satisfac-
tory tokens while under-targeting the tokens that actually need to change. We
introduce Token-Level Video Reinforcement Learning, TVRL, a framework that
derives token-level credit from the reward being optimized. Our key insight is that
the answer likelihood of a frozen vision-language model provides both signals:
its outputs contribute to the video-level reward, while magnitudes of its video-
input gradients reveal which generated video tokens most affect that score. We
instantiate TVRL in Group Relative Policy Optimization by averaging prompt-
derived question rewards into one group-relative advantage and using detached,
question-conditioned token-credit maps to reweight dense denoising-transition
log-probabilities inside the clipped policy ratio. On VBench-2.0, TVRL achieves
an Overall score of 57.69, outperforming the base model by 3.60 points. TVRL
also improves matched GRPO baselines across three SDE samplers (SAGE, Flow,
and Dance) by 2.68–3.15 points and across four reward models (VideoAlign,
VideoScore2, UnifiedReward2, and Qwen3.5-9B) by 1.33–3.15 points.

Despite this progress, video RL faces
a fundamental credit-assignment bot-
tleneck. Training must determine not
only whether one trajectory is better
than another, but also when and where
the reward-relevant evidence appears.
A video-level scalar reward can rank
completed denoising trajectories, but it
cannot indicate which video tokens are
responsible for success or failure (Wu
et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2026; Liang
et al., 2026) (Figure 2).
This limita-
tion matters because the evidence for
many prompt requirements is confined
to a small region or a few frames, for
example, whether the requested grand
piano is rendered as a piano rather than
a generic keyboard (Figure 1), whether a watch case is rose gold, whether the described fireflies
appear in the scene at all, or whether the camera pans to the right instead of staying static. Broad-
casting the same scalar advantage to every latent token therefore spends update capacity on regions
that have nothing to do with the evaluated error. Recent work separates the video-level reward from
token credit (Ni et al., 2026), but derives credits from perceptual features independent of rewards.
The missing quantity is a routing signal tied to the reward itself. Score gradients provide such a
signal because they measure how sensitive an evaluator’s score is to local changes in the video-
frame input (Xu et al., 2023; Clark et al., 2023; Prabhudesai et al., 2023; 2024). Prior differentiable
reward methods typically use these gradients to update the generator directly. In GRPO, we use
them differently: after detachment, their magnitudes weight denoising log-probabilities, while the
group-relative advantage remains the scalar RL signal. This preserves the clipped policy-gradient
structure of GRPO and avoids backpropagating the reward loss through the generator. The remaining
requirement is semantic alignment: the score being differentiated must represent the requirement
whose credit we want to localize.
This score–credit alignment makes the choice of evaluator interface central. Custom video reward
models provide differentiable scalar feedback, but a single scalar head does not expose which se-
mantic requirement its gradient should explain (Wang et al., 2026). We therefore use an evaluator
whose score is tied to an explicit video-grounded question, so the reward and the sensitivity map are
computed from the same semantic check. Pretrained VLMs (Qwen Team, 2026; Bai et al., 2025)
offer exactly such an interface: prompt following, physical plausibility, object interaction, and tem-
poral consistency can all be phrased as video-grounded questions, and the teacher-forced likelihood
of a reference answer measures whether each check is satisfied. Because every question–answer
likelihood is differentiable with respect to the VLM’s video-frame input, a single check provides
both a scalar contribution to the rollout objective and a question-conditioned sensitivity map for
routing its update. Using a frozen, off-the-shelf evaluator also removes the need for reward-model
training and per-check preference labels (Furuta et al., 2024). This is the central idea of our work:
the same semantic check that determines whether a prompt requirement is satisfied also estimates
where the resulting policy update should be applied.
Building on this observation, we propose TVRL (Token-Level Video Reinforcement Learning), a
training framework in which a video policy is evaluated by video-level rewards but updated through
explicit token-level credit; the name does not assume that video tokens are discrete actions. We
instantiate the framework for text-to-video diffusion with a clipped group-relative objective. For
each prompt, we construct binary video-grounded checks and score generated videos by the teacher-

forced VLM likelihood of the reference answers. Each check yields two coupled quantities from
the same scalar: a question reward given by the likelihood value, and a detached token-credit proxy
given by the magnitude of its video-frame-input gradient. The latter measures local sensitivity rather
than signed or causal credit. We average the question rewards before group-relative normalization to
obtain a single rollout-level advantage shared across checks, and we use each question-conditioned
credit map to reweight the dense denoising-transition log-probabilities inside the clipped GRPO
surrogate. The shared advantage sets the direction and strength of the update, while the credit
maps determine its spatio-temporal allocation. This design preserves group-relative rollout ranking
and ratio clipping, and it never backpropagates reward gradients through the generator. Averaging
several checks also reduces reliance on any single question. Our contributions are:
• We derive token-level credit for video GRPO from the reward itself: a frozen VLM supplies
both a question reward and a detached, question-conditioned sensitivity map, requiring
neither task-specific reward-model training nor dense credit labels.
• We develop a shared-advantage, localized-routing objective that aggregates question re-
wards into one rollout-level advantage and uses the credit maps to reweight dense
denoising-transition log-probabilities within the clipped GRPO ratio. Under the baseline’s
log-probability reduction, uniform weights recover scalar-reward GRPO.
• With the SAGE sampler, TVRL improves VBench-2.0 Overall over the corresponding
GRPO baselines by +1.36, +1.33, +1.85, and +3.15 points with VideoAlign, VideoScore2,
UnifiedReward2, and Qwen3.5-9B rewards, respectively. With the Qwen3.5-9B reward
fixed, it also improves over GRPO under the SAGE, Flow, and Dance samplers by +3.15,
+2.92, and +2.68 points. Ablations cover credit granularity and the choice of VLM critic.

RELATED WORK
Token-level credit for video optimization.
Alignment of video generators has increasingly
moved beyond a single global score toward feedback structured by space, time, or evaluation crite-
rion. Differentiable reward optimization obtains such feedback by backpropagating reward gradients
through the decoder and the denoising chain (Clark et al., 2023; Prabhudesai et al., 2023; 2024; Yuan
et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026). These gradients encode how sensitive the score is to local video
evidence, but using them to update the generator directly requires keeping the reward model, the
decoder, and the multi-step denoising graph in memory, and repeated Jacobian products along the
stochastic chain can amplify unstable local gradients. Other dense-feedback methods expose lo-
calized failures through temporal preference labels, dense captions, rich human or AI feedback, or
patch-level reward models (Wu et al., 2025b; Krishna et al., 2017; Xiong et al., 2024; Liang et al.,
2024; Liu et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a), at the cost of additional annotation or a separately
trained localized reward model. Structured alignment systems (Liang et al., 2026) and ViPO (Ni
et al., 2026) redistribute a scalar evaluation or GRPO advantage to finer units, yet their allocation
maps come from perceptual priors rather than from the check that produced the reward. Recent
VLM-based video evaluators instead express prompt requirements as semantic questions rather than
task-specific reward heads (Qwen Team, 2026; He et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b). TVRL uses
this interface to couple reward evaluation with token-level credit: teacher-forced answer likeli-
hoods score prompt-level checks, and detached video-frame-input gradients weight the denoising
log-probabilities within GRPO. Unlike Diffusion-DRF (Wang et al., 2026), which also uses struc-
tured VLM feedback but backpropagates it through the generator, the VLM gradient here stops at the
credit map; unlike scalar GRPO, it changes where the update is applied; and unlike dense-feedback
methods, it needs no dense labels or localized reward model.
GRPO for video generation.
GRPO removes the need for a learned value model by sampling
multiple outputs for the same prompt and normalizing their rewards within the group, which makes
it attractive for large video generators (Shao et al., 2024). Recent video GRPO methods adapt this
formulation to diffusion and flow models by treating denoising as a stochastic policy and defin-
ing transition log-probabilities along sampled latent trajectories. Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025a),
DanceGRPO (Xue et al., 2025), and SAGE-GRPO (Zheng et al., 2026) instantiate this framework
with different stochastic denoising samplers. All of them optimize a clipped policy-gradient objec-
tive, but with a scalar reward they assign one terminal reward to each rollout and apply the resulting
group-relative advantage uniformly across denoising steps and latent video tokens. Such a reward

5
CONCLUSION
We presented TVRL, a framework that derives token-level credit from the reward being optimized
in video reinforcement learning, and instantiated it in GRPO for text-to-video diffusion. Averaged
teacher-forced question rewards set the sign and strength of each update through a shared group-
relative advantage, and detached VLM gradients set its spatio-temporal allocation inside the clipped
policy ratio. On VBench-2.0, TVRL improves over matched GRPO baselines for all four reward
models under SAGE (+1.33 to +3.15 Overall) and for all three samplers with Qwen3.5-9B (+2.68 to
+3.15), and 3×3 spatial credit outperforms both coarser and unsmoothed 1×1 credit. TVRL shows a
promising direction for video RL: moving from trajectory-level to token-level updates, so that each
rollout’s advantage acts on the video tokens that determine its reward. We expect token-level credit
to extend to longer videos and other evaluators.

We use the first 200 prompts of VideoGen-Eval (Yang et al., 2025). For every prompt, each com-

ties hidden and judge text–video alignment as a win for TVRL, a loss, or a tie. We aggregate all
judgments into the win/loss/tie rates in Table 2; the decisive preference rate excludes ties and is
computed as Win/(Win + Loss).

A.6
QUESTION-CONDITIONED SPATIAL SENSITIVITY
Relation to the training control.
The shuffled 3×3 control in Table 3 obtains 53.70 Overall, com-
pared with 57.69 for unshuffled routing and 54.54 for uniform routing. In the local implementation,
shuffling permutes routing-grid weights before spatial smoothing, preserving their value distribution
at that stage; subsequent smoothing can change the distribution. This comparison supports struc-
tured routing but does not isolate semantic correspondence from spatial and temporal coherence
or match the final maps’ entropy. We separately test whether the critic’s video sensitivity follows
queried content when its position changes.
Controlled question and position swaps.
We fix 12 disjoint pairs of source prompts and two
generation seeds per pair, yielding 24 video pairs. Each source contributes 20 uniformly sampled
frames, center-cropped to a square and resized to fill an equal-size panel; the crop is fixed and
not object-aware. An earlier construction instead letterboxed each full frame into its panel with
neutral padding, which left the content in roughly half of the panel area; we report it as a second
row on the same pairs and questions. For each pair, we concatenate the panels as AB and BA
and ask separately whether target A or target B is present. The questions do not specify positions.
This gives 96 real Qwen3.5-9B gradient computations per panel construction of the teacher-forced
log-probability of the fixed answer Yes. Unlike the full training question set, this diagnostic uses
object-presence checks. Before computing gradients, we review three frames per pair and require
each target to be visible in at least two. This qualifies 20 crop-to-fill pairs: one pair contains an
ambiguous piano-like object, and in three pairs the fixed crop removes the target from at least two
reviewed frames. The letterbox construction qualifies 23 pairs under the same rule. Unqualified
pairs are retained in the all-pairs analysis, every prompt-pair group keeps at least one qualified pair,
and no pairs are replaced after observing gradients. The construction uses known panel boundaries
rather than detector or segmenter masks; each panel nevertheless contains background as well as the
target.

B.8
UNSMOOTHED CELL-LEVEL CREDIT
The main-text optimization comparison focuses on the consistent w/o-credit →frame →7×7 →
3×3 progression. Figure B1 additionally reports the unsmoothed 1×1 control. This extreme does
not continue the trend: it underperforms the spatially aggregated variants with Qwen3.5-9B, and its
VideoScore2 reward drops sharply, indicating evaluator-dependent optimization instability. A held-
out Qwen3.5-9B evaluation is consistent with this observation: 1×1 obtains 55.10 Overall, below
frame credit (56.47), 7×7 (57.08), and 3×3 (57.69).
The raw VLM-gradient map is a local sensitivity estimate rather than a noise-free causal attribution.
Without spatial aggregation, isolated high-frequency responses can survive normalization and assign
disproportionate credit to a few latent cells. This lowers the effective number of credited units
and can increase update variance, as described by Equation (B11). PPO clipping limits excessive
likelihood-ratio contributions but cannot correct a persistently mislocalized routing direction. The
more severe VideoScore2 failure suggests that robustness at cell-level resolution also depends on the
structure of the evaluator’s reward sensitivity; it implies neither that question-level advantages are
required nor that token credit is ineffective.
Together with the held-out result above, Figure B1 shows that removing spatial aggregation does not
extend the improvement obtained by moving from frame-level to spatially aggregated credit. The
complementary 3×3 versus 7×7 residual diagnostic appears in Figure 8 of the main paper, and
Figure B2 shows two further examples, with the strongest residuals near the pen barrel and writing
region and near the white headband.
