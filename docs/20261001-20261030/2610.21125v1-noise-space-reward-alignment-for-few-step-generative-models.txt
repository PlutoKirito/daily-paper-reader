ABSTRACT
Few-step generative models produce high-quality samples in one or a few eval-
uations. However, steering them toward desired properties remains difficult, es-
pecially when only black-box reward evaluations are available. While one could
apply approaches developed for iterative diffusion and flow-based models, such as
direct RL fine-tuning or test-time scaling, we find that neither transfers well to the
few-step regime: direct fine-tuning suffers from severe reward over-optimization,
and test-time scaling sacrifices the efficiency advantage of few-step generation.
We propose Noise Policy Optimization (NPO), which explicitly targets the align-
ment problem for few-step generative models. The key idea is to learn an amor-
tized flow policy over the initial noise space of the generator. The trained policy
transports Gaussian noise to a reward-tilted noise distribution, whose pushforward
distribution through the frozen generator is exactly the reward-tilted output distri-
bution. By keeping the generator frozen, we can effectively utilize the knowledge
encoded in the pretrained model, yielding a better reward-quality trade-off than
direct fine-tuning. The amortized policy moves the search cost into training, so
inference adds only a lightweight noise sampling step on top of the pretrained
model. To improve sample efficiency during training, we use ELBO-based like-
lihood estimation that requires only sampled endpoints rather than simulated tra-
jectories. Across image, language, and protein structure generation, NPO con-
sistently achieves a better reward-quality trade-off than direct fine-tuning, and
matches test-time search methods with 16 to 549× lower inference cost.

INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in few-step generative modeling have enabled high-quality generation with only
one or a few model evaluations (Song et al., 2023; Boffi et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Geng et al.,
2026; Hu et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2025). However, one may need to align the generator toward desired
properties in downstream applications, such as human preferences in image and text generation or
structural properties in biological design. Despite the growing importance of few-step generators,
their reward alignment remains relatively underexplored. Moreover, most of these downstream ob-
jectives are available only as scalar values whose gradients cannot be propagated through the gener-
ator due to non-differentiable rewards or discretization. Together, these considerations motivate an
important problem: how to align few-step generative models without access to reward gradients.
A straightforward way is to directly fine-tune the generator using reinforcement learning (RL) meth-
ods developed for iterative diffusion and flow-based models (Fan et al., 2023; Black et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2026). However, we find that direct fine-tuning transfers poorly to the few-step regime. As
demonstrated in Figure 1, direct methods either make limited progress or suffer from severe reward
over-optimization that degrades sample quality. We hypothesize that directly changing the parame-
ters of a few-step generative model is prone to losing the knowledge encoded during pretraining.

A promising alternative is to keep the generator frozen and optimize its input noise at test
time (Eyring et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2026; Yoon et al., 2026; Min et al., 2026). While search-
ing the noise through test-time scaling can effectively improve the target reward without modifying
the pretrained generator, the process must be repeated from scratch for every generated sample. This
incurs substantial inference cost and sacrifices the efficiency advantage of few-step generation.
We propose Noise Policy Optimization (NPO), which learns an amortized flow policy over the input
noise space of a frozen few-step generator. We use the identity that the problem of sampling from
the reward-tilted output distribution can be reduced to sampling from the reward-tilted input noise
distribution when the sampling path is deterministic. NPO learns this transformation with a flow
policy that transports the standard Gaussian distribution toward the reward-tilted input noise distri-
bution. By keeping the generator frozen, NPO preserves the knowledge in the pretrained model. By
amortizing the noise transformation, it moves the cost of test-time search to training. To enable more
efficient training, we use ELBO-based likelihood estimation from sampled noise endpoints instead
of using conventional trajectory-based likelihood estimation (Black et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026).
We observe that it drastically improves the sample efficiency of our method, as depicted in Figure 1.
We evaluate NPO across image, language, and protein structure generation tasks, covering a di-
verse range of one- and few-step generators and reward functions. Compared to direct generator
fine-tuning, NPO consistently achieves a better trade-off between improving reward and preserving
sample quality and diversity. Relative to test-time noise optimization, NPO achieves comparable
or higher rewards with 16 to 549× lower inference cost by learning an amortized policy. In com-
parison to trajectory-based noise-policy training, NPO reaches the same reward levels with fewer
reward evaluations. Together, these results demonstrate that NPO efficiently aligns few-step genera-
tive models using only black-box rewards, without sacrificing sample quality or inference efficiency.

RELATED WORK
Few-step generative models. Recent advances have enabled high-quality generation with only one
or a few model evaluations. Consistency models learn trajectory-consistent mappings (Song et al.,
2023; Song & Dhariwal, 2024; Geng et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2023a), while distillation-based methods
compress multi-step generators using objectives such as adversarial training or distribution match-
ing (Sauer et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2023b). Flow-map-based and related approaches
learn finite-time transport between noise levels or directly from noise to data (Kim et al., 2024; Frans
et al., 2025; Geng et al., 2026; Boffi et al., 2025; 2026). While these methods substantially reduce
sampling cost, aligning them with downstream rewards remains underexplored.
Reward alignment of generative models. Generative models can be aligned to downstream objec-
tives by reward backpropagation (Prabhudesai et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023; Kang et al., 2026), RL-
based policy optimization (Black et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026),
incorporating rewards during distillation (Duan et al., 2026), or using variational inference (Lee
et al., 2026c). Recent work has also studied alignment of few-step generators through policy op-
timization for consistency models (Oertell et al., 2024), sample-based variational inference (Lee
2


et al., 2026b), and flow-map-specific approaches (Mammadov et al., 2026; Potaptchik et al., 2026).
Although promising, these approaches either directly modify the pretrained generator, which can
lead to reward hacking, or rely on model-specific structures. In contrast, NPO keeps the pretrained
generator frozen to preserve sample quality and applies across diverse few-step generators.
Initial-noise optimization. For deterministic generators, alignment can alternatively be performed
by optimizing the input noise while keeping the generator fixed. Test-time methods optimize the ini-
tial noise for each sample using reward gradients (Eyring et al., 2024; Min et al., 2026; Yoon et al.,
2026) or gradient-free optimization (Ma et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026), but incur repeated inference-
time search. Amortized approaches instead learn reusable noise distributions or policies, including
Gaussian noise policies (Chen et al., 2024; Miao et al., 2025), reward-gradient-based transforma-
tions (Eyring et al., 2026; Wagenmaker et al., 2025), and diffusion-based noise samplers (Venkatra-
man et al., 2025). NPO similarly amortizes noise-space optimization, while supporting black-box
reward evaluations with an expressive flow policy trained using endpoint forward-noising updates
to improve sample efficiency.

PROBLEM SETUP & BACKGROUND
In this section, we study reward alignment of pretrained few-step generative models. Given a gener-
ator and a scalar reward function, our goal is to steer generation toward high-reward outputs without
assuming access to reward gradients. We first formalize the desired reward-tilted output distribution
and review trajectory-based policy optimization.

NOISE POLICY OPTIMIZATION
To address these challenges, we propose Noise Policy Optimization (NPO). NPO learns an amor-
tized flow policy over the input noise of a frozen generator. We first show that learning the reward-
tilted noise distribution is sufficient to realize the reward-tilted output distribution through the frozen
generator. Then, we propose an ELBO-based objective that approximates the target noise distribu-
tion with the flow policy in the noise space.

5.1
TEXT-TO-IMAGE GENERATION
We first evaluate NPO on text-to-image generation tasks. For pretrained few-step generators, we
use SD-Turbo (Sauer et al., 2024) and SANA-Sprint (Chen et al., 2025), which support one- and
four-step image generation. We provide additional experimental details in Appendix D.
5.1.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Rewards and Metrics. We optimize two target reward functions: the LAION aesthetic predic-
tor (Schuhmann, 2022) and JPEG compressibility (Black et al., 2024), without using reward gradi-
ents. To measure reward over-optimization, we report ImageReward (Xu et al., 2023) for semantic
fidelity and LPIPS diversity (Zhang et al., 2018) for sample diversity.
Baselines. We compare NPO against three categories of alignment methods. Implementation details
for all baselines are provided in Appendix C.
(1) Direct generator fine-tuning. Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2026) applies policy-gradient updates
directly to the generator using stochastic transition likelihoods. FAV-B (Lee et al., 2026b) fine-tunes
the generator using amortized SVGD with zeroth-order gradient estimation from reward evaluations.
(2) Test-time noise optimization. Best-of-N (Karthik et al., 2023) samples N candidate noises and
selects the highest-reward output. ReNO (Eyring et al., 2024) and ZeNO (Kim et al., 2026) perform
gradient-based and gradient-free noise optimization at test-time, respectively.
(3) Noise-policy learning. ODS (Venkatraman et al., 2025) learns a score model in noise space
using a trajectory-balance objective. We additionally implement Noise Flow-GRPO, a variant of
our method that instead uses trajectory-based likelihood estimation.
5.1.2
RESULTS
NPO improves reward while preserving sample quality. Figure 3 compares NPO with direct gen-
erator fine-tuning under one-step sampling. Across both pretrained generators, NPO consistently
improves the target rewards, whereas Flow-GRPO and FAV-B show limited or no improvement.
Under four-step sampling, Figure 4 shows that Flow-GRPO improves the target rewards, but Im-
ageReward and LPIPS diversity decrease substantially, indicating severe reward over-optimization.

LANGUAGE GENERATION

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

CONCLUSION
We study reward alignment of few-step generative models without access to reward gradients. We
introduce Noise Policy Optimization (NPO), which keeps the pretrained generator frozen and learns
an amortized flow policy over its input noise to sample from a reward-tilted noise distribution. NPO
further uses ELBO-based likelihood estimation to avoid dependence on trajectory-based objectives.
Extensive experiments across image, language, and protein generation confirm that NPO provides an
effective way to adapt few-step generators while preserving their sample quality and fast inference.
Limitation and future works. Although the noise policy is lightweight, it still requires about 2.2-
2.6× the inference cost of the frozen generator. Distilling the noise policy into a one-step or few-step
map could further reduce this overhead to nearly match the speed of the frozen generator.

IMPLEMENTATION OF THE BASELINES
We reimplement all baselines in our experimental framework, using their official implementations
as references when available. For ZeNO, whose official implementation is not publicly available,
we follow the algorithm of Kim et al. (2026). Within each domain, all methods share the generator,
the reward, and the evaluation protocol. Noise Flow-GRPO and ODS use the noise-policy backbone
of NPO (Table 3). Flow-GRPO and FAV-B fine-tune the attention layers of the generator with
LoRA (Hu et al., 2022). We set the LoRA scale to α = 2r and choose the rank r so that the number
of trainable parameters matches that of the noise policy (Table 4). All methods except FAV-B draw
64 samples per training step. For images, NPO, Noise Flow-GRPO, and Flow-GRPO use B = 8
prompts with a group of G = 8 samples each, and ODS uses 64 prompts with one sample each. The
language and protein generators are unconditional, and all methods draw 64 samples per step.

Noise Flow-GRPO.
Noise Flow-GRPO trains the noise policy with the GRPO objective of Liu
et al. (2026), with the reference flow of Appendix B.1 as the reference model. During training, it
samples the policy flow with a 10-step Euler–Maruyama discretization of an SDE with noise level
a = 0.7, and it clips the importance ratio at ϵ = 1e-4. At evaluation, it samples the policy flow with
a 10-step Heun solver. We train with AdamW, (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999), weight decay 10−4 (0 for
proteins), and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0 (5.0 for proteins).
Flow-GRPO.
Flow-GRPO fine-tunes the generator with the GRPO objective of Liu et al. (2026),
with the frozen generator as the reference model. To obtain transition likelihoods, we add Gaussian
noise after each sampling step for multi-step sampling and to the output for one-step sampling. We
clip the importance ratio at ϵ = 0.2 for images and ϵ = 1e-4 otherwise. At evaluation, the generator
samples without the added noise. We train with AdamW, (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999), weight decay
10−4, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0.
FAV-B.
We follow the black-box variant of Lee et al. (2026b). For each of C conditions, FAV-
B draws K particles from the fine-tuned generator and K particles from the frozen generator. It
estimates the reward gradient at each particle with an antithetic evolution-strategies estimate over
N random perturbations of scale σ, which costs K(2N + 1) reward calls per condition. It adds β
times this gradient to the kernel density estimate of the prior score and regresses the LoRA onto the
particles moved by the resulting velocity. We use K = 8 and N = 4, with C = 4 and σ = 0.1
for images, C = 1 and σ = 0.1 for language, and C = 1, N = 16, and σ equal to 0.01 times
the particle scale for proteins. The kernel bandwidths follow the median heuristic. We train with
AdamW, (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.01, and gradient-norm clipping at 2.0.
ODS.
We follow Venkatraman et al. (2025) and use the noise-policy backbone of NPO as the
score network. ODS samples the noise with a 25-step Euler–Maruyama discretization of a cosine
variance-preserving SDE, during training and at evaluation. It trains with the relative trajectory
balance objective toward qbase(z) r(gθ(z))β and sets log Z to the batch mean. Each step draws
trajectories from the prior with probability 0.2, from a uniform replay buffer of size 10,000 with
probability 0.2, and from the current policy otherwise. We use log(r + 2) for compressibility and
the exponential of the secondary-structure diversity score for proteins so that log r is defined. We
train with Adam and gradient-norm clipping at 0.1.
Test-time baselines.
Best-of-N (BoN), ReNO (Eyring et al., 2024), and ZeNO (Kim et al., 2026)
search the initial noise of each sample at inference time and leave the generator unchanged. For each
method, we search as many samples as the evaluation protocol of each domain uses and evaluate
them in the same way as the other methods.

IMAGE EXPERIMENT DETAILS
D.1
GENERATOR, REWARDS AND EVALUATION METRICS
Generator.
We use two frozen text-to-image generators. SANA-Sprint (Chen et al., 2025) is a
distilled 0.6B-parameter model that generates 1024 × 1024 images from a 32 × 32 × 32 latent. We
run it at one and four sampling steps with guidance scale 4.5. SD-Turbo (Sauer et al., 2024) is an
adversarially distilled Stable Diffusion 2.1 model that generates 512×512 images from a 4×64×64
latent. We run it at one and four sampling steps.
Target rewards.
We use two target rewards. The LAION aesthetic predictor (Schuhmann, 2022)
is a linear head on a frozen CLIP image embedding (Radford et al., 2021) and maps an image to a
scalar aesthetic score. JPEG compressibility (Black et al., 2024) is the negative file size of the image
after JPEG encoding.
Evaluation metrics.
We measure semantic collapse with ImageReward (Xu et al., 2023), a learned
reward model of prompt–image alignment that no method optimizes. We measure diversity collapse
with LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), the perceptual distance between two images, averaged over all
pairs of samples that share a prompt.

BIOLOGICAL EXPERIMENT DETAILS
F.1
GENERATOR, REWARD AND EVALUATION METRICS
Generator.
We use the five-step protein backbone generator of Xie et al. (2025), distilled from
Proteina (Geffner et al., 2025) with score identity distillation. We run it at K = 5 sampling steps
and generate backbones of L = 150 residues. We sample without re-injecting noise between steps,
which makes the sampler deterministic given the initial noise.
Target reward.
The target reward is the secondary-structure diversity score of Venkatraman et al.
(2025), computed from the proportions of α-helix, β-sheet, and coil residues in a backbone. We
assign the secondary structure with P-SEA (Labesse et al., 1997) from the Cα trace instead of DSSP,
which requires backbone atoms that the generator does not produce.
Evaluation metrics.
We measure designability with the self-consistency protocol of Geffner et al.
(2025) and Xie et al. (2025). ProteinMPNN (Dauparas et al., 2022) designs eight sequences for each
backbone, and ESMFold (Lin et al., 2023) folds each sequence. A backbone is designable when the
smallest Cα RMSD between the backbone and a refolded structure is below 2 ˚A, and we report the
designable fraction. We measure diversity with the mean TM-score (Zhang & Skolnick, 2004) over
all pairs of backbones, computed with TM-align (Zhang & Skolnick, 2005). A lower value indicates
higher diversity.
