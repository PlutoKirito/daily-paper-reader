SyncVid: World-Equivariant Diffusion for
Synchronized Video Generation
Farhad G. Zanjani, Animesh Karnewar, Fatih Porikli, Amirhossein Habibian
{fzanjani, karnewar, fporikli, ahabibia}@qti.qualcomm.com
Generating multiple videos of a dynamic scene from a single reference video requires synthesizing
both observed and unobserved content consistently across viewpoints. Existing camera-control
methods for video diffusion typically rely on architectural mechanisms, including camera
embeddings, cross-view attention, and geometric conditioning. Although effective for static scene
content, these approaches often struggle with the fine-grained, dynamic geometry that must remain
coherent across views and over time. We synchronize the diffusion process itself by sampling the
Gaussian noise prior in a canonical 3D space derived from the source video and rasterizing it
into target cameras, rather than sampling independently in each 2D image plane. Our method,
SyncVid, lifts a monocular video into a canonical 4D representation, associates noise with scene
geometry and unseen space, and rasterizes the resulting structured noise into arbitrary views.
Because rendering can introduce local correlations, we further propose a cross-view-consistent
whitening procedure that decorrelates neighboring pixels while preserving correspondence across
views. The structured noise serves as the diffusion prior for a LoRA-adapted pretrained video
diffusion model, requiring no architectural modifications. By enforcing spatiotemporal and
cross-view coherence at the source of stochasticity, SyncVid generates synchronized videos and
improves both perceptual quality and cross-view consistency in our experiments. Project page:
https://qualcomm-ai-research.github.io/SyncVid-Webpage.

Introduction
Video diffusion models have rapidly matured from generating a single plausible video to supporting fine-
grained user control, including camera trajectories [1, 2, 3]. A natural extension of this control is synchronized
video generation: producing multiple videos of the same dynamic scene from different viewpoints that
remain consistent with one another. This capability is central to applications such as virtual reshooting and
cinematography, multi-view content creation for AR/VR, and synthesizing training data for downstream 3D and
4D vision tasks. Recent work approaches this problem through camera-conditioned reshooting, self-supervised
pseudo-view training, or joint multi-view denoising with cross-view interaction modules [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. These
methods demonstrate that video diffusion models can produce multiple views with useful visual and geometric
agreement, but rely primarily on architectural coupling, rendered conditioning, or specialized training data.
A separate line of research has shown that the noise input to a video diffusion model is itself a powerful
conditioning signal, rather than merely an initialization to be denoised away. Correlating noise across frames,
instead of sampling it independently, improves temporal consistency and enables direct control over motion.
PYoCo [9] introduces correlated noise priors for video diffusion, while How I Warped Your Noise [10]
and Go-with-the-Flow [11] warp a continuous noise field according to optical flow. EquiVDM [12] further
formalizes the resulting equivariance between noise transformations and generated motion. These results
suggest that structured noise – noise with an explicit, non-trivial correlation structure tied to the underlying
content – can serve as an effective and lightweight conditioning mechanism, complementing changes in model
architecture or training data.

Despite this progress, current approaches for constructing structured noise typically warp a 2D noise map
using in-plane rotations and translations of 2D noise patches to propagate samples into future frames.
Alternatively, they require the optical flow of the target video, which is impractical to obtain before generating
that video. These limitations can substantially degrade video quality, particularly for dynamic objects
undergoing out-of-plane rotations, partial occlusions, and subsequent reappearance.
In this work, we show that synchronizing the diffusion prior in a canonical 3D space can induce world-
equivariant video generation. Specifically, camera-induced changes in the 3D noise representation are
reflected as consistent changes in the generated views. We achieve this by synchronizing the diffusion process
at its source of randomness: we construct a canonical 3D noise representation that spans the reconstructed
scene observed in the reference video as well as its surrounding unobserved space. We then rasterize
this representation into each target camera to produce view-consistent noise priors, including for complex
transformations such as out-of-plane rotations. Because all target views derive their stochastic initialization
from the same canonical representation, the diffusion model is encouraged to generate correlated completions
of unseen content, while conditioning on the reconstructed scene preserves consistency in observed regions.
An overview of our method is shown in Fig. 1. We summarize our contributions as follows:
• We propose a structured noise prior for video diffusion models by sampling and assigning noise vectors
in the 3D space of the video content, rather than independently in each camera’s 2D image plane.
Rasterizing this representation preserves dense spatiotemporal structure across views, without relying
on 2D warping or editing of image-plane noise.
• We introduce a view-consistent whitening procedure that reduces local spatial correlations introduced
by rasterization while preserving cross-view correspondence, keeping the resulting noise close to the
marginal distribution expected by the pretrained diffusion model.
• We establish an automated benchmarking pipeline for synchronized video generation on the Mixkit
subset of Open-Sora, enabling reliable and reproducible comparisons without manual or subjective
selection of evaluation samples.

Related Work
2.1
Structured Noise for Synchronized Video Generation
Structured noise has emerged as an effective mechanism for improving temporal consistency and controlling
motion in video diffusion models. PYoCo [9] observes that when an image diffusion model is adapted into a
video model, the noise injected at different frames should preserve temporal correlation rather than being
independent: independent per-frame noise lets the denoiser drift toward a slightly different appearance in
each frame, causing jitter and flicker, whereas correlated noise starts neighboring frames from related latent
structure and denoises them into consistent appearance and smoother motion. This gain requires no new
architecture—no motion modules, optical-flow supervision, or video transformers—but only a modified noise
prior, so a pretrained image model reaches strong temporal consistency by fine-tuning on comparatively little
video data at low cost. Later work makes the correlation explicit: How I Warped Your Noise [10] transports
a continuous Gaussian noise field across frames while preserving its distribution, Go-with-the-Flow [11]
warps noise by real-time optical flow to control object and camera motion, and EquiVDM [12] shows that
training with warped noise encourages equivariance between noise transformations and generated motion.
All build correspondence through 2D motion warping, which suits consecutive frames of a single camera,
where motion is well approximated by an in-plane flow field, but does not extend across cameras: a 2D warp
expresses neither out-of-plane camera rotation nor correspondence for surfaces that a different viewpoint
newly reveals or occludes.
2.2
Synchronized Camera-Controlled Video Generation
Early camera-control methods such as CameraCtrl [1], MotionCtrl [2], and CamCo [3] condition single-
view generation on camera trajectories, but do not explicitly enforce cross-view synchronization. Other
methods couple denoising across views: SynCamMaster [6] adds a multi-view synchronization module for
joint multi-camera generation, Collaborative Video Diffusion (CVD) [7] exchanges information through
epipolar-guided cross-view attention, and CAVIA [8] uses spatial and temporal cross-attention for view
consistency. A third line uses geometry as an indirect synchronization signal: ReCamMaster [4] reshoots
videos along novel trajectories using video conditioning and synchronized synthetic multi-view training data,
Reshoot-Anything [5] reduces dependence on paired multi-view data through self-supervised pseudo-view
training around a 4D point-cloud anchor, and ViewCrafter [13] guides diffusion-based novel-view video
synthesis with coarse rendered geometry. Diffusion-enhancement methods address related but different
settings: ArtiFixer [14] couples video diffusion with 3D reconstruction, using opacity-aware latent mixing
and autoregressive novel-view generation to enhance incomplete reconstructions, while SetDiff [15] enhances
Gaussian-Splatting novel-view renderings in a multi-view setting by set-based diffusion over variable-size
reference and target views grounded in pixel-aligned coordinate maps and pose-aware Plucker ray embeddings.
Overall, existing methods synchronize through camera embeddings, cross-view interaction modules [6, 7, 8],
or rendered geometric conditions [4, 5, 13, 15]. In contrast, we impose camera control with structured noise:
instead of sampling prior Gaussian noise independently in each camera plane, we sample a canonical 3D
field and project it into each view, providing both geometric conditioning and view-consistent diffusion
initialization.

Experiments
4.1
Datasets
Because real-world datasets rarely provide synchronized videos of dynamic scenes, we train on synthetic
dynamic videos and real-world static videos. For synthetic training, we use 36K video pairs from Mul-
tiCamVideo (MCV), introduced by ReCamMaster [4], which provides synchronized videos and camera
trajectories rendered from dynamic scenes in Unreal Engine 5. To reduce the synthetic-to-real domain gap,
we additionally sample 2K DL3DV-10K videos [19] for real-world training.
DL3DV contains monocular videos of static scenes rather than synchronized multi-view captures, so we
construct pseudo-synchronized pairs via temporal folding, following CVD [7]. For each 2𝑙−1-frame clip,
we split at the center frame (𝑙+ 1) and reverse the first segment, yielding two segments that share their
starting frame and then follow different temporal trajectories. Since the scenes are static, this reversal is
physically valid, and each folded pair provides two temporally aligned observations of the same scene along
distinct trajectories, with separate associated camera poses. Applying this procedure to the first two buckets
of DL3DV yields about 13.5K video pairs.
Multi-ViewBench. For evaluation on real-world videos we curate Multi-ViewBench, a 200-clip benchmark
drawn from the Mixkit subset of Open-Sora-Plan [20]. Because reshooting a real video admits no ground-truth
target view, the composition of such a set is easy to bias by hand and hard for others to reproduce, so we select
it automatically: a vision-language model judges every candidate clip once against six fixed admissibility
gates and four semantic attributes, and every subsequent decision is a deterministic function of those recorded
judgements. Admissible clips must be a single continuous real-time shot, landscape, and captured by a
physically moving camera; survivors are ranked by a richness index rewarding depth layering, occlusion,
and non-rigid motion. We report two disjoint splits of 100 clips each, defined by the model’s composition
judgement: standard holds the richest single-subject scenes, and complex the richest multi-subject scenes
with cluttered geometry, drawn from a category-balanced pool so that no Mixkit category monopolizes
the candidates. The complex split is the harder of the two by construction, with a mean richness of 71.2
against 30.9 for standard. We release the code, the per-clip judgements, and the frozen split CSVs; Sec. A.1
documents the pipeline, the per-stage counts, and its limitations in full.
All training and evaluation videos are standardized to 5 sec. clips at 15 fps and a resolution of 480P.

4.2
Metrics
We evaluate cross-view synchronization, visual quality, camera accuracy, and perceptual consistency.
Synchronization uses high-confidence matched pixels in corresponding regions (Mat. Pix.), cross-view FVD
(FVD-V), and cross-view CLIP similarity (CLIP-V). Temporal consistency uses frame consistency (CLIP-F);
following ReCamMaster [4], camera accuracy is measured by rotation and translation errors (RotErr/TransErr)
between the conditioning and recovered trajectories. We also report six no-reference VBench [21] scores
for aesthetic and imaging quality, temporal flickering and motion smoothness, and subject/background
consistency. Finally, a blind pairwise study presents the source and two methods’ renderings of the same
target trajectory in randomized left/right order; participants select the better result or no preference, covering
50 / 200 comparisons on two disjoint sets of 25 Multi-ViewBench scenes (SyncVid vs. Artifixer and SyncVid
vs. Reshoot-Anything).
4.3
Main results
Following the evaluation protocol of ReCamMaster [4], we compare SyncVid with recent state-of-the-art
methods on the real-world Multi-ViewBench test set. Table 1 shows that our method provides the strongest
overall cross-view synchronization. It achieves the highest number of matched pixels in dynamic regions
(1,332) and the second-highest number in static regions (1,834), behind Reshoot-Anything. It also obtains
the lowest cross-view FVD (146.99) and the highest cross-view CLIP similarity (91.66), indicating that
its generated views are both perceptually aligned and visually similar. In addition, it achieves the highest

Conclusion
We presented SyncVid, a framework for synchronized novel-view video generation that coordinates diffusion
at the source of its stochasticity. By attaching persistent noise to a canonical 4D representation and rasterizing
it into target cameras, SyncVid turns scene correspondence into a property of the diffusion prior rather than a
responsibility of image-plane warping or additional cross-view architecture. A view-consistent whitening step
keeps this structured prior compatible with the distribution expected by the pretrained model, allowing the
approach to be integrated through lightweight adaptation without modifying the diffusion backbone. These
findings suggest that synchronized generation can benefit from treating noise as a world-level representation:
the same stochastic ingredients can follow content across viewpoints and time while leaving the model free to
synthesize newly revealed regions.

A.1
Automated Evaluation Set Curation for OpenSora
Reshooting a real video admits no ground-truth target view, so the only thing a real-world benchmark can
measure is behavior on the clips it happens to contain. That makes the composition of the test set part of the
result. A set assembled by hand can flatter a method through nothing more than which scenes its author found
compelling, and it cannot be reproduced by anyone else. We therefore built Multi-ViewBench, the 200-clip
evaluation set used in Sec. 4.3, so that every accept or reject decision is either a recorded model judgement or a
deterministic function of recorded judgements. No clip was ever chosen, dropped, or reordered by inspection.
The pipeline has two stages. Stage 1 asks a vision-language model to judge each candidate clip once and
writes the judgement to disk. Stage 2 never calls a model again: it filters, de-duplicates, and ranks using only
the fields Stage 1 already recorded, so the entire path from corpus to final CSV can be replayed offline from
the released judgements.
A.1.1
Source corpus
Candidates come from the Mixkit subset of Open-Sora-Plan v1.1.0 [20], which contains 9,194 stock-footage
.mp4 clips filed under 40 topical category folders (forest, Street, Couple, Bicycle, and so on). Two
properties of this corpus shape the pipeline. First, the category folders are editorial tags rather than a partition:
the same physical clip is frequently filed under several categories, so the corpus contains substantially
fewer distinct videos than files. Second, the clips are heterogeneous in exactly the ways that matter for
camera-controlled generation, mixing locked-off tripod shots, time-lapses, portrait-orientation footage, and
heavy optical effects with the moving-camera material we actually want.
A.1.2
Stage 1: structured vision-language judgement
Each clip is judged independently by Gemini 3.1 Pro (gemini-3.1-pro-preview), accessed through an
OpenAI-compatible inference gateway. We sample 12 uniformly spaced frames per clip, rescale each so its
longest side is at most 512 pixels, JPEG-encode them, and submit the sequence with a fixed system prompt at
temperature 0.2. The response is constrained to a JSON schema, so the model cannot answer in prose or omit
a field, and every reply is machine-readable without parsing heuristics. One JSON file is written per clip,
which makes the pass resumable: an interrupted run skips clips whose output already exists.
The schema, summarized in Table 5, has four parts. Six boolean gates encode hard admissibility requirements.
A composition bucket assigns the clip to simple or complex and is the field that later defines our two splits:
simple denotes a single subject under smooth tracking against a clean background, and complex denotes
multiple subjects, cluttered environments, distinct depth layers, or dynamic backgrounds. Four semantic
scores rate the properties that make a clip informative for view synthesis. Finally, three free-text reasoning
fields record why the model answered as it did; these are released, and Stage 2 consults them in exactly one
narrow place (Sec. A.1.4).

A.1.5
Why the filter order matters
Two orderings in Table 6 are load-bearing rather than incidental.
De-duplication runs after the gates so that a copy which fails a gate can never win the richness comparison and
readmit a clip that should have been rejected. It runs before static-source classification so that each physical
clip receives exactly one verdict. Were the order reversed, a clip could enter the pool through whichever of its
category copies happened to be scored most favorably, which would let editorial filing decisions leak into the
benchmark.
Static-source removal precedes optical-effect removal because the two overlap: the adjudication step already
reclassifies most optical-effect clips as static, so only 4 clips survive to be dropped by the explicit optical filter.
Reversing the two would discard the handful of optical-effect clips that do accompany real camera motion.
A.1.6
Constructing the two splits
The pool is strongly unbalanced, holding 1,823 complex clips against 404 simple ones, because static-source
removal strips proportionally more single-subject tracking shots. The two splits are therefore built differently,
and deliberately so.
Qualcomm AI Research is an initiative of Qualcomm Technologies, Inc.
3


Standard (100 clips). Simple compositions are the comparatively solved case, so this split is a compact
reference rather than half the benchmark, and its specification is exactly take the 100 highest-richness simple
clips. No category cap and no diversity heuristic apply. With only 404 candidates, imposing balance here
would trade away richness for very little diversity.
Complex (100 clips). A pure richness sort over 1,823 complex clips collapses onto whichever categories are
largest, and the supply is severely skewed (150 clips for forest against 1 for Cats). We therefore construct
this split in two steps. First we select a category-balanced set of 1,000 clips in which balance is a hard
constraint and richness is maximized only within it. Because an equal quota of 1000/40 = 25 per category is
unavailable from most categories, we allocate quotas by max-min fair water-filling: give every category the
same quota, let categories that cannot meet it contribute everything they have, and redistribute the shortfall
equally among categories that still have supply, repeating until the budget is spent. This maximizes the
smallest category’s share, then the next smallest, and degenerates to an equal split when supply permits.
Each category then contributes its own richest clips up to its quota, so balance is never traded for richness.
The resulting 1,000 clips span all 40 categories with between 1 and 38 clips each, a mean of 25.0, and a
normalized category entropy of 0.940 against a maximum of 1. Second, we take the 100 highest-richness
clips from this balanced set to form the released split.
The two-step construction is worth stating plainly, because balance and richness pull against each other and
the second step partially undoes the first. Balancing at 1,000 determines which clips are available to be
ranked, preventing the fat categories from monopolizing the candidate set; truncating to the richest 100 then
concentrates on 19 of the 40 categories. What balance buys is that the final 100 is drawn from a category-fair
pool rather than from a raw richness sort over the whole corpus, not that the final 100 is itself uniform over
categories.
Both selections sort by richness descending with ties broken deterministically on (category, file name), and in
both cases the cut falls inside a tie group: the standard split cuts within the 𝜌= 24 tier, and the complex split
takes 39 clips above 𝜌= 64 and then 61 of the 210 clips at exactly 𝜌= 64. The tie-break rule is what makes
these selections reproducible, and it is the reason the released CSVs are byte-identical across reruns.

A.5
Experimental Details
Training. We evaluate all baseline methods using their officially released checkpoints. We train SyncVid with
the Wan2.2-14B backbone on eight H100 GPUs, using a per-GPU batch size of one and gradient accumulation
over four steps, for an effective batch size of 32. The model is trained for 10K iterations with a learning rate
of 1 × 10−4.
4D representation. We use an off-the-shelf feed-forward Gaussian Splatting model, NoPo4D [16], which
avoids per-video reconstruction optimization and produces dynamic primitives that are active over finite
temporal windows. Each primitive has a time-varying center, covariance, opacity, and appearance, together
with a time-invariant canonical anchor. To render noise for views not covered by the source video, we model
unobserved background regions with an enclosing cubemap: a finite, axis-aligned box centered at the centroid
of the canonical anchors, with a half-extent set to a fixed multiple of the reconstructed scene extent. The
cubemap therefore lies at a depth comparable to the scene rather than at infinity, as in an environment map.
We index background texels by the ray–box intersection point instead of ray direction alone, allowing pixels
that observe the same proxy surface to share a canonical location. The cubemap has six square faces, each
containing 𝑆× 𝑆texels with 𝑑channels; we use 𝑆= 1024 and 𝑑= 3.
Inference. Unless stated otherwise, we use 50 denoising steps for all SyncVid experiments, matching the
default inference configuration of the WAN video models. We use the same fixed template as the text prompt
during training and inference. Table 8 reports the runtime of the main SyncVid components. Constructing
the 4D representation takes 4.18 seconds and is inexpensive relative to total generation time; because it is
performed once per source video, this cost can also be amortized across multiple reshoots. The reported
noise-rasterization time was measured on a particularly large dynamic scene containing approximately 6.53M
Gaussian points. Video generation uses the original WAN configuration. More efficient step-distillation
methods could reduce this cost, but evaluating them is beyond the scope of this work.
Metrics. For pixel matching (Pix. Mat.), we use RoMA-v2 [25] to estimate point correspondences and count
matches with uncertainty below 0.5. We report the average number of matched pixels separately for dynamic
and static regions. To identify dynamic foreground objects, we obtain a segmentation mask from the source
video using SAM3 [26]. We use this mask to partition correspondences for each source–target frame pair,
enabling separate evaluation of dynamic and static regions.

A.7
Limitations and Failure Examples
Although SyncVid is trained with varied errors in the 4D noise representation and is therefore less sensitive to
reconstruction quality—the 4D representation is used primarily to rasterize a synchronized noise prior—it
remains limited by the quality of the monocular reconstruction. Errors in geometry, tracking, or primitive
re-instantiation can still appear as subject deformation or temporal drift, while disoccluded regions are only
weakly constrained and may be hallucinated by the diffusion model. Figure 8 illustrates these failure modes:
the Arc-right example exhibits severe foreground inconsistencies, whereas the Dolly-out example retains the
main subject and setting but introduces unrealistic content in newly exposed regions.
Although SyncVid excels on video-synchronization metrics and in preserving dynamic regions in the generated
videos, it does not outperform state-of-the-art methods in camera-pose estimation; methods such as ArtiFixer
perform well on this task. We speculate that this gap is partly due to the absence of explicit camera-pose
conditioning, such as the Plücker embeddings used by ArtiFixer. Providing this additional conditioning could
improve pose accuracy, but integrating it is orthogonal to the structured-noise formulation presented here and
is left for future work.
