Optimization to Practical Deployment
Ming Lu†, Junqi Shi†, Zhan Ma*
School of Electronic Science and Engineering, Nanjing University, Nanjing, 210023,
Jiangsu, China.
*Corresponding author(s). E-mail(s): mazhan@nju.edu.cn;

Abstract
End-to-end learned image coding (LIC) has achieved substantial improvements in rate–distortion (R–
D) performance, yet practical deployment requires addressing a broader set of system-level constraints.
This article provides a deployment-oriented review of LIC, with particular attention to the transition
from high-performance neural compression models to practical codecs. We first summarize the basic
end-to-end coding framework, including learned transforms, quantization, entropy modeling, and R–
D optimization. We then review practical extensions that are increasingly important for deployment,
including variable-rate coding and rate control, integer-precision inference and cross-platform con-
sistency, robustness and communication resilience, lightweight and hardware-aware implementation,
and standardization and interoperability. To study these issues under a common setting, we further
introduce TinyLIC as an open benchmark and reproducible case study for rate–distortion–complexity
analysis. Experimental results illustrate the trade-offs among compression efficiency, computational
complexity, numerical consistency, and robustness. In particular, TinyLIC with approximately 10
KMACs/pixel decoding complexity substantially outperforms the H.265/HEVC image-profile anchor
on Kodak, while the 50 KMACs/pixel configuration achieves performance comparable to the
H.266/VVC image-profile anchor on several test sets. The review highlights practical constraints and
design trade-offs that remain central to the deployment and interoperability of learned image codecs.
Materials are accessible at https://njuvision.github.io/TinyLIC.
Keywords: learned image coding, rate–distortion optimization, practical deployment, open benchmark
1 Introduction
Image coding has long been a cornerstone of visual
communication systems (Ivins Jr, 1969; Shannon
& Weaver, 1948). It supports a wide range of
visual communication and storage applications,
including social photo sharing, digital archiving,
mobile imaging, and remote sensing. For more
than three decades, mainstream image coding
standards have largely followed a modular and
rule-driven design philosophy (Bellard, 2016; Pfaff
et al., 2021; Wallace, 1991). Representative codecs
improve compression efficiency by refining individ-
ual components such as transform (Clarke, 1985),
prediction (Kobayashi & Bahl, 1974), quantiza-
tion (Gersho, 1978), and entropy coding (Senoo &
Girod, 1992), while maintaining interoperability
through carefully specified bitstream syntax and
standardization procedures. This paradigm has
achieved remarkable success, but its components

are mostly hand-crafted and optimized within a
constrained design space.
Over the past decade, image compression has
undergone a paradigm shift driven by deep learn-
ing. By learning compact latent representations
directly from data, end-to-end learned image cod-
ing (LIC) frameworks (T. Chen et al., 2021; Duan
et al., 2024; D. He, Yang, Peng, et al., 2022; J. Liu,
Sun, & Katto, 2023; M. Lu, Guo, Shi, Cao, &
Ma, 2022) have surpassed the Joint Photographic
Experts Group (JPEG) standard (Wallace, 1991),
JPEG 2000 (D.T. Lee, 2005), and even image-
profile anchors derived from modern video cod-
ing standards, including Better Portable Graph-
ics (BPG, H.265/High Efficiency Video Coding
(HEVC) image-profile anchor) and VTM Intra
(H.266/Versatile Video Coding (VVC) image-
profile anchor) (Bellard, 2016; Pfaff et al., 2021),
on many public benchmarks in terms of rate–
distortion (R–D) performance (Ding et al., 2021;
Y. Hu, Yang, Ma, & Liu, 2021). At the same
time, the flexibility of learned representations has
extended image coding beyond conventional pixel-
fidelity optimization toward perceptual recon-
struction, ultra-low-bitrate communication (Ke et
al., 2025), and machine-oriented visual represen-
tation (Duan, Ma, & Zhu, 2023).
However, as LIC matures, the field’s primary
bottleneck is shifting. The central question is no
longer only whether learned codecs can achieve
better R–D curves, but also whether they can
operate as reliable, controllable, efficient, and
interoperable compression subsystems in real com-
munication environments. Practical deployment
imposes constraints that are often underrepre-
sented in academic benchmarks, including compu-
tational complexity, decoding latency, rate-control
flexibility, robustness to content variation and per-
turbations, and compatibility with heterogeneous
hardware platforms (Sze, Chen, Emer, Suleiman,
& Zhang, 2017).
This
shift
has
redirected
attention
from
performance-driven
model
design
to
system-
oriented
codec
design.
Recent

have
explored lightweight architectures (Bao et al.,
2025), low-complexity entropy models (D. He,
Zheng, Sun, Wang, & Qin, 2021), variable-rate
and multi-rate coding (T. Chen & Ma, 2020),
robustness under distribution shifts and adversar-
ial perturbations (T. Chen & Ma, 2023), model
quantization and integer inference (Sun, Yu, &
Katto, 2022), and hardware-friendly implementa-
tions (C. Chen et al., 2024). In parallel, the stan-
dardization of JPEG Artificial Intelligence (JPEG
AI)1 (Ascenso, Alshina, & Ebrahimi, 2023) and
IEEE 1857.112 (IEEE, 2024) indicates that LIC is
moving from academic prototypes toward indus-
trial validation. These developments motivate a
survey that treats compression efficiency, deploy-
ability, and interoperability as coupled design
objectives.
Existing surveys have provided valuable per-
spectives on this rapidly evolving field. Early
reviews focused on the transition from traditional
coding tools to neural-network-based or end-to-
end compression (Ding et al., 2021; D. Liu, Li,
Lin, Li, & Wu, 2020; S. Ma et al., 2019), while
later studies offered increasingly comprehensive
coverage of learned transforms, entropy models,
perceptual coding, rate control, and benchmark
performance (B. Chen, Yin, Chen, Wang, & Ye,
2024; Z. Chen, Sun, Zhang, & Zhang, 2024; Y. Hu
et al., 2021; C.-H. Huang & Wu, 2024; Jamil,
Piran, Rahman, & Kwon, 2023; Mishra, Singh, &
Singh, 2022; Y. Zhang, Zhu, Jiang, Kwong, & Kuo,
2023). These reviews provide valuable algorithmic
and conceptual context, but deployment-oriented
issues such as integer inference, decoder consis-
tency, entropy-coder runtime, robustness, rate-
control guarantees, lightweight implementation,
and standard conformance are usually discussed
separately or only as future challenges.
This article therefore reviews LIC from a
deployment-oriented
perspective.

The main contributions of this survey are

Transform. Learned transforms determine
how visual information is mapped into compact
latent representations. Early end-to-end codecs
were dominated by convolutional architectures
(Ball´e et al., 2017; Ball´e, Minnen, et al., 2018),
which provide efficient local feature extraction
and remain attractive for practical implementa-
tions. Subsequent methods introduced non-local
attention and Transformer-based architectures
(T. Chen et al., 2021; H. Li et al., 2024; J. Liu et
al., 2023; M. Lu et al., 2022) to capture longer-
range spatial dependencies, while more recent
studies have explored state-space models (Zeng
et al., 2025) and hierarchical latent representa-
tions (Cong et al., 2026; Duan et al., 2024; Y. Li,
Zhang, Li, & Liu, 2025). These developments
improve representation capacity, but also lead to
different computational, memory, and paralleliza-
tion characteristics. Transform design therefore
increasingly reflects a trade-off between represen-
tation capability and implementation efficiency
rather than compression performance alone.

Entropy Modeling. Compression efficiency
further depends on how accurately the probabil-
ity distribution of quantized latents is modeled.
Early end-to-end methods used factorized latent
distributions (Ball´e et al., 2017), followed by
hyperprior-based models (Ball´e, Minnen, et al.,
2018) that transmit auxiliary side information to
capture spatially varying latent statistics. Autore-
gressive models subsequently exploit dependencies
among previously decoded symbols and can oper-
ate along spatial, channel, or joint spatial-channel
dimensions (T. Chen et al., 2021; Minnen et
al., 2018; Minnen & Singh, 2020). More recent
approaches combine richer context models with
hierarchical representations to further improve
probability estimation (Cong et al., 2026; Jiang et
al., 2025; Y. Li, Zhang, Li, & Liu, 2025). Stronger
entropy models generally reduce statistical redun-
dancy, but often introduce additional decoding
steps, memory accesses, or sequential dependen-
cies. The balance between probability-modeling
accuracy and decoding efficiency is therefore par-
ticularly important for practical deployment.

learned codecs primarily optimize signal fidelity
using distortion measures such as mean squared
error (MSE), resulting in the conventional R–
D formulation (Ball´e et al., 2020, 2017). The
same end-to-end framework can, however, accom-
modate other notions of reconstruction utility.

faithful signal reconstruction toward perceptually
and semantically useful representation, although
their evaluation criteria and deployment require-

fidelity-oriented coding.

This observation marks an important transi-
tion in LIC. The transform-quantization-entropy-
optimization framework has become increasingly
mature, and further improvements in R–D per-
formance alone do not guarantee practical usabil-
ity. A deployable codec must additionally deter-
mine how bitrate is controlled, whether decoding
remains numerically consistent across heteroge-
neous platforms, how the codec behaves under
input or transmission perturbations, whether its
computation maps efficiently to available hard-
ware, and how interoperability is maintained
across implementations.
These properties are closely coupled with
the underlying coding framework. For example,
stronger autoregressive dependencies may improve
entropy modeling but increase decoding latency
and error propagation; low-precision inference
may improve hardware efficiency while altering
R–D behavior; and robustness-oriented mecha-
nisms may sacrifice part of the coding efficiency
to improve reliability. Practical LIC is therefore
better viewed as a multi-objective system-design
problem rather than an extension of R–D opti-
mization alone.
For this reason, the remainder of this survey
treats the core LIC framework as the algorithmic
foundation rather than the primary organizing
dimension. Section 3 focuses on the practical
extensions required to turn an R–D-optimized
neural compression model into a deployable codec,
including rate flexibility and control, model quan-
tization and cross-platform consistency, robust-
ness and communication resilience, lightweight
and hardware-aware deployment, and standard-
ization and interoperability.
3 Practical Extensions for
Deployable LIC
The core framework reviewed in Sec. 2 defines
how learned codecs represent, quantize, and com-
press visual information. Practical deployment,
however, imposes requirements that are not fully
characterized by nominal R–D performance. A
deployable codec must adapt to varying bitrate
constraints, produce deterministic results across
heterogeneous platforms, remain reliable under
content and transmission perturbations, operate
within computation and memory budgets, and
maintain interoperability across implementations.
These requirements arise at different levels of
the coding pipeline and are often coupled with
the underlying transform, entropy model, and
optimization objective.
Accordingly, we organize practical LIC around
five closely related dimensions. Rate flexibility
and control
determine whether a codec can
adapt its operating point to bandwidth or qual-
ity requirements. Model quantization and cross-
platform consistency address low-precision execu-
tion and deterministic entropy decoding. Robust-
ness and communication resilience characterize
codec behavior under input perturbations, distri-
bution shifts, and transmission errors. Lightweight
and hardware-aware deployment concerns com-
putational cost, memory, latency, and hardware
efficiency. Finally, standardization and interoper-
ability provide common bitstream, decoder, and
conformance requirements for practical adoption.
Rather than treating these capabilities as inde-
pendent add-on modules, this section emphasizes
their interactions with compression efficiency and
with one another.

Practical LIC must adapt to varying bandwidth,
storage, and quality requirements without main-
taining multiple independently trained models.

Three related capabilities are particularly impor-
tant. Variable-rate coding allows a single codec
to cover multiple R–D operating points; progres-
sive coding further requires these operating points
to be nested within a common bitstream; and
rate control selects coding parameters so that the
actual bitrate approaches a prescribed target. We
review these three capabilities in turn.
3.1.1 Variable-rate Coding
Variable-rate coding (VRC) aims to span a range
of R–D operating points within a single model,
rather than training an independent network for
each bitrate or quality level. In contrast to con-
ventional codecs, where bitrate can often be
adjusted directly through a quantization parame-
ter (QP), learned codecs couple bitrate with latent
representations, entropy statistics, and network
activations. Rate adaptation therefore requires
an explicit mechanism for modifying the coding
behavior of the learned model. As illustrated in
Fig. 3, existing approaches can be broadly grouped
into recurrent refinement, latent decomposition,
feature modulation, and adaptive quantization.
Recurrent Refinement. Early VRC meth-
ods obtain scalability through iterative residual
coding. Toderici et al. (Toderici et al., 2016, 2017)
introduced recurrent neural codecs in which each
iteration encodes additional information to pro-
gressively refine the reconstruction. Subsequent
studies improved spatially adaptive rate allocation
and recurrent coding structures (Islam, Dang, Lee,
& Moon, 2021; C. Jia, Liu, Wang, Ma, & Gao,
2019; Johnston et al., 2018; Su, Cheng, Sun, &
Katto, 2020). The number of iterations provides
a natural rate-control variable, but higher rates
require additional sequential inference, causing
computation and latency to increase with bitrate.
Latent Decomposition. A second strategy
constructs scalable representations within a sin-
gle forward pass. Rippel and Bourdev (Rippel
& Bourdev, 2017) decompose latent representa-
tions into ordered components, while multi-scale
approaches generate hierarchical representations
whose subsets correspond to different reconstruc-
tion qualities (C. Cai, Chen, Zhang, & Gao,
2018; Nakanishi, Maeda, Miyato, & Okanohara,
2018). Compared with recurrent refinement, latent
decomposition avoids repeatedly executing the
complete codec and naturally supports multi-rate
or progressive decoding. Nevertheless, the indi-
vidual representation levels are jointly optimized
and can be strongly coupled. Moreover, when rate
adaptation is determined by discrete bitplanes,
channels, or scales, the available operating points
may remain relatively sparse, limiting fine-grained
coverage of the R–D curve.
Feature Modulation. A more flexible family
of methods conditions the transform or latent rep-
resentation on the desired operating point. Chen
and Ma (T. Chen & Ma, 2020) showed that
latent representations learned at different bitrates
exhibit substantial structural similarity and can
be adapted through learned scaling factors. This
idea has subsequently been extended by condition-
ing transform features, normalization parameters,
or latent activations on the R–D trade-off param-
eter (S. Cai et al., 2024, 2022; Choi, El-Khamy, &
Lee, 2019; Cui et al., 2021; F. Yang et al., 2020).
Typically, λ, a quality index, or another control
variable is mapped to a learned embedding that
modulates intermediate features.
An
important
practical
property
of
modulation-based VRC is that the codec topol-
ogy remains unchanged across operating points.
It can therefore provide dense or approximately
continuous rate adaptation while maintaining
7


nearly constant computational complexity. The
same principle can be extended spatially through
quality or importance maps, allowing different
regions to operate at different quality levels (Kao,
Weng, Chen, Chiu, & Peng, 2023; Liang, Liu,
Yao, Lin, & Zhao, 2023; Y. Ma et al., 2021; Song,
Choi, & Han, 2021). Such spatial control is useful
for region-of-interest (ROI) coding (C. Cai, Chen,
Zhang, & Gao, 2019), although determining an
optimal quality map introduces an additional
rate-allocation problem.
Adaptive Quantization. Rate adaptation
can also be introduced directly at the quantization
stage. The basic idea is to scale latent variables
before quantization, thereby changing the effec-
tive quantization step size and consequently the
bitrate. This mechanism is closely related to QP-
based control in conventional codecs and can
often be integrated without modifying the main
transform architecture.
Existing methods differ mainly in the granular-
ity and parameterization of the scaling operation.
Global schemes apply a common scale or quan-
tization parameter to a latent tensor (Dumas,
Roumy, & Guillemot, 2018; Presta, Tartaglione,
Fiandrotti, & Grangetto, 2025; Zhou et al., 2020),
whereas finer-grained approaches assign different
scales to channels or individual latent elements
(Akbari, Liang, Han, & Tu, 2020, 2021; S. Li et
al., 2025; Theis et al., 2017; Tong et al., 2023).
The latter provide greater flexibility in adapting
to heterogeneous latent statistics, at the cost of
additional parameters or optimization complexity.
In either case, the achievable rate range depends
on whether the learned representation and entropy
model remain well calibrated when the quantiza-
tion behavior deviates from that seen at individual
training points.
Overall,
recurrent
refinement
and
latent
decomposition provide explicit scalability but rely
on sequential or discrete operating points, whereas
feature modulation and adaptive quantization
better preserve a fixed codec topology and pre-
dictable inference complexity. The main remaining
challenge is to maintain stable and monotonic
R–D behavior over a sufficiently wide rate range.
3.1.2 Progressive Coding
Progressive coding imposes a stronger constraint
than general variable-rate coding. While a VRC
model only needs to provide multiple R–D oper-
ating points, a progressive codec requires these
operating points to be nested: a low-rate recon-
struction should be obtained from a prefix or
ordered subset of the same bitstream, and addi-
tional transmitted symbols should progressively
refine the reconstruction. This property is par-
ticularly useful when the available bandwidth or
transmission duration is not known in advance,
since the sender can terminate transmission with-
out re-encoding the image.
Early neural codecs obtain progressiveness
through iterative refinement, where additional
coding stages successively improve reconstruction
quality (Baig, Koltun, & Torresani, 2017; Toderici
et al., 2017). Although conceptually simple, such
approaches generally require repeated inference as
more information is decoded. More recent meth-
ods instead construct an ordered representation
within a single coding framework, so that progres-
sive reconstruction can be performed by decoding
an increasing subset of the latent representation.
Structured
quantization
and
latent
decomposition. One class of approaches explic-
itly partitions the representation into ordered cod-
ing units. DPICT and its context-based extension
encode latents using trit planes, where succes-
sive planes refine the reconstruction (S. Jeon,
Choi, Park, & Kim, 2023; J.-H. Lee, Jeon, Choi,
Park, & Kim, 2022). Nested quantization con-
structs compatible quantization grids across qual-
ity levels (J. Lee, Jeong, & Kim, 2026; Y. Lu,
Zhu, Yang, Said, & Cohen, 2021), while channel-
or resolution-structured representations provide
alternative forms of scalability (Han, Duan, Tao,
Xu, & Lu, 2020; D. Zhang et al., 2023). These
methods provide an explicit progressive struc-
ture and well-defined truncation points. However,
enforcing nested representations constrains the
optimization of individual operating points, which
may introduce an R–D penalty compared with
independently optimized fixed-rate coding.
Importance-aware ordering and mask-
ing. Another family of methods learns or esti-
mates the relative importance of latent elements
and transmits them accordingly. Importance can
be inferred from latent statistics, learned impor-
tance maps, or external feature priors (Baldas-
sarre, El-Nouby, & J´egou, 2023; Caron et al., 2021;
8


J. Lee, Jeong, & Kim, 2022). ProgDTD (Hoj-
jat, Haberer, & Landsiedel, 2023) encourages an
intrinsic ordering of latent elements through tail-
drop training, allowing a single representation to
support multiple truncation points without sub-
stantially changing the codec architecture. Presta
et al. (Presta, Tartaglione, Fiandrotti, Grangetto,
& Cosman, 2025) further use element-wise impor-
tance ranking and variance-aware masking for
efficient progressive decoding. Compared with
explicitly layered representations, masking-based
approaches are attractive because progressiveness
can be introduced with relatively limited archi-
tectural modification, although their performance
depends on how accurately the ordering reflects
the contribution of each latent element to recon-
struction quality.
Hierarchical
and
context-dependent
progressiveness.
Hierarchical
representa-
tions provide another natural mechanism for
progressive
coding.
Different
latent
levels
or
autoregressive stages can be decoded successively,
producing
increasingly
refined
reconstructions
(Cong et al., 2026; G. Zhang et al., 2025;
Z. Zhang et al., 2026). Such designs can exploit
dependencies across representation levels and
achieve strong coding efficiency, but the result-
ing dependency structure also reduces decoding
independence between stages. This issue becomes
particularly
relevant
in
unreliable
networks,
where the loss of an early portion of the repre-
sentation may affect subsequent decoding. Recent
work therefore combines progressive representa-
tion with network-aware training or loss-resilient
coding (Sha et al., 2025), linking scalability with
communication reliability.
From a deployment perspective, progressive
coding enables transmission to terminate or refine
dynamically without re-encoding. Its main chal-
lenge is to provide fine-grained truncation and
stable partial reconstruction without introducing
strong representation dependencies or substantial
R–D loss. This trade-off is particularly relevant to
bandwidth-limited and unreliable communication.
3.1.3 Rate Control
Variable-rate coding determines the set or range
of R–D operating points that a codec can pro-
vide, whereas rate control (RC) addresses the
inverse problem: given a target bitrate, how should
the available coding parameters be selected so
that the resulting bitstream satisfies the rate con-
straint with minimal distortion? This distinction
is important in practical systems, where the avail-
able bandwidth or storage budget is usually given
explicitly rather than represented by an abstract
quality parameter. Rate control can be formulated
as

(4)
where {αi} denotes adjustable coding parameters,
such as quantization scales, modulation factors,
or quality indices, and Rtarget is the prescribed
rate budget. The main challenge is that the map-
ping from these parameters to the actual bitrate is
content-dependent and generally cannot be deter-
mined analytically. Existing approaches therefore
differ primarily in how this mapping is estimated
and how much additional encoding overhead is
introduced.
Repeated
encoding
search.
The
most
direct strategy is to encode the image repeatedly
with different coding parameters and search for an
operating point close to the target bitrate. JPEG
AI adopts a coarse-to-fine rate-matching proce-
dure, in which an appropriate model or operating
range is first selected and the coding parameters
are subsequently refined (Ascenso et al., 2023;
P. Jia et al., 2025). Jia et al. (P. Jia et al., 2025)
further describe a two-stage procedure in which a
model near the desired rate is first identified, fol-
lowed by local estimation of the bitrate–parameter
relationship and iterative refinement toward the
target.
Search-based rate control is attractive because
it directly evaluates the actual coded rate and can
therefore provide accurate bitrate matching with-
out requiring a highly accurate predictive model.
Its disadvantage is the need for multiple encod-
ing passes. Since neural encoding itself can be
computationally expensive, repeated evaluation
substantially increases encoding latency and lim-
its its applicability when fast or real-time rate
adaptation is required. Practical search strate-
gies therefore need to balance the number of
evaluations against rate-control accuracy.
Bitrate-parameter
mapping. To reduce
repeated encoding, another line of work explic-
itly models the relationship between the target
bitrate and the codec control parameters. Shi et
al. (Y. Shi, Zhang, Wang, Ling, & Yin, 2022)
9


investigate the relationship between bitrate and
the Lagrange multiplier λ and use this relation-
ship for rate selection. Zhang et al. (S. Zhang,
Wang, Mao, Yang, & Wan, 2022) introduce a
Rate-Feature-Level (RFL) model that predicts
suitable coding parameters from both the tar-
get bitrate and image content. Similarly, Xue
and Zhang (N. Xue & Zhang, 2023) use shallow,
high-resolution encoder features to estimate the
mapping from the target rate to λ. Pan et al. (Pan,
Ding, Chen, & Chen, 2025) formulate rate control
as a prediction problem over discrete model and
parameter indices, allowing an appropriate config-
uration to be selected directly for a given image
and target rate.
Compared with repeated search, predictive
mapping can substantially reduce the number of
encoding passes and is therefore more suitable
for latency-sensitive applications. The difficulty is
shifted to the accuracy and generalization of the
predictor: an inaccurate bitrate-parameter map-
ping may lead to rate mismatch, particularly for
image content or target rates that are insuffi-
ciently represented during training. Effective rate
control therefore requires the predictor to capture
both the global behavior of the codec and the
content-dependent variation of the actual bitrate.
Block-level rate allocation. Global rate
control assigns a single operating condition to
the complete image and may therefore overlook
substantial spatial variation in coding difficulty.
Block-level rate allocation addresses this issue by
distributing a global rate budget across spatial
regions according to their local R–D character-
istics. Wang et al. (X. Wang, Lu, & Ma, 2022)
divide an image into blocks, estimate the bitrate–
parameter relationship for individual blocks, and
use a greedy optimization procedure to allocate
bits under the global rate constraint. Because
independently estimating such relationships for
all blocks requires repeated encoding, Dong et
al. (Dong, Lu, & Ma, 2024) exploit correla-
tions among neighboring blocks and estimate the
behavior of most blocks from a smaller set of
sampled blocks, thereby reducing the number of
required encoding operations.
Block-level control provides finer adaptation
to heterogeneous image content and creates an
explicit connection between rate control and spa-
tial bit allocation. However, it also introduces
an additional optimization layer on top of the
neural codec, and its practical value depends on
whether the resulting R–D gain justifies the cost of
local rate estimation and allocation. Similar con-
siderations arise in ROI- and importance-aware
coding, where spatial priorities must ultimately be
translated into a global bit budget.
Overall, rate control introduces an additional
requirement beyond the rate flexibility discussed
in Sec. 3.1.1: the operating point must be not only
available, but also predictable and reachable under
a prescribed rate budget. Repeated search favors
accuracy but incurs encoding overhead, predic-
tive mapping reduces computation but depends
on generalization, and block-level allocation pro-
vides finer spatial control at the cost of additional
optimization. A practical RC scheme therefore
needs to balance rate accuracy, encoding com-
plexity, and R–D performance, while remaining
compatible with the variable-rate mechanism of
the underlying codec.
3.1.4 Discussion and Perspective
Practical rate adaptation should be evaluated
beyond R–D performance alone. A deployable
codec should provide sufficiently wide and smooth
rate coverage, accurate target-rate matching, lim-
ited control overhead, and predictable inference
complexity. Modulation-based VRC and adaptive
quantization are particularly attractive in this
respect because they retain a fixed codec topology
while providing dense operating-point adaptation
(Duan et al., 2024; Z. Jia et al., 2025).
A remaining challenge is the content depen-
dence of the mapping between control parameters
and actual bitrate. Spatial quality maps, ROI
coding, and block-level allocation provide finer
adaptation to heterogeneous image content (Dong
et al., 2024; Kao et al., 2023; Y. Ma et al.,
2021; X. Wang et al., 2022), but local decisions
must ultimately satisfy a global bitrate constraint.
Joint modeling of global rate budgets and spatial
importance is therefore a promising direction.
More generally, rate adaptation interacts with
other
deployment
requirements.
Low-precision
inference should preserve the behavior of the rate-
control mechanism, while robustness and trans-
mission resilience may change both the available
bitrate and its preferred allocation. Extending
10


these principles to video further introduces tempo-
ral dependencies, buffer constraints, and sequen-
tial allocation, as discussed in Sec. 5.
3.2 Model Quantization and
Cross-Platform Consistency
As illustrated in Fig. 4, model quantization
plays a particularly important role in practi-
cal LIC. Unlike the signal quantization discussed
in Sec. 2, which discretizes latent representa-
tions for entropy coding, model quantization
reduces the numerical precision of network weights
and activations. It serves two closely related
deployment objectives. First, low-precision arith-
metic reduces model size, memory traffic, and
computational cost, and facilitates execution on
integer-oriented accelerators. Second, determinis-
tic numerical behavior is required by a codec
whose encoder and decoder may run on different
hardware and software platforms.
The latter requirement makes quantization in
LIC fundamentally different from that in con-
ventional recognition networks. In a classification
model, small numerical differences usually result
only in small changes in the output scores. In a
learned codec, however, the entropy decoder relies
on probability parameters reproduced at both the
encoder and decoder. Small floating-point dis-
crepancies in this shared probability-estimation
path may produce different probability tables
and eventually desynchronize entropy decoding.
Model quantization is therefore not only a model-
compression technique, but also an important
mechanism for codec interoperability.
Early work by Ball´e et al. (Ball´e, John-
ston, & Minnen, 2018) demonstrated that learned
compression can be implemented using integer
arithmetic, providing a foundation for determin-
istic execution. Subsequent studies have explored
different quantization strategies for balancing
R–D performance, computational efficiency, and
numerical consistency. We review these methods
from three perspectives: the optimization proce-
dure, the precision granularity, and the level of
cross-platform consistency required by the codec.
3.2.1 Quantization-Aware Training
and Post-Training Quantization
From the optimization perspective, model quan-
tization methods can be broadly divided into
quantization-aware training (QAT) and post-
training quantization (PTQ). The two approaches
differ mainly in whether the model is allowed to
adapt to low-precision arithmetic during training.
Quantization-aware training. QAT simu-
lates low-precision arithmetic during optimiza-
tion so that model parameters can adapt to
the resulting quantization error. Early studies
first quantized convolutional weights (Sun, Cheng,
Takeuchi, & Katto, 2020; Sun, Yu, & Katto,
2021b) and later extended low-precision computa-
tion to both weights and activations (Sun, Yu, &
Katto, 2021a). Compared with directly quantiz-
ing a pretrained network, QAT generally provides
greater flexibility in compensating for numerical
errors, but requires access to the training data and
an additional training stage.
LIC also introduces compression-specific dif-
ficulties that are not fully captured by generic
neural-network quantization. The transform and
entropy models affect not only reconstruction fea-
tures but also latent statistics and probability
estimation. Consequently, quantization error may
influence both distortion and coding rate. Hong
et al. (Hong, Chen, Lu, Pu, & Ma, 2020) devel-
oped fixed-point inference with adaptive numer-
ical ranges for efficient neural image decoding.
Q-LIC (Sun et al., 2022) reduces quantization
error by splitting channels with large dynamic
ranges, while IQ-LIC (G.-W. Jeon, Yu, & Lee,
2023) introduces quantization-related supervision
to improve integer inference. QAT has also been
combined with structured pruning (Hossain &
Zhu, 2024) and variable-rate coding (Ye, Li, & Liu,
2025), illustrating that model precision must often
be optimized together with other deployment con-
straints.
The main advantage of QAT is therefore its
ability to preserve R–D performance under a
prescribed numerical format. Its main limitation
is deployment flexibility: every change in preci-
sion configuration may require additional opti-
mization, which becomes costly when multiple
hardware targets or codec configurations must be
supported.

Post-training quantization. PTQ converts
a pretrained floating-point codec to low precision
without full retraining. This substantially reduces
deployment cost and makes it possible to quantize
existing models after model development. Generic
PTQ methods typically minimize the discrepancy
between floating-point and quantized weights or
activations (Y. Li et al., 2021; Nagel et al., 2021),
but such criteria are not necessarily aligned with
the objective of image compression.
He et al. (D. He, Yang, Chen, et al., 2022)
adapt PTQ to learned image compression and
combine low-precision inference with determinis-
tic entropy coding for cross-platform deployment.
RDO-PTQ (J. Shi, Lu, & Ma, 2023) further shows
that minimizing tensor-level quantization error
does not necessarily minimize the degradation in
compression performance. Instead, the quantiza-
tion configuration should be optimized according
to its effect on the R–D objective. This observation
is particularly relevant to LIC because different
modules affect rate and distortion in substantially
different ways.
QAT and PTQ therefore provide complemen-
tary deployment strategies. QAT offers stronger
adaptation to low precision and is attractive when
the target hardware configuration is known during
model development. PTQ preserves the original
training pipeline and is more suitable for con-
verting existing codecs or supporting multiple
deployment targets. In both cases, however, deter-
mining where and how aggressively to quantize the
codec is as important as choosing the optimization
procedure itself.
3.2.2 Precision Granularity and
Mixed-Precision Quantization
A second design dimension concerns the granu-
larity of numerical precision. The simplest strat-
egy applies a uniform precision, such as INT8,
throughout the network. Uniform quantization
is straightforward to implement and maps well
to commonly available low-precision hardware.
However, different codec modules, layers, and
channels can exhibit substantially different sensi-
tivities to numerical error. Applying the same bit
width everywhere may therefore allocate exces-
sive precision to insensitive components while
over-quantizing sensitive ones.
Mixed-precision
quantization
(MPQ)
addresses this issue by assigning different numer-
ical precisions according to their impact on
compression performance. FMPQ (Hossain, Duan,
& Zhu, 2024) and mixed-precision PTQ (J. Yu,
Mai, Zhang, Jiang, & Cheng, 2025b) evaluate the
sensitivity of individual components to quanti-
zation and allocate precision accordingly. Other
approaches perform finer-grained selection over
candidate bit widths (J. Yang, Wang, Li, Qiao, &
Zhou, 2025), while DynaQuant (Bao et al., 2026)
12


introduces dynamic routing among different pre-
cision configurations. These methods enlarge the
design space from selecting a single bit width to
jointly optimizing precision allocation and R–D
performance.
Another line of work modifies the numerical
distribution before quantization. Long-tailed or
highly non-uniform activations may lead to poor
utilization of a uniformly spaced quantization
range. Distribution balancing or transformation
can reduce this mismatch and improve PTQ per-
formance. For example, Yu et al. (J. Yu, Mai,
Zhang, Jiang, & Cheng, 2025a) adapt weight and
activation distributions to reduce quantization
error before low-precision inference.
From an algorithmic perspective, fine-grained

an

From

on hardware support. A theoretically favorable
combination of, for example, 4-, 6-, and 8-bit
operations does not necessarily translate into
lower latency if the target accelerator executes
these data types inefficiently or requires frequent
precision conversion. Thus, precision allocation
should ultimately be evaluated using both R–D
sensitivity and the operator support of the target
hardware, rather than bit width alone.
3.2.3 Cross-Platform Consistency
For a codec, low-precision inference and cross-
platform consistency are related but not identical
objectives. Quantizing the complete network may
improve hardware efficiency, but deterministic
entropy decoding only requires numerical agree-
ment in the computation that is shared between
the encoder and decoder and affects symbol prob-
abilities. This distinction provides an opportunity
to achieve interoperability without unnecessarily
quantizing every component of the codec.
Koyuncu et al. (Koyuncu, Solovyev, Alshina,
& Kaup, 2022) identify the learned probability
model as a primary source of device-dependent
entropy-decoding mismatch. Their results show
that, for the studied still-image codecs, quantizing
the shared entropy-related modules is sufficient
to obtain consistent entropy decoding while leav-
ing other parts of the transform in floating point.
Subsequent work further quantizes decoder com-
putation to provide deterministic reconstruction
(Koyuncu, Solovyev, Sauer, Alshina, & Kaup,
2024). These results suggest two different levels
of numerical consistency: entropy-decoding con-
sistency, which guarantees that the transmitted
bitstream is decoded into the same latent symbols,
and reconstruction consistency, which addition-
ally requires the decoded pixels to be numerically
reproducible across platforms.
Alternative mechanisms attempt to tolerate or
remove the source of numerical mismatch. Pang
et al. (Pang, Lodhi, Ahn, Huang, & Tian, 2024)
introduce auxiliary safeguarding information that
allows the decoder to tolerate limited numerical
deviations, although the additional information
increases the bitstream overhead. Rather than
forcing identical learned probability estimation,
Yang et al. (R. Yang, Liu, Wu, & Gao, 2025)
explore cross-platform entropy coding based on
chain coding, providing another route toward
reproducible decoding.
This distinction between full-network quanti-
zation and selective deterministic computation is
important in practice. If interoperability is the
primary goal, quantizing only the modules that
affect entropy decoding can preserve most of the
floating-point transform while minimizing the R–
D penalty. If low-power or accelerator-oriented
inference is also required, broader or full-network
quantization becomes necessary. Practical codec
design should therefore determine the required
level of numerical consistency before deciding the
scope of quantization.
3.2.4 Discussion and Perspective
Model quantization in learned image coding serves
two related but distinct deployment objectives:
efficient low-precision inference and determinis-
tic codec operation. The former generally favors
broader quantization of transforms and entropy
models, whereas the latter may require deter-
ministic computation only along the encoder–
decoder shared probability-estimation path. Selec-
tive quantization can therefore provide cross-
platform entropy-decoding consistency with lim-
ited
R–D
degradation,
while
full
or
mixed-
precision quantization further targets runtime and
energy efficiency.
The practical benefit of a numerical for-
mat also depends strongly on hardware sup-
port. Although mixed-precision designs can reduce
13


quantization error, accelerators typically provide
efficient kernels for only a limited set of data
types. Consequently, precision selection should
consider operator availability, memory behavior,
and execution efficiency together with R–D sen-
sitivity. This issue becomes more challenging in
predictive video coding, where small numerical
mismatches may accumulate along the prediction
chain, making efficient deterministic decoding an
important open problem (Concei¸c˜ao, Porto, Peng,
& Agostini, 2025; Van Rozendaal et al., 2024).
3.3 Robustness and Communication
Resilience
Beyond nominal R–D performance, practical LIC
must remain reliable when the input distribution,
numerical conditions, or communication environ-
ment deviates from the ideal setting used during
training and evaluation. This requirement is par-
ticularly important for learned codecs because
nonlinear transforms and data-driven entropy
models may respond unpredictably to small per-
turbations. A perturbed input may affect not only
reconstruction quality, but also latent statistics,
probability estimation, and ultimately the coded
bitrate.
In this survey, robustness is therefore consid-
ered as a system-level property rather than only
an adversarial-learning problem. It includes sensi-
tivity to adversarial perturbations and backdoor
attacks, but also repeated compression, benign
distribution shifts, packet loss or bitstream cor-
ruption, and the propagation of compression
errors to downstream vision tasks. These fail-
ure modes arise at different stages of the coding
and communication pipeline and expose differ-
ent trade-offs between compression efficiency and
reliability.

image compression (Y. Yu et al., 2024, 2023),
while compression-resistant trigger designs aim to
preserve malicious behavior after image coding
(M. Xue et al., 2023). These attacks are espe-
cially relevant when models are trained, adapted,
or distributed using data and checkpoints from
untrusted sources.
From a deployment perspective, adversarial
vulnerability is important not only because a
codec may be intentionally attacked, but also
because these studies reveal regions of numerical
and statistical instability in learned representa-
tions. They therefore provide useful stress tests for
evaluating codec reliability beyond conventional
clean-image benchmarks.
3.3.2 Robustification Strategies
Existing defenses can be broadly categorized
according to where robustness is introduced: in the
codec architecture, during training, or at inference
time.
Architecture-level robustness. The struc-
ture of the entropy model can strongly affect
sensitivity to input perturbations. Highly expres-
sive autoregressive models improve probability
estimation under nominal conditions, but they
also introduce stronger dependencies among coded
symbols. Studies of manipulation attacks suggest
that simplifying or stabilizing these dependencies
can improve robustness (K. Liu et al., 2022, 2023).
This reveals a recurring trade-off in practical LIC:
stronger statistical modeling may improve R–D
efficiency while increasing sensitivity to distribu-
tion mismatch or error propagation.
Training-time robustification. Adversarial
training incorporates perturbed samples during
optimization so that the codec learns to main-
tain stable reconstruction under bounded input
changes (T. Chen & Ma, 2023; Madry, Makelov,
Schmidt, Tsipras, & Vladu, 2018). Compression-
specific variants can combine robust optimiza-
tion with representation consistency or knowledge
transfer. For example, Cao et al. (Cao et al., 2024)
integrate knowledge distillation with adversarial
training, while successive-coding stability can be
improved by constraining latent or reconstruction
consistency across repeated compression cycles
(J.-H. Kim, Jang, Choi, & Lee, 2020). Training-
based methods generally provide stronger adap-
tation to known perturbation models, but they
increase training cost and may introduce a penalty
on clean-image R–D performance.
Inference-time protection. Robustness can
also be improved without modifying the origi-
nal training procedure. Representative strategies
include input preprocessing, self-ensemble, ran-
domized transformations, and test-time filtering
(T. Chen & Ma, 2023; Song, Choi, & Han, 2024).
Compression itself can also act as a preprocessing
defense by removing high-frequency adversarial
noise (X. Jia, Wei, Cao, & Foroosh, 2019). Such
approaches are attractive because they can be
applied to existing codecs, but their effectiveness
may depend strongly on the attack model and can
degrade against adaptive perturbations.
These three classes of methods provide differ-
ent practical trade-offs. Architecture-level design
changes the codec itself, training-time defenses
increase
development
cost
but
can
provide
stronger robustness, and inference-time defenses
offer greater flexibility at the expense of less
predictable protection.
3.3.3 Practical Robustness and
Communication Resilience
Adversarial attacks represent only one form of
non-ideal input. In deployed compression sys-
tems, more common reliability problems arise
from repeated processing, content variation, and
imperfect communication channels.
Repeated compression. Images are often
compressed multiple times as they move across
storage,
editing,
communication,
and
social-
media pipelines. Learned codecs may exhibit
accumulated reconstruction drift under succes-
sive encoding–decoding cycles (J.-H. Kim et
al., 2020). Unlike a single-pass R–D evaluation,
repeated compression exposes whether reconstruc-
tion errors alter the latent distribution in a way
15


that is amplified by subsequent coding stages.
Robustness to such repeated processing is there-
fore relevant to practical interoperability and
long-term media reuse.
Transmission errors. A more direct com-
munication challenge is packet loss or bitstream
corruption. Conventional coding systems typically
rely on channel-side protection, such as forward
error correction (FEC) (Mizuochi, 2006). Learned
codecs provide an additional possibility: the rep-
resentation and decoder can themselves be opti-
mized for incomplete or corrupted observations.
LossResilientLIC (Sha et al., 2025) incorpo-
rates network perturbations during training and
constructs representations that remain decodable
when part of the transmitted information is lost.
ResiComp (S. Wang et al., 2025) similarly stud-
ies loss-resilient representations through masked
visual-token modeling, while progressive probabil-
ity models have been explored for streaming over
unreliable channels (H. Zhang, Koyuncu, Ahonen,
Zou, & Cricri, 2025). These approaches shift part
of the reliability problem from channel coding to
source representation.
However, robustness to packet loss is closely
coupled with entropy modeling. Autoregressive
dependencies can improve coding efficiency under
error-free
transmission,
but
missing
symbols
may invalidate the context required for subse-
quent decoding and amplify error propagation.
More independent or hierarchical representations
can reduce this dependency, but often at a
cost in nominal R–D performance. Communi-
cation resilience therefore introduces an explicit
performance–reliability trade-off.
Interaction
with
downstream
tasks.
Compression is increasingly used as a front-end
component of recognition, detection, and other
machine-vision pipelines. In such systems, codec
robustness cannot be evaluated solely from recon-
structed PSNR or perceptual quality. Compres-
sion may suppress adversarial noise and improve
downstream stability (X. Jia et al., 2019), but arti-
facts or manipulated coding behavior may also
propagate to later models (Y. Liu, Gu, Zhang, &
Qin, 2024; Sui et al., 2024). The relevant system-
level criterion is therefore whether the compressed
representation preserves the information required
by the downstream task under realistic perturba-
tions.
3.3.4 Discussion and Perspective
Robustness in learned image coding should be
viewed as a system-level reliability problem rather
than an isolated adversarial-learning issue. Prac-
tical evaluation should therefore consider not only
performance under perturbations or packet loss,
but also the clean-condition R–D cost introduced
by the corresponding robustness mechanism. In
particular, stronger protection may require addi-
tional training, redundancy, simplified entropy
dependencies, or error-resilient representations,
each of which can affect bitrate, distortion, or
complexity.
A remaining challenge is the lack of common
evaluation protocols across different robustness
settings. Perturbation budgets, attack objectives,
packetization strategies, and channel models vary
substantially across studies, making direct com-
parison difficult. Standardized test conditions that
jointly report clean performance and degradation
under specified impairments would therefore be
valuable for deployment-oriented LIC. Overall, the
desired codec should remain stable under input
variation and degrade gracefully when transmitted
information is incomplete.

3.4.1 Knowledge Distillation
Knowledge distillation (KD) transfers informa-
tion from a high-capacity teacher model to a
smaller student network (Gou, Yu, Maybank, &
Tao, 2021). In learned image compression, this
objective is more involved than matching class
predictions in recognition networks, because the
student must reproduce not only reconstruction
behavior but also compact latent representations
and accurate probability models.
Early
approaches
use
reconstruction-
or
entropy-related supervision to transfer coding
behavior from the teacher (Fu et al., 2024). More
recent studies introduce finer-grained supervision
at intermediate features or latent representations
(Allemand, Fiandrotti, Chaudhuri, & Mazouz,
2025). Chen et al. (Y. Chen et al., 2025) further
perform multi-level distillation across network
blocks, allowing the lightweight student to inherit
representation and reconstruction capability from
a larger model.
Rather than explicitly defining distillation
losses, EVC (G.-H. Wang, Li, Li, & Lu, 2023)
adopts a mask-decay strategy that progressively
reduces the effective model capacity during train-
ing, thereby transferring the behavior of a larger
network into a compact architecture. CSLIC
(S. Wang et al., 2026) further combines KD
with neural architecture search to derive mul-
tiple encoder–decoder configurations at different
complexity levels.
From a deployment perspective, KD is attrac-
tive because complexity reduction is achieved
mainly during model development and does not
introduce additional inference-time modules. Its
effectiveness, however, depends on how much of
the teacher’s coding capability can be retained
under a substantially smaller transform or entropy
model. Distilling only reconstruction features may
therefore be insufficient when the dominant per-
formance loss originates from probability model-
ing or latent representation capacity.
3.4.2 Model Compression:
Quantization and Pruning
Model compression provides a second route to
lightweight LIC by reducing the numerical or
structural redundancy of a trained network. Quan-
tization reduces the precision of weights and acti-
vations and can substantially improve memory
and arithmetic efficiency (Gholami et al., 2022).
Because its implications for R–D performance and
cross-platform consistency have been discussed in
detail in Sec. 3.2, we focus here primarily on its
role as a computational optimization.
Pruning instead removes redundant parame-
ters or computational structures from the network
(Zhu & Gupta, 2017). For LIC, structured pruning
is generally more relevant to practical deployment
than unstructured sparsity because removing com-
plete channels or filters can translate more directly
into reduced computation. Liao et al. (Liao et al.,
2022) apply structured pruning specifically to the
17


decoder, demonstrating that substantial complex-
ity reduction can be achieved while retaining com-
petitive reconstruction performance. Quantization
and pruning can also be combined (Hossain &
Zhu, 2024), jointly reducing arithmetic precision
and network capacity.
The practical effectiveness of model com-
pression
ultimately
depends
on
whether
the
resulting sparsity or numerical precision is effi-
ciently supported by the target platform. Param-
eter reduction alone does not necessarily imply
proportional runtime reduction. Consequently,
hardware-oriented compression should consider
deployable tensor shapes, supported numerical
formats, and efficient operators rather than only
nominal parameter counts.
3.4.3 Architecture-Level Design
A more direct strategy is to design the codec
for efficiency from the beginning rather than
compressing an already expensive model. This
includes reducing transform complexity, simpli-
fying
entropy
dependencies,
improving
paral-
lelism, and allocating computation asymmetri-
cally between the encoder and decoder. Such
architecture-level
optimization
is
particularly
important because computational structures that
provide strong R–D performance on GPUs may
not map efficiently to mobile or edge accelerators.
Efficient entropy modeling. Entropy mod-
els are a major source of practical decoding over-
head. Autoregressive contexts improve probabil-
ity estimation by exploiting dependencies among
latent symbols, but their sequential nature can
restrict parallelism and increase latency. Ali et
al. (Ali et al., 2023) explore efficient learned
compression without conventional autoregressive
dependencies, illustrating that part of the R–D
gain from stronger context modeling can be traded
for substantially improved execution efficiency.
More generally, reducing the number and granu-
larity of sequential context steps is an important
design dimension when decoding throughput is a
primary constraint.
Asymmetric codec design. Encoder and
decoder complexity need not be balanced. In many
storage, delivery, or content-distribution scenar-
ios, an image is encoded once but decoded repeat-
edly on heterogeneous client devices. This moti-
vates shifting computation toward the encoder
while maintaining a lightweight decoder. Asym-
LLIC
(S.
Wang
et
al.,
2024),
for
example,
explicitly adopts an asymmetric encoder–decoder
architecture to reduce decoding complexity. Shal-
lowNTC (Y. Yang & Mandt, 2023) pushes this
principle further by using a shallow, nearly linear
synthesis transform, achieving performance com-
petitive with BPG at less than 50 KMACs/pixel
decoding complexity.
Efficient operators and dynamic com-
putation. Another direction replaces expensive
neural operations with more hardware-friendly
alternatives. Lightweight attention (X. Chen et
al., 2026; Z. He, Huang, Luo, Yang, & Zhu,
2024), efficient convolutions (M. Li et al., 2022),
and shift-based processing have all been explored
to reduce redundant computation. ShiftLIC (Bao
et al., 2025), for example, replaces computation-
ally expensive spatial operations with spatial–
channel shifts and small convolutions. ULIC
(Dong, Sha, Lu, & Ma, 2023) similarly tar-
gets highly constrained wearable-device settings
through an explicitly lightweight codec design.
FastNIC (H. Zhang, Li, Li, & Liu, 2025) jointly
considers transform and prior complexity through
switchable entropy priors, achieving BPG-level or
better performance on Kodak with encoding and
decoding complexities below approximately 12
and 10 KMACs/pixel, respectively. Beyond fixed
architectures, AdaNIC (Tao, Gao, Li, & Zhang,
2023) uses dynamic transform routing to adapt
computational paths according to image content.
Such conditional computation provides another
dimension of flexibility: instead of assigning the
same complexity to every image, the codec can
allocate computation according to coding diffi-
culty.
These examples suggest that lightweight LIC
increasingly requires joint optimization of the
transform and entropy-model execution paths.
Reducing the complexity of only one component
may leave another component as the runtime
bottleneck, while nominal MAC reduction may
provide limited benefit if the resulting operators
are poorly supported by the target hardware.
3.4.4 Test-Time Adaptation
Test-time adaptation (TTA) provides a different
approach to the performance–complexity trade-
off. Rather than permanently increasing model
18


capacity, TTA spends additional computation or
transmits a small number of instance-specific
parameters to adapt a shared codec to the cur-
rent image or domain (D. Chen, Wang, Darrell,
& Ebrahimi, 2022; Liang, He, & Tan, 2025).
In learned compression, this strategy is closely
related to the amortization gap of VAE-based
models, where a fixed encoder may not produce
the optimal latent representation for every input
(Y. Yang, Bamler, & Mandt, 2020).
Latent refinement. Encoder-side adaptation
directly optimizes the latent representation for the
current image under the R–D objective. Campos
et al. (Campos, Meierhans, Djelouah, & Schroers,
2019) refine latent variables through iterative opti-
mization, while Yang et al. (Y. Yang et al., 2020)
jointly optimize latent representations and associ-
ated side information to reduce the amortization
gap and discretization error. These approaches
can improve compression performance without
increasing decoder complexity or model size, but
require additional optimization iterations during
encoding.
Decoder-side
adaptation. Another fam-
ily of methods adapts part of the decoder and
transmits the corresponding parameter updates
together with the bitstream. Instance-adaptive
compression (van Rozendaal, Huijben, & Cohen,
2021)
optimizes
decoder
and
entropy-model
parameters for individual inputs, while related
work restricts adaptation to a smaller subset of
parameters, such as biases or multiplicative coeffi-
cients (Lam, Zare, Cricri, Lainema, & Hannuksela,
2020; Zou et al., 2021). The additional adaptation
can improve coding efficiency, but its benefit must
compensate for both the optimization cost and the
bits required to signal the parameter updates.
Parameter-efficient transfer learning provides
a more lightweight alternative. Inspired by tech-
niques such as LoRA (E.J. Hu et al., 2022), recent
compression methods introduce compact adapters
or low-rank updates instead of modifying the full
decoder (Lv et al., 2023; Shen, Yue, & Yang, 2023;
Tsubota, Akutsu, & Aizawa, 2023). Such modules
are particularly attractive for adapting a com-
mon codec to different content domains. Chen et
al. (K. Chen et al., 2025) further study test-time
adaptation under distribution shifts and regular-
ize adaptation of the probability model to improve
cross-domain compression.
TTA therefore exposes a different form of
resource allocation from lightweight architecture
design. A compact shared model can recover part
of the performance of a larger model by spending
additional encoder-side computation or transmit-
ting instance-specific information. This trade-off
is attractive when decoding complexity and per-
sistent model size are strictly constrained but
additional encoding effort is acceptable.
3.4.5 Discussion and Perspective
Lightweight LIC should be evaluated as a system-
level performance–complexity problem. Param-
eter
count
and
KMACs/pixel
provide
useful
architecture-level proxies, but they do not fully
characterize
deployment
efficiency.
Sequential
entropy decoding, memory movement, intermedi-
ate feature size, operator availability, and hard-
ware utilization can cause models with similar
arithmetic complexity to exhibit substantially dif-
ferent runtime behavior. Actual latency, mem-
ory consumption, and target hardware should
therefore be considered together with algorithmic
complexity.
More
broadly,
reducing
model
complexity
is useful only when the resulting computation
maps efficiently to the target platform. Distil-
lation, pruning, quantization, architecture-level
redesign, and test-time adaptation provide com-
plementary ways to trade model capacity, numer-
ical precision, and encoder-side computation for
efficiency. Practical LIC should therefore opti-
mize the R–D–complexity trade-off jointly with
entropy-model dependencies, numerical precision,
memory behavior, parallelism, and hardware-
supported operators. The TinyLIC benchmark in
Sec. 4 follows this perspective by exposing multi-
ple computational budgets under a common codec
topology.
3.5 Standardization and
Interoperability
Standardization represents a critical step in the
transition from high-performance learned com-
pression models to deployable codecs. A practical
coding standard must define not only compression
performance, but also interoperable bitstreams,
decoder behavior, operating profiles, complexity
configurations, test conditions, and conformance
19


requirements. These issues are particularly impor-
tant for LIC, where model architecture, numerical
precision, entropy-model execution, and hard-
ware implementation can all affect reproducibility
across platforms.
Recent standardization activities indicate that
learned image coding is moving beyond isolated
research prototypes toward interoperable coding
systems (Gao, 2025b; D. Liu, Liu, Ascenso, Tian,
& Yu, 2024). Two representative efforts are JPEG
AI and IEEE 1857.11. Although their specific
architectures and evaluation methodologies differ,
both retain the basic learned transform-coding
paradigm reviewed in Sec. 2: an analysis transform
maps the input image to quantized latent repre-
sentations, which are entropy coded and recon-
structed by a learned decoder. More importantly,
both standards extend this basic formulation with
explicit considerations of complexity, functional-
ity, and implementation.
3.5.1 JPEG AI
JPEG AI is an international standard for end-
to-end learning-based image coding developed by
the ISO/IEC Joint Photographic Experts Group
(Alshina, Ascenso, & Ebrahimi, 2024; Ascenso
et al., 2023). The activity was initiated in 2019
and has subsequently evolved into the ISO/IEC
6048 / ITU-T T.840 family of standards. Rather
than specifying only a high-performance neu-
ral model, JPEG AI addresses a broader set of
system requirements, including multi-complexity
decoding, rate adaptation, progressive and par-
tial decoding, compressed-domain processing, and
interoperable
implementation
(Esenlik
et
al.,
2026).
Multi-complexity
coding
architecture.
An
important
characteristic
of
JPEG
AI
is
the availability of decoder configurations span-
ning substantially different computational bud-
gets. Reported configurations range from approxi-
mately 8 to more than 200 KMACs/pixel, allowing
the same standardization framework to target dif-
ferent hardware classes (Esenlik et al., 2026). A
configuration of approximately 23 KMACs/pixel
has also been demonstrated in mobile-oriented
implementations, illustrating the importance of
explicitly considering decoder complexity when
defining a practical learned codec.
The coding framework also adopts structured
color processing to balance compression efficiency
and memory consumption. Input images are con-
verted to a YUV representation, where luminance
is encoded using a dedicated neural path and
chrominance components are coded conditionally
on the reconstructed luminance. Such asymmet-
ric processing exploits inter-channel correlation
while reducing the computational and memory
requirements of chrominance coding (Esenlik et
al., 2026).
Extended coding functionalities. JPEG
AI incorporates several functionalities beyond
conventional single-point image reconstruction.
Progressive decoding is enabled by organizing
transmitted representations according to their
importance, so that reconstruction quality can
improve as additional information becomes avail-
able. Spatially partial decoding is supported
through tiled representations, allowing selected
image regions to be reconstructed without pro-
cessing the complete image. These capabilities
connect standardization with the variable-rate
and progressive coding mechanisms discussed in
Sec. 3.1.
The standard also considers processing directly
from compressed representations. Representative
functionality includes image classification, super-
resolution, and denoising (Ascenso et al., 2023;
Esenlik et al., 2026). This direction extends the
role of the coded representation beyond human-
view reconstruction and provides an early stan-
dardized interface between image compression
and downstream visual processing. Recent stud-
ies have further investigated compressed-domain
analysis using JPEG AI representations (Alkha-
teeb et al., 2024).
Evaluation and implementation. JPEG
AI places substantial emphasis on perceptual qual-
ity and reproducible evaluation. Its Common Test
and Training Conditions (CTTC) specify common
datasets, coding conditions, and perceptual qual-
ity measures. The evaluation framework includes
metrics such as MS-SSIM, IW-SSIM, VMAF, VIF,
NLPD, and FSIM, reflecting a broader quality
criterion than conventional PSNR-oriented evalu-
ation (Ascenso et al., 2023; Esenlik et al., 2026).
Implementation is also explicitly considered. In
representative implementations, entropy decoding
can be executed on CPUs to preserve bit-exact
20


behavior, while computationally intensive neu-
ral transforms are mapped to GPUs or NPUs.
Such heterogeneous execution illustrates a prac-
tical principle that also appears throughout this
survey: the most efficient implementation need
not execute the complete codec using a single
numerical format or hardware backend. Instead,
deterministic and computationally intensive com-
ponents can be assigned to different processors
according to their requirements.
JPEG AI has consequently become an impor-
tant platform for studying deployment-oriented
LIC. Recent work has examined its variable-rate
coding and bitrate matching mechanisms (P. Jia
et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025), robustness under
distribution shifts and adversarial perturbations
(Kovalev et al., 2024), adaptation to remote-
sensing content (J. Zhang, Pan, Chen, & Liu,
2025), and compressed-domain inference (Alkha-
teeb et al., 2024). These studies show that stan-
dardization does not terminate codec develop-
ment; rather, it provides a common framework
within which practical coding functions can be
evaluated and extended.
3.5.2 IEEE 1857.11
IEEE 1857.11-2024 provides another standardiza-
tion path for neural network-based image cod-
ing (IEEE, 2024). The standard was developed
through the IEEE 1857.11 Subworking Group
and formally published in 2024. A distinguishing
characteristic is its use of multiple profiles target-
ing different complexity–performance operating
regions.
Complexity-oriented
profiles.
IEEE
1857.11 defines Base, Main, and High profiles.
The
corresponding
reference
implementations
include BEE, NIC, and iWave (T. Chen et al.,

Liu, Yan, Li, & Wu, 2020; Z. Zhang et al., 2023).
These profiles illustrate different design points in
the R–D–complexity space.
The Base profile emphasizes low-complexity
implementation and incorporates efficient mecha-
nisms such as wavefront parallel processing, adap-
tive quantization, and feature scaling. The Main
profile adopts a more expressive learned coding
architecture with non-local processing, hierarchi-
cal priors, and context-based entropy modeling,
providing a stronger balance between coding effi-
ciency and complexity. The High profile employs a
learned wavelet-like transform and targets higher
compression performance. Rather than enforcing
a single neural architecture for every deployment
scenario, the profile structure explicitly recognizes
that different applications have different compu-
tational budgets.
Common evaluation conditions. The ver-
ification
of
IEEE
1857.11
considers
images
across multiple resolutions, including ultra-high-
resolution content and commonly used benchmark
material. Common training and evaluation condi-
tions are used to reduce variations caused by train-
ing data and testing protocols. Under its reported
evaluation settings, the standard demonstrates
substantial bitrate savings relative to BPG under
PSNR and MS-SSIM-based comparison (IEEE,
2024).
This standardized evaluation environment is
important because cross-paper comparison of LIC
models can otherwise be strongly affected by
training data, color conversion, resolution han-
dling, entropy-coder implementation, and anchor
configuration. The increasing use of IEEE 1857.11
as a reference point in subsequent coding studies
(Z. Li, Liao, et al., 2025; Ye, Liu, Li, & Liu, 2025)
further illustrates the value of a common stan-
dardized baseline beyond the individual models
contained in the standard.
3.5.3 Discussion and Perspective
The emergence of JPEG AI and IEEE 1857.11
marks an important transition from research-
oriented neural compression models to interop-
erable codec systems. Unlike cross-paper R–D
evaluation, standardization requires reproducible
bitstream syntax, decoder behavior, numerical
consistency, complexity profiles, and conformance
procedures. These requirements make implemen-
tation constraints and interoperability first-class
codec-design considerations rather than secondary
engineering details.
A remaining challenge is how to reconcile rapid
model evolution with long-term decoding compat-
ibility. Learned codecs can continue to improve
through new architectures, training strategies,
and model updates, whereas a coding standard
must preserve stable decoder behavior across

4 TinyLIC: A Reproducible
Practical Benchmark
The practical requirements reviewed in Sec. 3 are
typically studied in isolation and often evaluated
using different codec architectures, training set-
tings, and test protocols. This makes it difficult
to determine whether an observed gain or degra-
dation originates from the deployment mechanism
itself or from differences in the underlying com-
pression model. A common experimental platform
is therefore useful for studying these system-level
properties under controlled conditions.
To this end, we introduce TinyLIC as an
open and reproducible benchmark for deployment-
oriented learned image coding. TinyLIC provides
a lightweight reference architecture together with
configurable complexity levels and modular sup-
port for representative practical functions. Its pur-
pose is to make the interactions among compres-
sion efficiency, computational complexity, con-
trollability, numerical consistency, and robustness
measurable within a shared codec framework.
As illustrated in Fig. 7, TinyLIC exposes
three
complementary
dimensions
of
practical
codec
design.
First,
the
backbone
can
be
scaled across multiple computational budgets,
enabling direct analysis of the R–D–complexity
relationship. Second, both fidelity-oriented and
perceptual-realism-oriented optimization are sup-
ported, allowing the same framework to cover
different reconstruction objectives. Third, prac-
tical functions corresponding to the deployment
issues reviewed in Sec. 3 can be enabled as con-
figurable modules, including variable-rate coding,
quantization-aware consistent decoding, adver-
sarially robust training, progressive coding, and
network-aware loss-resilient coding.
This organization allows each practical func-
tion to be evaluated against a common base-
line rather than across independently developed

what cost it introduces in bitrate, distortion,
computational complexity, or clean-condition per-

experimental platform for studying the trade-offs
that arise when a learned compression model is
extended toward a practical codec.
The remainder of this section first introduces
the design philosophy and lightweight architec-
ture of TinyLIC, followed by the implementa-
tion of its deployment-oriented tools. We then
evaluate the resulting rate–distortion–perception
performance, practical functionality, and model
complexity under a common experimental proto-
col.

4.1.1 Efficient Architecture
As illustrated in Fig. 8, TinyLIC follows a
lightweight VAE-based coding pipeline.
Lightweight transform. The main trans-
forms are constructed primarily from convolu-
tional operations rather than computationally
intensive attention modules. The basic unit is an
efficient depthwise ConvNeXt-style block, termed
the EDN block, which combines lightweight spatial
filtering, normalization, pointwise projection, and
a compact feed-forward path. Downsampling and
upsampling are implemented using simple convo-
lutional and shuffle-based operations. The same
basic topology is retained across different com-
plexity configurations, making complexity scaling
easier to interpret.
Lightweight entropy modeling. TinyLIC
models the main latent representation using a
Gaussian likelihood together with a channel-
autoregressive (CAR) context model (Minnen &
Singh, 2020):

decoding complexity.
Complexity scaling. TinyLIC provides three
configurations—Nano, Small, and Base—covering
approximately 10, 20, and 50 KMACs/pixel of
decoding complexity, respectively. The configura-

block depth while preserving the overall codec
topology. This creates a controlled complexity axis
for studying how compression efficiency changes as
additional computational capacity is introduced.

adversarial optimization to improve visual realism
at low bitrates. These two variants allow the effect
of computational complexity to be studied under
both fidelity- and perception-oriented objectives
without changing the basic codec architecture.
4.1.2 Practical Tools
Beyond the lightweight backbone, TinyLIC inte-
grates five configurable tools corresponding to the
deployment issues reviewed in Sec. 3: variable-rate
coding, quantization-aware consistent decoding,
adversarially robust training, progressive coding,
and network-aware loss-resilient coding. These
modules are optional rather than mandatory,
reflecting the fact that practical functionality may
introduce different R–D or implementation costs.
Variable-rate coding. TinyLIC adopts λ-
conditioned feature modulation to support multi-
ple R–D operating points within a single model,
following the general modulation paradigm dis-
cussed in Sec. 3.1.1 (T. Chen & Ma, 2020).
Given an intermediate feature f, the modulation
is formulated as
f ′ = f + γ(λ) ⊙[α(λ) ⊙f + β(λ)] ,
(7)
where α(λ), β(λ), and γ(λ) denote learned scale,
bias, and gate parameters, respectively. Two
implementations are considered.
24


• VR-Adaptive Layer. A lightweight multi-
layer perceptron maps λ to the modulation
parameters dynamically, providing continu-
ous control over the operating point.
• VR-Dictionary. A discrete dictionary of
modulation parameters is indexed by a qual-
ity parameter (QP), with each entry associ-
ated with a predefined λ. This design pro-
vides a simpler implementation while retain-
ing effective variable-rate behavior.
Quantization-aware
consistent
decod-
ing. TinyLIC supports INT8 quantization with
channel-wise weight quantization and layer-wise
activation quantization. Two configurations are
used to distinguish numerical consistency from full
integer deployment.
• Quant-Shared. Only the modules shared by
encoder and decoder for entropy-parameter
generation are quantized. This configura-
tion targets deterministic entropy decoding
while retaining most transform computation
in floating point.
• Quant-All. The complete codec is quantized
for INT8 inference, targeting accelerator-
oriented execution in addition to entropy-
decoding consistency.
This separation directly reflects the distinction
discussed in Sec. 3.2: the numerical scope required
for bitstream interoperability can be smaller than
that required for full low-precision inference.
Adversarially robust training. To exam-
ine codec reliability under input perturbations,
TinyLIC incorporates two adversarially robust
training configurations inspired by compression-
specific robustness studies (T. Chen & Ma,
2023). Adversarial samples are generated under a
bounded perturbation constraint, including CW-
style optimization (Carlini & Wagner, 2017).
• AR-Source. The reconstruction from the
adversarial input is encouraged to remain
close to the clean source image, explicitly
promoting recovery of the original content.
• AR-Noise. The reconstruction from the
adversarial input is constrained to remain
consistent with that obtained from a noise-
perturbed reference, encouraging local stabil-
ity around the source distribution.
Both strategies modify training rather than
inference and therefore do not introduce addi-
tional runtime modules.
Variance-aware
progressive
coding.
TinyLIC
introduces
progressiveness
through
latent-element ordering rather than an indepen-
dent multi-stage codec. Following variance-aware
progressive coding (Presta, Tartaglione, Fian-
drotti, Grangetto, & Cosman, 2025), the predicted
variance of each latent element is used as a proxy
for its coding importance. More informative ele-
ments are transmitted first, while untransmitted
elements are replaced by their predicted means:
˜yi = miˆyi + (1 −mi)µi,
mi ∈{0, 1},
(8)
where mi indicates whether the i-th latent element
is available. Increasing the number of transmitted
elements therefore progressively improves recon-
struction quality without changing the basic VAE
pipeline.
Network-aware
loss-resilient
coding.
Finally,
TinyLIC
considers
unreliable
trans-
mission
in
which
compressed
representations
may experience packet loss. In the loss-resilient
configuration, the channel-autoregressive depen-
dency is removed and a hyperprior-based entropy
model is used instead. This choice reflects the
performance–resilience
trade-off
discussed
in
Sec. 3.3: autoregressive context improves com-
pression efficiency under error-free transmission
but can propagate decoding failures when context
symbols are missing (Sha et al., 2025; S. Wang et
al., 2025).
Packet loss is explicitly simulated during train-
ing using two channel models.
• LR-GE. A Gilbert–Elliott two-state Markov
model is used to simulate bursty packet loss
by alternating between good and bad channel
states.
• LR-Random. Packet losses are sampled
independently according to a fixed loss prob-
ability, providing a simpler stochastic-loss
baseline.
The five tools intentionally impose differ-
ent constraints on the same underlying codec.
Variable-rate
modulation
mainly
changes
the
operating condition; selective and full quanti-
zation modify numerical execution; adversarial
25


training changes the optimization distribution;
progressive coding constrains latent ordering; and
loss-resilient coding alters entropy dependencies.
The benchmark evaluation in Sec. 4.2 uses these
configurations to quantify how different deploy-
ment requirements affect compression efficiency
under a common architectural basis.

We
next

4.2.1 Experimental Setup
Training protocol. TinyLIC is trained on the
MLIC-100K dataset (Jiang et al., 2025). Unless
otherwise specified, all models are trained for
400 epochs with a batch size of 32. The ini-
tial learning rate is set to 2 × 10−4 and reduced
to 2 × 10−5 after 300 epochs. Fidelity-oriented
models are optimized using the conventional R–D
objective with MSE as the distortion term. The
perceptual-realism-oriented variants additionally
incorporate feature-space and adversarial objec-
tives, as described in Sec. 4.1.1.
Test
datasets. We evaluate TinyLIC on
Kodak, Tecnick (Asuni, Giachetti, et al., 2014),
CLIC Professional validation, and the JPEG AI
test set (Ascenso et al., 2023). These datasets span
different image resolutions and content character-
istics. Kodak contains 24 images at 768 × 512
resolution, Tecnick contains high-quality 1200 ×
1200 images, CLIC Professional validation con-
tains 41 images with approximately 2K resolution,
and the JPEG AI test set contains 50 natural
images ranging from approximately 1K to 4K
resolution.
For high-resolution JPEG AI images, TinyLIC
performs inference using non-overlapping tiles no
larger than 1024 × 1024 to satisfy memory con-
straints. This should be noted when interpreting
comparisons with conventional codecs that pro-
cess the complete image at full resolution.
Compared methods. For fidelity-oriented
evaluation, we use VTM Intra 22.0 under the
H.266/VVC image-profile configuration (Bross,
Chen, Ohm, Sullivan, & Wang, 2021) and BPG,
corresponding to an H.265/HEVC image-profile
anchor (Bellard, 2016), as conventional refer-
ences. Representative learned codecs include ELIC
(D. He, Yang, Peng, et al., 2022) and recent
lightweight approaches such as EVC (G.-H. Wang
et al., 2023), AsymLLIC (S. Wang et al., 2024),
ShiftLIC (Bao et al., 2025), CSLIC (S. Wang et
al., 2026), ShallowNTC (Y. Yang & Mandt, 2023),
and FastNIC (H. Zhang, Li, et al., 2025).
For perceptual-realism-oriented compression,
we compare against HiFiC (Mentzer et al., 2020)
and MS-ILLM (Muckley et al., 2023). HiFiC
represents a classical GAN-based neural codec
optimized for perceptual reconstruction, while
MS-ILLM serves as the reference method for the
reported perceptual BD-rate comparisons.
For fidelity-oriented evaluation, compression
efficiency is reported using PSNR BD-rate with
VTM Intra as the anchor. Perceptual-realism-
oriented performance is evaluated using LPIPS
and FID BD-rate with MS-ILLM as the anchor.
Computational complexity is reported in KMAC-
s/pixel.

the dictionary-based variant obtains −0.94%,
−2.21%, and −4.26%.
The apparent improvement over the fixed-
rate Base model should be interpreted carefully.
BD-rate is obtained by interpolating a finite set
of R–D operating points. The fixed-rate evalua-
tion contains four operating points, whereas the
variable-rate models are evaluated using 15 points,
resulting in denser coverage of the R–D curve
and potentially reducing interpolation error. The
result therefore primarily demonstrates that rate
flexibility can be introduced without a clear R–D
penalty.
Although
the
adaptive-layer
variant
pro-

dictionary-based variant offers a simpler imple-

quent practical configurations.
Quantization-aware
consistent
decod-
ing. Quant-Shared quantizes only the modules
required for consistent entropy decoding and
introduces a relatively small degradation relative
to the variable-rate baseline. Its PSNR BD-rate on
Kodak changes from −0.94% for VR-Dictionary
to −0.41%, while maintaining consistent decoding
across platforms.
Quant-All applies INT8 quantization more
broadly to support hardware-oriented integer

with a Kodak BD-rate of 2.21%. The difference
between Quant-Shared and Quant-All illustrates
the distinction discussed in Sec. 3.2: selective
quantization can provide codec consistency with
limited performance loss, whereas full integer

Progressive
coding. Variance-aware pro-
gressive coding introduces an additional R–D

degradation reflects the cost of constraining a
representation optimized for complete decoding
to also support meaningful reconstruction from
partial bitstreams.
Importantly, this functionality is introduced
without redesigning the overall codec backbone,
allowing the cost of progressiveness to be mea-
sured relative to the same underlying representa-
tion.
Adversarial
robustness.
Attack-resilient
training introduces a smaller clean-image penalty
than the progressive and loss-resilient configura-
tions. AR-Source achieves a Kodak PSNR BD-
rate of 0.75%, while AR-Noise reaches −0.20%.
AR-Noise also consistently preserves better clean-
image R–D performance than AR-Source across
the reported datasets.
These results illustrate an important property
of robustness-oriented training: improved behav-
ior under perturbations need not require addi-
tional inference-time computation, but it may
alter the clean-data optimum learned by the
codec.
Loss resilience. The loss-resilient variants
introduce the largest clean-channel R–D degra-
dation among the evaluated practical tools. At a
training packet-loss rate of 20%, LR-GE and LR-
Random achieve Kodak PSNR BD-rates of 9.78%
and 9.69%, respectively.
This degradation is largely attributable to the
removal of the autoregressive entropy model in
the loss-resilient configurations. While autoregres-
sive dependencies improve probability estimation
under reliable transmission, they can amplify error
propagation when transmitted symbols are miss-
ing. Replacing them with a more independent
representation therefore trades nominal coding
efficiency for transmission robustness. The sim-
ilar clean-channel behavior of LR-GE and LR-
Random further suggests that much of this cost
29


originates from the resilience-oriented entropy-
model simplification rather than from the partic-
ular packet-loss model used during training.
Taken together, these results illustrate a recur-
ring characteristic of deployment-oriented LIC:
additional functionality is generally associated
with a measurable cost, but the magnitude and
source of that cost differ substantially across
requirements. Variable-rate coding and selective
quantization can be introduced with limited R–
D degradation, whereas progressive and especially
loss-resilient coding impose stronger structural
constraints on the learned representation.

4.3 Discussion and Perspective
The TinyLIC benchmark illustrates that prac-
tical
learned
image
coding
is
inherently
a
multi-objective design problem. Compression effi-
ciency, computational complexity, rate adaptabil-
ity, numerical consistency, and robustness are cou-
pled through the underlying representation and
entropy model. Evaluating these properties under
a shared architecture therefore makes their inter-
actions more explicit than comparisons among
independently developed codecs.
Three observations emerge from the bench-
mark. First, the R–D–complexity trade-off can
be adjusted over a relatively wide range without
changing the fundamental codec topology. The
31


Nano, Small, and Base configurations show that
increasing model capacity consistently improves
coding efficiency, although the marginal gain
decreases at higher complexities. Complexity scal-
ability should therefore be regarded as a codec-
design dimension rather than merely an imple-
mentation metric.
Second,
different
deployment
requirements
introduce different costs. Variable-rate coding and
selective quantization can be incorporated with
limited R–D degradation, whereas full integer
inference imposes a stronger numerical constraint.
Progressive and loss-resilient coding further alter
the structure or dependency of the transmitted
representation and therefore introduce more vis-
ible compromises in nominal coding efficiency.
The cost of practical functionality thus depends
strongly on where the corresponding constraint is
introduced into the coding pipeline.
Third, the experiments highlight the distinc-
tion between algorithmic efficiency and practical
usability. A codec with the strongest uncon-
strained R–D performance is not necessarily the
most suitable operating point once decoding com-
plexity, numerical reproducibility, or communi-
cation reliability is considered. Practical com-
parison therefore extends beyond a conventional
R–D curve toward a broader rate–distortion–
complexity–reliability design space.
TinyLIC provides a common reference for
studying this design space rather than defining a
single optimal solution. Different applications may
prioritize different properties, such as low decod-
ing complexity, bit-exact interoperability, flexible
bitrate control, or resilience to unreliable trans-
mission. The role of the benchmark is to make
the corresponding trade-offs measurable under a
controlled codec architecture.
5 Concluding Remarks and
Future Perspectives
5.1 Concluding Remarks
End-to-end
LIC
has
progressed
from
proof-
of-concept neural compression toward increas-
ingly complete codec systems. Improvements in
learned transforms, quantization, entropy model-
ing, and end-to-end optimization have substan-
tially advanced R–D performance, but practical
adoption requires a broader set of properties. Rate
adaptability, deterministic decoding, robustness,
computational efficiency, and interoperability are
becoming integral components of codec design
rather than secondary implementation considera-
tions.
This survey has reviewed LIC from this
deployment-oriented perspective, treating the core
transform–quantization–entropy–optimization
framework as the algorithmic foundation and
focusing on the practical capabilities required
beyond nominal R–D optimization. The TinyLIC
benchmark further provides a common setting in
which representative deployment constraints can
be evaluated under controlled architectural and
computational conditions.
The standardization of JPEG AI and IEEE
1857.11 further demonstrates that learned image
coding is entering a stage of practical validation
(Alshina et al., 2024; Ascenso et al., 2023; IEEE,
2024). Bitstream conformance, decoder complex-
ity, numerical reproducibility, and implementa-
tion interoperability are becoming first-class codec
requirements. These developments provide the
basis for the broader evolution of learned coding
discussed next.
5.2 Future Perspectives
While the preceding sections focus primarily on
making current LIC architectures deployable, sev-
eral emerging developments may further change
what visual coding is expected to represent and
support. We highlight four directions that are
likely to influence the next stage of learned coding.
5.2.1 Toward Coding for Intelligence
The conventional objective of image coding is
to
reconstruct
a
signal
for
human
viewing.
Future visual communication systems, however,
will increasingly deliver the same visual informa-
tion to both humans and machines. This motivates
coding for intelligence, in which the coded rep-
resentation should support not only high-quality
reconstruction but also recognition, analysis, and
decision-making (W. Yang, Huang, Hu, Duan, &
Liu, 2024; Q. Zhang et al., 2024).
Existing studies have explored task-oriented
compression and unified human–machine repre-
sentations (X. Zhang et al., 2024; X. Zhang, Lu,
Chen, & Ma, 2025). Nevertheless, many current
32


methods remain dependent on particular down-
stream networks or tasks. Jointly training the
codec with a detector or classifier can provide
strong task performance, but it reduces generality
and makes the representation sensitive to changes
in downstream models.
A more scalable objective is therefore to con-
struct a common compressed representation that
can serve multiple consumers. Instead of maintain-
ing different bitstreams for human reconstruction,
object recognition, and semantic understanding,
future codecs may expose a shared representation
that can be selectively decoded or adapted accord-
ing to the requested task. Such a design would
extend the scalability discussed in Sec. 3 from
different rates toward different uses of the same
coded information.
The key challenge is determining what infor-
mation should be preserved when the down-
stream task is not known at encoding time.
Signal fidelity, perceptual realism, and machine
utility do not necessarily assign the same impor-
tance to visual information. Developing repre-
sentations and evaluation protocols that preserve
broadly useful information while maintaining bit-
stream interoperability therefore remains an open
problem. Recent discussions connecting efficient
representation with biological or machine intel-
ligence provide an interesting conceptual moti-
vation (Y. Huang, Zhang, Shan, & He, 2024;
J. Zheng & Meister, 2025), but translating this
idea into general-purpose coding systems requires
substantially stronger empirical foundations.
5.2.2 Beyond Latent-Grid
Representations
Most current LIC methods represent an image
using spatial latent tensors decoded by a shared
neural network. Emerging neural representations
broaden this design space. Implicit neural rep-
resentations (INRs), for example, encode a sig-
nal through the parameters or latent variables
of a coordinate-conditioned function (Sitzmann,
Martel, Bergman, Lindell, & Wetzstein, 2020).
This representation can provide continuous spatial
querying and naturally support resolution-flexible
reconstruction.
Recent work has explored INRs and related
overfitted neural representations for image, video,
and scientific-data compression (H. Kim, Bauer,
Theis, Schwarz, & Dupont, 2024; Ladune et al.,
2026; J. Shi et al., 2025, 2024; Str¨umpler, Pos-
tels, Yang, Gool, & Tombari, 2022). In parallel,
explicit neural scene representations such as Gaus-
sian primitives have become effective represen-
tations for view synthesis and immersive media
(Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimk¨uhler, Drettakis, et al.,
2023). These developments suggest that future
visual codecs may no longer be restricted to trans-
mitting a conventional latent feature map, but
may instead encode functions, primitives, or other
scene-level representations.
Such representations also introduce differ-
ent deployment trade-offs. Instance-specific opti-
mization can reduce the amount of transmitted
data but increase encoding time; transmitting
model or representation parameters requires new
entropy models and numerical formats; and ran-
dom access, hardware acceleration, and determin-
istic decoding may differ substantially from those
of conventional VAE-based codecs. Their practi-
cal value should therefore be assessed using the
same deployment criteria emphasized throughout
this survey rather than compression performance
alone.
5.2.3 Foundation-Model-Assisted
Compression
Foundation models provide another possible shift
in the coding paradigm. Large vision and multi-
modal models contain powerful priors for visual
structure and semantics, while continuing progress
in model compression and edge inference is making
their deployment increasingly feasible (Y. Zheng
et al., 2025). Instead of explicitly transmitting
every visual detail, a codec may transmit compact
structural, semantic, or latent information and
rely on a shared generative prior to reconstruct
plausible content.
Recent studies already demonstrate the poten-
tial of this direction. Large models can provide
strong probability models for lossless compression
across different data modalities (Z. Li, Huang,
et al., 2025), while generative image codecs can
reconstruct visually realistic images at very low
rates (Careil, Muckley, Verbeek, & Lathuili`ere,
2024; Ke et al., 2025). Such approaches may be
particularly attractive in extremely bandwidth-
constrained scenarios, where preserving all pixel-
level information is infeasible.
33


The resulting coding objective, however, dif-
fers fundamentally from conventional fidelity-
oriented compression. A generative reconstruction
can be perceptually convincing while differing
semantically from the original signal. Halluci-
nation, semantic consistency, controllability, and
uncertainty therefore become codec-level concerns
rather than purely generative-model concerns. In
applications such as archival storage, medical
imaging, remote sensing, or machine decision-
making, plausible reconstruction cannot automat-
ically be treated as correct reconstruction.
Foundation-model-assisted
compression
will
consequently require evaluation beyond conven-
tional R–D and perceptual quality. Future proto-
cols may need to characterize semantic fidelity and
reconstruction reliability together with bitrate
and computational cost. Efficient deployment is
equally important: a reduction in transmitted bits
is of limited practical benefit if reconstruction
requires an excessively large model or substantial
inference latency.
5.2.4 From Learned Image Coding to
Deployable Video Coding
Many principles reviewed in this article naturally
extend from image to video coding, but their
system implications become substantially more
difficult. Video introduces temporal prediction,
reference management, buffer constraints, random
access, and continuous real-time processing. Con-
sequently, numerical inconsistency or reconstruc-
tion error is no longer confined to a single coding
instance but may propagate through subsequent
frames.
Recent work has demonstrated increasingly
practical neural video codecs, including real-time
mobile implementations (Van Rozendaal et al.,
2024) and initial studies of cross-platform deter-
ministic execution (Concei¸c˜ao et al., 2025). Never-
theless, practical learned video coding must simul-
taneously satisfy substantially tighter constraints
on latency, memory bandwidth, energy consump-
tion, and temporal consistency than still-image
coding.
Rate adaptation also becomes a sequential sys-
tem problem. Network conditions and frame-level
coding decisions interact over time, connecting
video compression with adaptive streaming and
communication control (H. Chen, Xu, Wang, Cao,
& Ma, 2024; Y. Li, Zhang, Chen, & Ma, 2023; Xu,
Chen, & Ma, 2023). In addition, decoder complex-
ity and reference dependencies limit the degree
to which high-capacity image-coding architectures
can be transferred directly to real-time video.
For these reasons, still-image LIC is likely
to remain an important testbed for deployment
techniques before they become mature in learned
video coding. Advances in efficient neural accel-
erators, deterministic low-precision computation,
lightweight temporal modeling, error control, and
standardization will be central to closing this gap
(Gao, 2025a).
5.3 Closing Perspective
Learned image coding is moving from optimiz-
ing a neural compression model toward design-
ing a complete visual communication system. In
the near term, the most important challenge is
to make compression gains reproducible, con-
trollable, efficient, and interoperable under real
deployment constraints. In the longer term, the
representation itself may evolve as coding becomes
increasingly integrated with machine perception,
neural scene representations, and generative foun-
dation models.
These two directions should not be viewed
independently. New representations and more
powerful priors will only become useful coding
technologies if their computational cost, numeri-
cal behavior, robustness, and interoperability can
also be controlled. Conversely, the deployment
mechanisms developed for current LIC provide an
important foundation for future AI-native visual
coding systems. The next generation of learned
codecs will therefore be shaped not by R–D per-
formance alone, but by the joint optimization
of compression efficiency, practical deployability,
and intelligence compatibility.
