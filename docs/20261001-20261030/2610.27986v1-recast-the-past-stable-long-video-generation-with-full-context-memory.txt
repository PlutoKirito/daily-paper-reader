Recast the Past: Stable Long Video
Generation with Full Context Memory
Yukuo Ma1,2, Cong Liu2, Junke Wang1, Junqi Liu2, Haibin Huang2, Zuxuan Wu†1,
Xuelong Li†2
1Fudan University, 2Institute of Artificial Intelligence (TeleAI), China Telecom
†Corresponding authors.
Long-horizon video generation requires retaining an ever-expanding history, but conditioning on its
growing token sequence is costly. Existing methods restrict or compress historical context, potentially
losing information needed for future generation. This work proposes ReCam, which groups local
spatiotemporal blocks of historical latents into vectors and projects them directly into the high-
dimensional hidden space of a Diffusion Transformer. The model learns to use the resulting memory
tokens through native self-attention, reducing token count while retaining block-level access to the
full history. To mitigate temporal drift during autoregressive rollout, we further introduce dual-
pointer denoising with separate noise schedules for recent context and target frames. Among the
evaluated methods, ReCam achieves the highest VBench-Long overall score of 80.1 and competitive
static-scene revisitation performance on R2M-Bench. It also leads on all four consistency metrics
for off-screen subject reappearance, including clothing consistency of 4.09 versus 3.03 for the best
baseline.
Keywords: Long video generation; video memory; diffusion transformers; temporal consistency

Introduction
Advances in autoregressive video models have enabled streaming and interactive generation for applications
such as world modeling [Parker-Holder et al., 2025, Sun et al., 2025, Team et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026c,
Gao et al., 2026a], embodied intelligence [Ye et al., 2026, Kim et al., 2026, Li et al., 2026a], and creative
content generation [Gao et al., 2025, Wan et al., 2025, Teng et al., 2025].
These applications shift the
focus from generating locally plausible short clips to maintaining long-term memory over extended temporal
horizons (e.g., world models must maintain consistent scene states across interactions, whereas embodied
agents must retain information from earlier observations to support subsequent decisions).
Given that relevant information may vary across temporal positions and denoising stages [Yu et al.,
2026a], effective long-term memory requires access to such evidence throughout the accumulated history.
However, naively retaining the complete history incurs substantial computational and memory costs for
videos ranging from ten seconds to one minute, which is a practical range for these applications. Existing
methods can be broadly categorized along two design dimensions (Fig. 1): how historical information is
retrieved and how it is preserved. Selecting historical frames or sparsifying attention reduces conditioning
cost by restricting the observations or interactions available to the generator [Yu et al., 2025, Cai et al., 2026,
Jia et al., 2026]. Yet, the resulting selection may not fully capture the historical evidence needed, leaving
relevant cues inaccessible even when they remain in the history. Other methods encode history into compact
embeddings or mediate access through a limited set of query tokens [Zhang et al., 2025, Yu et al., 2026a].
These designs can retain full-history coverage, yet may discard fine-grained details needed when a scene is
revisited or a subject reappears.
These limitations motivate a method that allows video models to leverage the full history during autore-
gressive rollout directly. Our key insight is that a coarse read does not require a coarse historical representa-
tion. We reduce the historical token count by retrieving at a coarser granularity while retaining full-history

We compare our method with other representative methods.
Keyframe-based approaches, such as CaM [Yu et al., 2025], typically employ an external rule-based selector to retrieve
a subset of frames as memory. In contrast, compression-based methods [Zhang et al., 2025] encode history into a
compact representation through a separate reconstruction stage. Different from these approaches, ReCam allows the
video model itself to learn how to represent history and how to retrieve it via its native attention mechanism.
coverage. Each local spatiotemporal block of historical latents is flattened into a vector and projected into
the high-dimensional hidden space of a Diffusion Transformer. The resulting memory representations are
learned jointly with the video model and processed through native self-attention. This allows local detail to
be encoded within each memory token, giving the model access to every historical block through a shorter
token sequence.
Nevertheless, effective use of history requires more than access: errors in generated frames can propagate
to subsequent predictions [Ning et al., 2024, Zhang and Agrawala, 2025, Ma et al., 2026]. To mitigate this
drift, we introduce dual-pointer denoising, which assigns separate noise schedules to recent context frames
and current target frames. At inference, context frames start from pure noise and follow a faster denoising
trajectory, providing temporal cues while reducing the influence of errors in previously generated context.
Building on these designs, we propose ReCam, which enables stable and consistent long-video generation.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• Full-context memory. We introduce ReCam, a simple yet effective method that allows each token
to encode a larger amount of spatiotemporal content, enabling end-to-end training for long-video
generation and yielding a native long-term memory mechanism.
• Dual-pointer denoising. We assign separate denoising trajectories to recent context frames and current
target frames, providing temporal cues while masking prediction errors in the context frames.
• Empirical evaluation.
Extensive experiments on complementary benchmarks show that ReCam
achieves the highest overall VBench-Long score among the evaluated methods [Huang et al., 2024],
competitive performance on static-scene revisitation [Gu et al., 2026], and the best results across all
four metrics for off-screen subject reappearance [Chen et al., 2026a, Shi et al., 2026]. These results
support the effectiveness of ReCam as a native long-term memory mechanism.

Method
ReCam combines a learned representation of accumulated video history with dual-pointer denoising of recent
context. We first describe how historical latents are turned into memory tokens and accessed through native
self-attention, then explain how recent context is incorporated during training and autoregressive rollout.

2.1
A Coarse Read on Fine-Grained History
We reduce the number of historical tokens by grouping local spatiotemporal content into larger read units.
Each unit is represented in the high-dimensional hidden space of the DiT, allowing the model to learn how
to encode and use local detail while retaining access to the full history.

The immediately preceding segment is encoded into latent space as historical context. We progressively
increase the maximum history length from 10 to 30 seconds. The first frame remains a clean anchor, while
the remaining historical latents are converted into memory tokens and fed directly into the DiT, bypassing
native patchification. During training, the first latent frame of the current chunk is reserved as short-term
context for dual-pointer denoising. At inference, the last latent frame of the previous chunk serves this role,
creating a one-latent-frame overlap between consecutive chunks.

Optional camera control.
For camera-controlled tasks such as scene revisitation, we follow Yu et al.
[2026b] and inject camera trajectories through fused-PRoPE. We apply PRoPE [Li et al., 2026b] after the
original DiT RoPE and before self-attention, without adding learnable parameters. During training and
inference, only the trajectory of the current generation window is provided, expressed as relative poses with
respect to its first frame. Historical consistency must therefore be learned without an explicit geometry-based
memory. Please see Appendix A for more details.
3
Experiments
3.1
Experimental Setup
We initialize ReCam from the high-noise expert of Wan2.2-I2V-A14B and generate 480p videos at 16 fps,
adding 40 pixel frames per autoregressive step. Training uses a global batch size of 64 and three stages of
10,000 steps, with maximum history lengths of 10, 20, and 30 seconds. We use AdamW with learning rates
of 10−5, 10−6, and 10−6 across the three stages. Default inference uses 40 Euler steps and classifier-free
guidance.
Training data.
We train on an internal corpus of 2 million general-purpose videos lasting 5–60 seconds,
mostly sourced from the web and covering diverse real-world scenarios. No manually constructed memory
labels, such as revisit segments identified by FOV overlap, are used. For static-scene revisitation only, we
additionally train on SpatialVID-HQ [Wang et al., 2026a] to acquire camera control. Appendix B provides
further details.
Evaluation settings.
We evaluate overall long-video quality and two forms of historical recall.
The
protocols and metric definitions are detailed in Appendix C.
(1) Long-horizon generation. The official VBench-Long I2V suite [Huang et al., 2024] evaluates 30-second
videos at 480p across six dimensions of consistency, motion, and visual quality.
(2) Static-scene revisitation.
We use 100 reference scenes from R2M-Bench [Gu et al., 2026] to test
whether previously observed content is recovered when the camera returns. MemoryGain (MG) and the
Normalized Memory Ratio (NMR) calibrate revisit consistency against controls from the same rollout across
appearance fidelity, scene identity, and object identity.
(3) Off-screen subject reappearance. Following the MSVBench protocol [Shi et al., 2026], we evaluate 64
histories covering 40 subjects and 10 backgrounds. Each history ends after the subject leaves the frame, and
generation must recover that subject upon reappearance. In half of the histories, the subject first appears
after the initial frame; in every history, it is absent for the final five seconds. We measure face, character,
background, and clothes consistency against the reference history.

3.2
Comparison with Existing Methods
Long-horizon generation.
All methods generate 30-second videos at 480p under the VBench-Long I2V
protocol, except Helios, which uses its training resolution of 368p because performance degrades at other
resolutions. In Tab. 1, ReCam achieves the highest overall score of 80.1 among the evaluated methods,
supporting its effectiveness across visual quality and long-term consistency.
Static-scene revisitation.
Table 2 compares ReCam with raw historical KV-cache models (LingBot-
World-Fast and LingBot-World-2-Fast [Gao et al., 2026a,b]), FOV-based retrieval (HY-WorldPlay [Sun et al.,
2025]), and recurrent state-space memory (Sana-WM [Zhu et al., 2026]) under the same R2M-Bench protocol.
Without an explicit geometry-based memory or manually constructed revisit supervision, ReCam remains
competitive: it achieves the highest MG on MVPR-sim (0.212), ties for the highest MG on Sem.Persist
(0.026), and obtains the highest NMR on SSIM (0.493).
The remaining gap is most visible against HY-WorldPlay on NMR. We observe minor viewpoint and
position shifts, motion instability, and occasional degradation on second or third revisits. Limited camera-
control and revisit data may contribute to these failures. The results show that learned historical memory
transfers to scene revisitation, while leaving room for improvement in camera accuracy and repeated-return
consistency.
Off-screen subject reappearance.
We compare ReCam with six baselines using identical histories and
reappearance targets (Tab. 3). FramePack supports only 19 context latents, corresponding to less than five
seconds, so we use its official FramePack-F1 continuation strategy, which retains the first frame and a sliding
window of recent frames.

ReCam leads on all four metrics over the full evaluation set, including clothes consistency of 4.09 versus

3.3
Ablation Studies
We examine history efficiency, granularity, and context denoising. Unless otherwise specified, variants use
the same resolution of 640 × 368, a maximum history of 20 seconds, and 10K training steps. Evaluation uses
fixed subsets of VBench and the off-screen subject reappearance benchmark.
Efficiency with longer history.
We measure GPU memory usage and single-forward-pass time across
history lengths on one H100 GPU with FlashAttention-3 [Shah et al., 2024]. In Fig. 4(a), a one-minute
history requires memory comparable to the standard 480p, five-second short-video configuration and has
a shorter forward time. This measured scaling supports end-to-end training and inference with extended
historical context.
History granularity.
Table 4(a) varies the history block shape and MLP bottleneck dimension. At the
same nominal history-token budget, (16, 4, 8, 8) and (16, 16, 4, 4) produce similar overall VBench scores, with
minor differences in memory metrics. Reducing the MLP bottleneck to 128 dimensions lowers VBench and
character consistency, supporting the value of sufficient projection capacity. Finer spatial grouping, from
(16, 2, 8, 8) to (16, 2, 4, 4), improves face consistency when the subject is absent from the first frame. However,
it requires approximately four times as many history tokens and does not improve the overall VBench score.
We use (16, 2, 8, 8) as the default balance between efficiency and generation quality.
Clean context, single pointer, or dual pointer?
We compare clean context, single-pointer denoising
(adding noise to context tokens), and dual-pointer denoising using changes in VBench metrics between
the first and last two-second windows. Dual-pointer denoising performs best on all four reported metrics
(Tab. 4(b)). Clean context also maintains stable subject and background consistency, suggesting that native
long-video training already helps limit error accumulation.
The training curves in Fig. 4(b) show that dual-pointer denoising and clean context reach similar final
losses, both below single-pointer denoising. This is consistent with the paired-view shortcut making noisy
context easier to use during optimization.
Figure 5 illustrates the difference during rollout.
Both variants exhibit color drift, but small shifts
accumulate under clean context until the scene becomes excessively dark. With dual-pointer denoising, the
drift is less pronounced in the final chunk, illustrating reduced error propagation across chunks.
4
Related Work
Autoregressive video generation and temporal drift.
Autoregressive video models support stream-
ing [Yuan et al., 2026], generative transmission [Shao and Li, 2025, An et al., 2026], and interactive applica-
tions [Yang et al., 2025, Chen et al., 2026b], but conditioning on generated history can propagate prediction
errors. Prior work addresses this through more robust context conditioning, error-aware training, and plan-
ning. Noise perturbation uses scheduled noise levels for historical and newly generated frames to improve
tolerance to imperfect context [Chen et al., 2024, Song et al., 2025]. Error-aware training instead simulates
erroneous histories [Yuan et al., 2026] or feeds model predictions back as context [Huang et al., 2025, Liu
et al., 2025].
Planning methods generate farther-ahead frames or organize the rollout globally to limit the accumulation
of local errors [Ma et al., 2026, Zhang and Agrawala, 2025]. Recent error-blocking work uses the same noise
strength for historical and newly generated frames to restrict error propagation [Zou et al., 2026].
Our
dual-pointer design uses separate context and target noise schedules, together with paired context views
that expose the denoising direction. The prediction objective remains focused on the current chunk.
Historical access and memory representation.
Long-term memory must balance the cost of reading
history with the information retained for future generation. Keyframe- and view-based methods retrieve
frame-level observations using temporal or spatial cues, including FOV overlap, camera pose, and surfel
associations [Yu et al., 2025, Li et al., 2025, Xiao et al., 2025]. Explicit 3D memories, such as point clouds
9


and depth-guided latent caches, preserve scene structure across viewpoints [Zhao et al., 2025, Wang et al.,
2026b]. These approaches organize historical access around selected observations or geometric structure.
Other methods reduce the memory footprint through packed frame contexts, compact history embeddings,
recurrent states, or learned query interfaces [Zhang and Agrawala, 2025, Zhang et al., 2025, Po et al., 2025,
Peng et al., 2026, Yu et al., 2026a]. Sparse attention reduces reading cost through chunk routing or token
grouping [Cai et al., 2026, Jia et al., 2026]. ReCam packs local spatiotemporal blocks into high-dimensional
tokens and exposes all blocks to native self-attention. The block representations are learned jointly with
video generation, combining full-history coverage with a shorter conditioning sequence.
5
Conclusion
We introduced ReCam, which learns long-term video memory by packing local spatiotemporal history into
high-dimensional tokens processed through native self-attention. This design reduces historical token count
while preserving full-history coverage, allowing the video model to jointly learn how historical information is
represented and used. Dual-pointer denoising complements this memory mechanism by assigning separate
noise schedules to recent context and target frames to mitigate error propagation during autoregressive
rollout. ReCam achieves the highest overall VBench-Long score of 80.1 among the evaluated methods and
leads on all four consistency metrics for off-screen subject reappearance, while remaining competitive on scene
revisitation. These results demonstrate that effective long-term memory can be learned from general-purpose
video data.
Reliable memory use in out-of-domain settings remains an open challenge. Future work will explore more
demanding revisitation scenarios, diverse downstream applications, and learning from in-context demonstra-
tions.

A
Camera Control
For the static-scene revisitation evaluation, we adapt ReCam using 36k video clips from SpatialVID-HQ
[Wang et al., 2026a], paired with text captions and camera poses.
Pose preprocessing.
SpatialVID-HQ provides sparse camera poses as quaternions and translations in
world-to-camera coordinates. We convert them to camera-to-world coordinates, interpolate the poses to the
sampled frames, and associate one camera pose with each temporal latent using the last source frame in the
corresponding temporal group (with a VAE temporal stride of 4, we sample every 4th frame). During both
training and inference, camera-to-world poses are expressed relative to the first frame of the current chunk.
For a window of F temporal latents, the camera input is represented as an F × 12 array containing the first
three rows of each relative camera-to-world matrix in row-major order.

Training Data
General-purpose video corpus.
We train ReCam on an internal corpus of 2 million distinct videos,
totaling approximately 8000 hours. The corpus consists primarily of web-sourced footage from commercial
stock-video collections (e.g., iStock), supplemented by other supplied datasets, public stock-video websites
(e.g., Pexels and Mixkit), and publicly available video datasets (e.g., Koala-36M [Wang et al., 2025]). Videos
have a median duration of 13.15 seconds, and nearly all last between 5 and 60 seconds. Fig. 6(a) summa-
rizes the duration distribution. We further provide a qualitative overview of the training video content by
visualizing the distribution of caption keywords as a word cloud in Fig. 6(b).
Data preparation and sampling.
We filter the source videos using several quality signals, including
aesthetics, motion magnitude, sharpness, image quality, and text overlays. The videos are resampled to
16 fps, resized while preserving their aspect ratio, and center-cropped to 832 × 448 pixels. We then use
Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B [Bai et al., 2025] to generate dense captions for temporal segments of five seconds each.

lies within the selected caption segment, while the visual history is drawn from the immediately preceding
portion of the same video. Although this history may span multiple earlier caption segments, only the caption

Evaluation Details
For VBench-Long, we use the official evaluation protocol without modification [Huang et al., 2024]. Below,
we detail the protocols for static-scene revisitation and off-screen subject reappearance.
C.1
Static-Scene Revisitation
Inputs and camera trajectory.
We use the 100 reference images and their accompanying text prompts
from R2M-Bench [Gu et al., 2026]. In our experiments, we found that the original trajectories in R2M-
Bench are overly complex for our method and some baselines (e.g., Sana-WM and LingBot-World-Fast),
sometimes leading to degraded generation quality and camera trajectory adherence.
To fairly evaluate
memory capability, we construct a 30-second trajectory that rotates left by 90◦over the first 7.5 seconds,
right by 180◦over the next 15 seconds, and left by 90◦over the final 7.5 seconds, finally returning to the
initial orientation. Before scoring, videos from all methods are resized while preserving their aspect ratio and
center-cropped to 832 × 480 pixels, with their frame counts unchanged. This common evaluation resolution
allows pixel-level metrics to be computed under the same spatial preprocessing.
Frame-pair construction.
We evaluate three sets of frame pairs within each generated video. Revisit
pairs connect temporally separated frames with matching camera orientations and positions. Gap-matched
frame pairs are sampled to match the temporal separations of revisit pairs and serve as a baseline for
measuring frame similarity at comparable temporal gaps. Short-range pairs measure local consistency, with
temporal gaps between 2 and max(6, 0.02T) frames for a video of T frames. Following the original setting,
we randomly sample 100 pairs per set.
16


Evaluation metrics.
We report eight metrics from R2M-Bench: PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, DINO similarity,
BoQ similarity, MVPR similarity, object appearance persistence, and object semantic persistence.
The
first six metrics compare frames at the image level. The last two metrics characterize object persistence:
object appearance persistence measures the visual similarity between matched objects, while object semantic
persistence measures how strongly the matched detections support the semantic concepts of the anchor
objects.
For each frame pair, we detect anchor objects in the earlier frame using GroundingDINO [Liu et al., 2024]
with a fixed vocabulary of object concepts, using box and text thresholds of 0.25 and 0.20, respectively, and
a non-maximum suppression threshold of 0.6. We retain at most 20 anchors and discard objects whose
area is less than 0.2% of the image.
SAM2 provides a mask for each object, from which we extract a
DINOv2 [Oquab et al., 2023] descriptor. In the later frame, we independently detect up to 5 candidates for
each anchor concept and compute their masks and descriptors.

The history for evaluation.
We use 50 sampling steps at a native resolution of 960 × 544 and resize the
outputs to 1280 × 720. We generate 15-second videos to serve as historical context for the baselines. We
present two prompt templates: one for cases where the subject appears in the first frame and one for cases
where the subject does not appear in the first frame (Fig. 7).
Note that we supply the same source histories to all methods, but the amount of history accessible
through their continuation interfaces differs. Specifically, ReCam and the two LingBot-World variants access
the full 15-second history. LongLive 2.0 retains the first 29 pixel frames and the last 96 pixel frames, while
FramePack-F1 and Helios retain the initial frame and the most recent 73 pixel frames.
Evaluation metrics.
We compare the generated continuation with the reference history using four metrics
from MSVBench [Shi et al., 2026]. We first apply GroundingDINO [Liu et al., 2024] and SAM [Kirillov et al.,
2023] to detect people in each frame and group consecutive detections into intervals of subject visibility.
For face and character consistency, we select one interval of subject visibility from the reference history
and one from the generated continuation, then uniformly sample 10 frames from each interval. We compare
every sampled history frame with every sampled continuation frame, yielding 10 × 10 = 100 pairs without
temporal alignment.

We observe that small
context noise shifts (0 and 0.1) expose a cleaner context too early, which introduces prediction errors into
more denoising steps and degrades the temporal drift metric. This is consistent with our design hypothesis.
As the context noise shift increases, the generated videos become increasingly static, as reflected by a lower
dynamic degree (from 47.73 to 42.33). This finding echoes Zou et al. [2026]: a noisier history may make it
harder for the model to determine the motion structure of newly generated frames in early denoising steps,
leading to static videos. A moderate shift, such as 1 or 2, achieves the best trade-off between these two
extremes, yielding the highest VBench scores and the most favorable temporal drift metrics. We therefore
adopt sc = 2 as the default setting in our experiments.
