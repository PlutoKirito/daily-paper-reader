ACWM-RM: A REFERENCE-FREE REWARD MODEL
FROM COMPLEMENTARY MISMATCH PREFERENCES
Shibo Hong1, Siyu Ren2, FengJiao Chen2, Zhongyuan Peng1, Wei Shi2, Yixin Cao1
1College of Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence, Fudan University 2Meituan
ABSTRACT
Reinforcement learning has gained attention for improving action-conditioned
world models (ACWMs), as it enables reward-driven optimization of visual qual-
ity (VQ) and action-following fidelity (AF) beyond flow-matching optimization.
Realizing this potential requires reward signals that reliably rank videos by both
VQ and AF. However, existing rewards capture only one dimension: metric-based
rewards assess VQ but require ground-truth videos and do not directly assess AF,
while inverse dynamics model (IDM) rewards assess AF without references but
do not guarantee VQ. Moreover, IDMs, typically trained on real videos, may
provide unreliable rewards for generated videos containing visual artifacts and
physical inconsistencies. In this work, we introduce the Action and Video Mis-
match Benchmark (AVM-Eval), comprising 67.2k pairs that evaluate how reli-
ably IDMs assess AF on real and generated videos. We then propose ACWM-
RM, a reward model that jointly assesses VQ and AF without ground-truth videos
during reward computation. To train it, we first construct the Action and Video
Mismatch Preference Dataset (AVM-Pref) with 506.3k pairs providing comple-
mentary supervision: action-mismatch pairs supervise AF, while video-mismatch
pairs supervise VQ and expose the model to generated videos. We then pretrain
an action-conditioned predictor on real videos to learn forward dynamics before
jointly training it with a patch-weighted score head on AVM-Pref to transfer these
dynamics to AF assessment on generated videos. Evaluation on AVM-Eval re-
veals the limited reliability of IDMs on generated videos, while downstream RL
experiments show that ACWM-RM outperforms reward baselines in both criteria.

INTRODUCTION
Action-conditioned world models (ACWMs) (Yang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025;
Liao et al., 2025) predict videos conditioned on robot actions, supporting embodied policy evaluation
and optimization. For these applications, generated videos must preserve visual quality while faith-
fully reflecting the commanded actions. However, standard flow-matching training (Lipman et al.,
2022) does not explicitly optimize these criteria. This motivates reinforcement learning (RL) such
as Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2026a; Wu et al., 2026a), which optimizes these criteria using rewards.
The effectiveness of RL, however, depends on reward signals that reliably rank videos generated
under the same conditioning input, assigning higher scores to those with better visual quality (VQ)
and action-following fidelity (AF). Existing reward signals fall into two main categories. First,
full-reference rewards (Wu et al., 2026b) use metrics such as PSNR, SSIM, or LPIPS to compare
generated videos with the corresponding ground-truth videos, providing VQ supervision but re-
quiring reference videos and not directly assessing AF. Second, inverse dynamics model (IDM)
rewards (Seo et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a) assess AF without requiring reference videos by com-
paring actions inferred from generated videos with the input actions used to generate those videos,
but do not directly assess VQ. Moreover, IDMs are typically trained on real-world videos, where
motion and interactions obey physical laws, but are applied during RL to ACWM-generated videos
that may contain visual artifacts and physical inconsistencies, such as robot-arm deformation and
interpenetration. To quantify this issue, we generate 100 videos with each of four ACWMs un-
der identical inputs. As shown in Figure 1, ACWM-generated videos exhibit more visual artifacts
and physical inconsistencies than real-world videos. This gap raises the question of whether strong
action-mismatch discrimination on real videos translates to reliable assessment of generated videos.

In this work, we first construct the Action and Video Mismatch Benchmark (AVM-Eval), comprising
67.2k pairs. It evaluates how reliably IDMs assess AF on real videos using action-mismatch pairs
and on generated videos using video-mismatch pairs. We then propose a reward model (ACWM-
RM) for jointly assessing VQ and AF without ground-truth videos at reward computation time. To
train ACWM-RM, we construct the Action and Video Mismatch Preference Dataset (AVM-Pref)
with 506.3k pairs of two types that provide complementary supervision: Action-mismatch pairs
contrast original and perturbed actions for the same real video, providing AF supervision; Video-
mismatch pairs contrast different generated videos under the same actions, providing VQ supervi-
sion while exposing the model to the generated-video distribution. For training strategy, we first
pretrain an action-conditioned predictor on features extracted from real robot videos by a frozen
V-JEPA 2.1 encoder (Mur-Labadia et al., 2026), establishing a dynamics representation for AF
evaluation. We then jointly optimize the predictor and patch-weighted score head on AVM-Pref,
transferring the dynamics learned from real-world videos to AF assessment of generated videos.
Our evaluation of four architecturally distinct IDMs on AVM-Eval shows that accurate AF assess-
ment on real videos does not ensure reliable AF evaluation of generated videos. The best-performing
IDM achieves 86.1% accuracy on action-mismatch pairs from real videos but only 70.0% on video-
mismatch pairs from generated videos. To evaluate ACWM-RM’s downstream effectiveness as
an RL reward, we fine-tune DreamDojo-2B with Flow-GRPO, comparing ACWM-RM against the
best-performing IDM on AVM-Eval, a metric-based reward, and a VLM-based reward. On held-out
tasks, ACWM-RM yields the best VQ and AF, while maintaining VLM-assessed physical consis-
tency (PC) scores close to the original model before RL fine-tuning.
Our main contributions: (1) We introduce AVM-Eval and show that, across four IDMs, accurate AF
assessment on real videos does not ensure reliable AF evaluation of generated videos. (2) We de-
velop AVM-Pref and ACWM-RM, combining dynamics pretraining with complementary mismatch
preferences to assess both criteria without references during reward computation. (3) We demon-
strate that ACWM-RM outperforms reward baselines in VQ and AF when used for RL fine-tuning.

RELATED WORK
2.1
ACTION-CONDITIONED WORLD MODELS
Action-conditioned world models generate videos conditioned on past observations and actions,
simulating the resulting robot interactions to support policy evaluation and planning.
To learn
transferable dynamics, DreamDojo (Gao et al., 2026) pretrains on human videos using continu-
ous latent actions and adapts to target robots with robot data. To condition predictions on actions,
iVideoGPT (Wu et al., 2024) models observations and actions as token sequences with an autoregres-
sive transformer, whereas IRASim (Zhu et al., 2025) strengthens action-frame alignment through
frame-level conditioning in a diffusion transformer. These predictive capabilities enable downstream
applications: Genie Envisioner (Liao et al., 2025) uses GE-Sim for action-conditioned simulation
and closed-loop policy evaluation. Such applications require generated videos to preserve both VQ
and AF, motivating our study of rewards that jointly assess these properties for ACWM post-training.
2.2
REWARD MODELING FOR WORLD MODELS
Reliable reward signals are essential for effective RL, as they determine which properties of videos
are optimized. For general video generation, VideoReward (Liu et al., 2026b) learns human pref-

World (Peng et al., 2026) and RoboAlign-R1 (Wu et al., 2026a) assess video quality and instruction

models, RLVR-World (Wu et al., 2026b) provides visual rewards through ground-truth video com-

METHOD
We first describe AVM-Eval construction (Sec. 4.1) and then our ACWM-RM, including AVM-Pref
construction, model architecture, and two-stage training (Sec. 4.2).
4.1
ACTION AND VIDEO MISMATCH BENCHMARK
Existing IDMs are normally trained and evaluated on real-world videos. However, IDM-based
rewards are applied to generated videos, whose distributions can differ from those of real-world
videos. Generated videos may contain visual artifacts and physical inconsistencies, including robot-
arm deformation and interpenetration, absent from real videos. To our knowledge, no existing
benchmark compares the reliability of IDM-based AF assessment on real and generated videos.
To address this gap, we construct AVM-Eval using a pairwise evaluation protocol (Lambert et al.,
2025). AVM-Eval compares the reliability of AF assessment on real and ACWM-generated videos,
using action-mismatch pairs and video-mismatch pairs, respectively. We next describe the source
data preparation and benchmark construction.
4.1.1
SOURCE DATA PREPARATION
We use AgiBotWorld (Bu et al., 2025), a large-scale bimanual robot dataset with real-world videos,
as one source dataset, and partition its data by task into three subsets: AgiA, AgiB, and AgiC.

4.1.2
AVM-EVAL CONSTRUCTION
AVM-Eval comprises two complementary types of pairs: A video-mismatch pair holds the action
chunk fixed and contrasts two videos, whereas an action-mismatch pair holds the video fixed and
contrasts two action chunks. Together, these two types constitute 67.2k pairs in AVM-Eval.
Action-mismatch pairs (53.9k). To evaluate IDM-based AF assessment on real videos, we con-
struct action-mismatch pairs. Each pair is represented as (I0, a+, a−, I), where a+ is the action
chunk recorded with I and a−is a perturbed counterpart. We generate a−using two types of pertur-
bations: Numerical Perturbations (N-P) and Semantic Perturbations (S-P). N-P probe sensitivity to
low-level numerical errors in end-effector translation and rotation magnitudes or directions, gripper
opening degree, and action ordering. S-P probe sensitivity to high-level semantic errors through po-
sition offsets during grasping or placement, incorrect gripper timing, and mid-motion drift or freeze.
We also perturb action chunks from Gen, RoboD, and RoboT to assess IDM generalization across
embodiments and scenes. Detailed perturbation procedures are provided in Appendix A.
Video-mismatch pairs (13.3k). To evaluate AF on generated videos, we considered constructing
action-mismatch pairs in the same way. However, ACWMs cannot guarantee that generated videos
faithfully follow the action chunk, so a perturbed action chunk may be more consistent with the gen-
erated video than the original action chunk, reversing the intended preference label. We therefore
construct video-mismatch pairs by fixing the action chunk and ranking generated videos using Worl-
dArena’s nDTW (Shang et al., 2026) for AF, with additional filtering to limit the influence of visual
quality (Appendix B). We use four ACWMs with varying AF performance: DreamDojo-2B (DD-
2B), DreamDojo-14B (DD-14B) (Gao et al., 2026), GE-Sim-2B (GE-2B), and OSCAR-2B (OS-
2B) (Wu & Gao, 2026). Under the same I0 and a, each ACWM independently samples six 12-frame
videos using the stochastic SDE sampler adopted by Flow-GRPO. Within each ACWM, we pair a
higher-nDTW video ˆI+ with a lower-nDTW video ˆI−to form an intra-model pair (I0, a,ˆI+,ˆI−).
We rank videos generated by any two ACWMs under the same input to construct cross-model pairs.

4.2
REWARD MODEL FOR ACTION-CONDITIONED WORLD MODELS
Existing rewards provide only partial supervision, with metric-based rewards also requiring ground-
truth videos. We therefore propose ACWM-RM, a reference-free reward model that jointly assesses
VQ and AF. We next describe the construction of its training dataset, termed AVM-Pref (Sec. 4.2.1),
followed by the architecture and the two-stage training strategy (Sec. 4.2.2).
4.2.1
AVM-PREF CONSTRUCTION
To train ACWM-RM with the Bradley-Terry loss, we construct the Action and Video Mismatch Pref-
erence Dataset (AVM-Pref) from Agitrain
A
, comprising 506.3k action-mismatch and video-mismatch
pairs that provide complementary supervision. Action-mismatch pairs (156.3k): We use the same
numerical and semantic action perturbations as AVM-Eval to provide AF supervision.
Video-
mismatch pairs (350k): We follow AVM-Eval to generate videos from four ACWMs and construct
intra-model and cross-model pairs, but use four visual quality metrics (global and local PSNR and
LPIPS) instead of nDTW to provide VQ supervision. Since action-mismatch pairs already provide
broader AF supervision than nDTW’s 2D trajectory alignment, including translation, rotation, and
gripper opening, we use video-mismatch pairs for complementary VQ supervision. Although action-
mismatch pairs use real videos and thus differ from the generated-video distribution, our empirical
results show that combining both types improves AF assessment on generated videos. Finally, to
reduce sampling bias, we aim for comparable pair counts across models and tasks for intra-model
pairs and across model combinations and tasks for cross-model pairs. Appendix C details how the
four metrics are used, while Appendix H provides AVM-Pref statistics.
4.2.2
REWARD MODEL ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING STRATEGY
ACWM-RM scores a candidate video conditioned on an action chunk and its associated state se-
quence, producing a scalar reward for VQ and AF. It consists of a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 encoder E, a
bimanual action-conditioned predictor Pϕ, and a patch-weighted score head hψ. Training proceeds
in two stages: dynamics pretraining optimizes Pϕ on real-world videos, while preference training
jointly optimizes Pϕ and hψ on AVM-Pref to adapt the learned dynamics for AF assessment.

frozen. Unlike the original single-arm setting, our predictor takes bimanual actions. For bimanual
inputs, we express actions and states in a coordinate system aligned with the head-camera axes,
normalize them, project them to the predictor’s hidden dimension, and add a learnable identity
embedding for each arm. At each time step t, tokens are ordered as

EXPERIMENTS
We first describe experimental setup (Sec. 5.1) and evaluate IDMs and ACWM-RM on AVM-Eval
(Sec. 5.2). We then compare Flow-GRPO reward signals (Sec. 5.3) and conduct ablations (Sec. 5.4).
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Baselines.
On AVM-Eval, we compare ACWM-RM with four architecturally distinct IDMs:
EVA-IDM (EVAI) (Wang et al., 2026), which uses a convolutional backbone; GR00T-IDM
(GRTI) (Bjorck et al., 2025), which uses a diffusion-based model; TAP-IDM (TAPI) (Shi et al.,
2026), a vision-language model equipped with an action expert; and J-IDM (JI) (Li et al., 2026),
which recovers actions by inverting a predicted per-pixel Jacobian field. This evaluates how reliably
IDMs with different architectures transfer from real-video training to generated-video evaluation.
For Flow-GRPO training of ACWMs, we compare ACWM-RM with IDM-based, metric-based,
and VLM-based rewards. The IDM-based baseline uses the best-performing IDM on AVM-Eval.
The metric-based baseline combines the four visual quality metrics used in AVM-Pref following
the fusion procedure of RLVR-World (Wu et al., 2026b). We further explore whether VLM-as-a-
Judge (Hong et al., 2025) can provide effective rewards for ACWM optimization. Specifically, we
use Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct to score videos using embodiment-specific prompts and criteria
from RBench. Prompts and criteria definitions are provided in the Appendix F.
Training setup. We train the four IDMs on Agitrain
A
using their official pretrained backbones and
implementations, following the original training paradigm of predicting actions from real-world
videos. Each IDM and ACWM-RM has a nominal budget of approximately 7.12M 13-frame video
draws, including both ACWM-RM training stages. For Flow-GRPO, we fine-tune DreamDojo-2B
on AgiB and evaluate it on AgiC. Implementation details for the IDMs, ACWM-RM, and Flow-
GRPO are provided in Appendices E, D, and G, respectively.

CONCLUSION, LIMITATIONS, AND FUTURE WORK
Through AVM-Eval, we showed that strong action-mismatch discrimination on real videos does not
ensure reliable AF assessment of generated videos. To address this limitation while also assess-
ing VQ, we introduced ACWM-RM, a reward model requiring no ground-truth videos for reward
computation. ACWM-RM outperforms IDMs on generated-video AF and, when used to fine-tune
DreamDojo-2B, yields better VQ and AF than alternative reward signals.
Limitations and future work. ACWM-RM is trained to assess VQ and AF, but does not explic-
itly supervise physical consistency (PC). Although RL fine-tuning with ACWM-RM maintains PC
scores close to those of the pre-RL model, the reward does not directly target physical violations
such as interpenetration. Moreover, downstream RL improvements have so far been demonstrated
only for DreamDojo-2B on AgiBotWorld. Future work will incorporate physical-consistency super-
vision and evaluate ACWM-RM for RL optimization across a broader range of ACWMs.

A.2
NUMERICAL ACTION PERTURBATIONS
Numerical action perturbations modify a+ using operators drawn from a perturbation pool whose
membership is determined by the six activity flags. We first select the temporal windows within the
action chunk to be edited, then assemble the perturbation pool, then apply the drawn perturbations
channel by channel, and finally check whether the negative differs sufficiently from the positive.
Temporal Window Sampling.
Perturbing every action would make the negative wrong every-
where, which is both easy to detect and unrepresentative of the localized errors an ACWM actually
makes. We therefore confine each edit to a few windows of consecutive actions and leave the remain-
ing actions clean. In addition, many different combinations of windows and perturbation operators
remain available, so a single action chunk can yield many distinct negatives.

taken as a single window. Retaining these fully perturbed negatives keeps a coarse difficulty level
in the mix. Otherwise, we sample one to three windows, each covering one to seven consecutive
actions, and require neighboring windows to be separated by at least one unedited action so that
the edited segments stay distinguishable. If the sampled windows cannot all be placed within the
12 actions under this constraint, we drop windows until the remaining ones fit. In either case, the
outcome is a set of windows W = {[wlo

A.3
SEMANTIC ACTION PERTURBATIONS
Semantic action perturbations differ from numerical action perturbations in what they edit and where
the edits are applied. The semantic procedure never perturbs a+ directly, but edits the absolute state
sequence s and obtains a−by differencing the edited states through Eq. 7. The perturbation is
anchored at a grasp, release, or mid-motion event rather than at an individual action, so the error
corresponds to a semantic-level mistake. These events can be identified from the gripper events: a
gripper closing marks a grasp, a gripper opening marks a release, and the interval between a closing
and the following opening marks mid-motion drift or freeze. We therefore locate these events and
anchor the semantic perturbations to them.
Applying perturbation at a gripper event requires more context than a single clip provides. We
therefore subsample the episode to 8 fps, detect the events and apply the perturbation over this whole
subsampled episode, and crop the 13-frame clip afterward.

FLOW-GRPO OPTIMIZATION DETAILS
We fine-tune the policy using LoRA with rank 32 on 16 NVIDIA H800 GPUs. We optimize the
LoRA parameters with AdamW at a learning rate of 3 × 10−4 for 120 outer training iterations.
Each rollout uses 20 Flow-GRPO denoising steps and a diffusion noise level of 0.7. Each sampled
episode from AgiB (Table 13) supplies a shared input condition—the initial frame I0 and action
chunk a—for a group of G = 8 rollouts. In each outer iteration, we collect four rollout batches with
a per-GPU sampling batch size of 8 episodes and group size G, yielding 8 × 8 = 64 rollouts per
batch and 256 rollouts per GPU per outer iteration. We then perform four inner PPO-style policy-
optimization epochs on the collected data and accumulate gradients over 16 rollouts. The PPO-style
ratio clipping range is 10−3, the KL penalty coefficient is β = 0.004, and gradients are clipped to a
maximum norm of 1.0. We use seed 42 for policy optimization. ACWM-RM is frozen throughout
policy optimization and uses the conditioning state sequence integrated from the initial state and
each sampled action chunk.
For evaluation on AgiC, we generate videos from each optimized ACWM using five video-
generation seeds and report the mean and standard deviation across seeds for visual quality, action-
following fidelity, and physical consistency.
