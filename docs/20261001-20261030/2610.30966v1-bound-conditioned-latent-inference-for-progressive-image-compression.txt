Abstract
Progressive image compression reconstructs images from prefixes of a single bit-
stream and therefore involves sequential latent inference under partial observations.
Existing trit-plane codecs mainly treat decoded trits as discrete symbols for proba-
bility prediction, without fully exploiting their role as interval constraints on the
underlying continuous latents. We propose bound-conditioned latent inference
(BLI), a framework that reformulates progressive trit-plane coding as sequential
latent inference under shrinking interval constraints. At each coding step, the trits
decoded so far define lower and upper bounds for each latent element. BLI uses
the center and width of these bounds, together with hyperprior information shared
by the encoder and decoder, to jointly refine the Gaussian parameters (µ, σ) for
entropy modeling and the latent estimate ˆy for reconstruction. As additional trits
are decoded, the feasible intervals shrink monotonically, yielding tighter constraints
for the next inference step and enabling flexible in-plane refinement schedules.
Experiments on Kodak, CLIC, and JPEG-AI show that BLI achieves state-of-
the-art rate-distortion performance among progressive image codecs, reducing
BD-rate by 17.78% over DPICT and by 7.96% over CTC on Kodak, while replac-
ing CTC’s plane-specific predictors with a shared model that uses about 5.3× fewer
parameters and achieves lower latency than CTC.

Introduction
Progressive image compression differs from standard fixed-rate compression in that it allows the
decoder to reconstruct an image from prefixes of a single bitstream. At an intermediate decoding step,
only part of the latent representation has been transmitted, while the unresolved latent values must
be inferred from partial observations. Thus, progressive compression is not merely a rate-distortion
(R-D) optimization problem, but also a sequential latent inference problem under partial observations.
This property is essential for adaptive streaming, bandwidth-limited transmission, and storage systems
that require multiple quality levels from a single bitstream.
Traditional and learned codecs have achieved strong R-D performance, from hand-crafted transform
codecs such as JPEG and JPEG2000 (Wallace, 1992; Skodras et al., 2001) to neural codecs with
learned transforms, hyperpriors, and context models (Agustsson et al., 2017; Ballé et al., 2017, 2018;
Minnen et al., 2018; He et al., 2021). However, most high-performance learned codecs are optimized

Learned progressive codecs provide decodable bitstream prefixes by decomposing latent represen-
tations into successively refinable components. PLONQ (Lu et al., 2021) uses nested quantization
grids for coarse-to-fine refinement, while DPICT (Lee et al., 2022) discretizes each latent element
into trits that are transmitted sequentially. CTC (Jeon et al., 2023) further improves trit-plane coding
by introducing context models that refine ternary probabilities and partial reconstructions. However,
existing trit-plane codecs still primarily treat decoded trits as discrete symbols for probability pre-
diction and reconstruction update. In particular, CTC improves discrete trit prediction and partial
reconstruction, but it does not explicitly perform in-plane continuous latent inference conditioned on
the feasible intervals induced by previously decoded trits. The key observation behind our approach
is that decoded trits are not merely discrete symbols: in trit-plane coding, they also restrict each
underlying continuous latent element to a feasible interval, which can serve as an explicit constraint
for latent inference.
We present bound-conditioned latent inference (BLI), a framework that explicitly exploits these
feasible intervals by reformulating progressive trit-plane coding as sequential latent inference under
shrinking interval constraints. In BLI, each partially decoded latent element is modeled as a continu-
ous variable constrained by lower and upper bounds determined by the trits decoded so far. At each
coding step, BLI takes the center and width of the current feasible interval, together with hyperprior
information shared by the encoder and decoder, and jointly refines the Gaussian parameters (µ, σ)
for entropy modeling and the latent estimate ˆy for reconstruction. As additional trits are decoded,
the feasible intervals shrink monotonically, yielding tighter constraints for the next inference step.
As illustrated in Figure 1, this bound-conditioned formulation naturally supports flexible in-plane
transmission schedules: each trit-plane can be encoded at once or divided into finer substeps, allowing
the model to update the latent distribution and latent estimate more frequently. Conceptually, BLI
can be interpreted as constraint-consistent latent refinement, related to projection-based feasibility
methods such as POCS (Bauschke and Borwein, 1996) and to denoising or inpainting under interval
constraints. Unlike exact metric projection, however, BLI uses a learned amortized estimator trained
directly for entropy modeling and reconstruction quality.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We reformulate progressive trit-plane coding as sequential latent inference under shrinking
interval constraints, where decoded trits define feasible ranges for the underlying continuous
latent elements.

Related Work
Learned image compression: Traditional codecs (Wallace, 1992; Bellard, 2014; Bross et al., 2021)
follow the transform coding paradigm (Goyal, 2001), while learned codecs replace hand-crafted
transforms and entropy models with neural networks. Early learned codecs introduced differentiable
quantization and end-to-end R-D optimization (Agustsson et al., 2017; Ballé et al., 2017), and
hyperprior-based models improved entropy estimation by transmitting side information about latent
distributions (Ballé et al., 2018). Later methods further developed expressive priors, including
Gaussian mixture and asymmetric Gaussian models (Cheng et al., 2020; Cui et al., 2021).
Entropy models improve probability estimation by exploiting dependencies among latent elements.
Autoregressive context models (Minnen et al., 2018; Mentzer et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2019) improve
compression efficiency through sequential prediction, but incur high coding latency due to limited
parallelism. Partially parallel models (Minnen and Singh, 2020; He et al., 2021) reduce this cost by
exploiting latent correlations more efficiently.
Recent learned codecs incorporate attention mechanisms and transformers into analysis-synthesis
networks and entropy models. Transformer-based codecs (Zhu et al., 2022; Zou et al., 2022; Liu et al.,
2023; Li et al., 2024) improve global context modeling and yield compact latents, while transformer-
based entropy models (Qian et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2022) capture long-range dependencies for more
accurate probability estimation. These advances provide strong fixed-rate baselines, but most of them
are not designed to produce a single progressively decodable bitstream.
Scalable image compression: Beyond fixed-rate learned codecs, scalable compression aims to
support multiple bitrates using a single trained model or a single embedded bitstream. Variable-rate
methods adjust the operating rate by modulating internal representations, such as gain parameters,
channel width, or spatial transforms (Cui et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; Song et al., 2021), or by
using adjustable quantizers (Chen and Ma, 2020; Tong et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025). Although these
methods provide flexible rate control from a single model, each target bitrate generally requires a
separately encoded bitstream. Therefore, they differ from progressive codecs, in which every prefix
of a single bitstream remains decodable at multiple quality levels.
Progressive image compression uses embedded bitstreams, where reconstruction quality improves as
additional bits are appended. Classical codecs such as progressive JPEG (Wallace, 1992), JPEG2000
(Skodras et al., 2001), and SPIHT (Said and Pearlman, 1996) achieve this through spectral selection,
embedded bitstreams, or significance-driven coefficient ordering. In learned compression, PLONQ
(Lu et al., 2021) introduced nested quantization for coarse-to-fine refinement, while DPICT (Lee
et al., 2022) realized fine-grained scalability by slicing latents into trit-planes. CTC (Jeon et al.,
2023) further improved ternary probability modeling and partial reconstructions. However, DPICT
and CTC still primarily model decoded trits as discrete symbols. As a result, the feasible intervals
induced by decoded trits are not explicitly exploited for in-plane continuous latent inference, which
is the key limitation addressed by BLI.

3.2
BLI Codec Architecture
Unlike previous trit-plane codecs (Lee et al., 2022; Jeon et al., 2023) that reuse the initial Gaussian
parameters (M0, Σ0) throughout progressive decoding, BLI performs step-wise autoregressive
refinement. Each trit-plane is divided into multiple coding steps, and both the Gaussian parameters
(Mk, Σk) and the latent estimate ˆYk−1 are inferred within the current feasible bounds at step k.
Before entropy coding at step k, the BLI module operates within the feasible interval Ik−1 =
[Lk−1, Uk−1] determined by the trits decoded so far. As shown in Figure 4, the BLI module receives
the interval center Ck−1, the half-width Dk−1/2, and the hyperprior parameters (M0, Σ0) as inputs.
A stack of Swin attention blocks (Liu et al., 2021) extracts bound-conditioned features by modeling
spatial dependencies among partially decoded latent elements. These features are then processed
by CNN blocks and plane-wise MLP heads to estimate (Mk, Σk) and ˆYk−1. The plane-wise MLP
heads allow the module to adapt its prediction to each trit-plane level.
For the estimation of Mk and ˆYk−1, the CNN features are additionally concatenated with Ck−1 and
Dk−1/2, since the interval center provides a natural anchor and the half-width specifies the allowable
residual range. The outputs for Mk and ˆYk−1 are processed through a tanh activation and rescaled
by Dk−1/2 around Ck−1, ensuring consistency with the feasible interval Ik−1. As illustrated in
Figures 2 and 3, the estimates at step k are used to code the next subset of trits, after which the

3.3
Implementation
We embed the proposed BLI framework into FTIC (Li et al., 2024), a state-of-the-art learned image
codec. The original entropy model in FTIC performs channel-wise autoregressive prediction for
standard fixed-rate decoding. This design is not directly compatible with trit-plane coding, where
each latent element may be only partially decoded at intermediate steps. We therefore replace the
channel-wise autoregressive entropy model with a non-autoregressive hyperprior variant that directly
estimates the initial Gaussian parameters (M0, Σ0) from the hyper latent tensor Z.
Entropy coding is performed using the ANS algorithm (Duda, 2013). We train the codecs on the
Flickr2W dataset (Liu et al., 2020) using 256 × 256 patches, a batch size of 4, and the AdamW
optimizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019). Training is conducted on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU.
The BLI codec is optimized in two stages. First, we train the modified FTIC backbone at the highest
bitrate point. It is initialized from pretrained FTIC weights and optimized for 1.0M iterations with
a learning rate of 10−4 using the corresponding Lagrangian multiplier. This trained backbone is
used as the common basis for BLI and for the DPICT (Lee et al., 2022) and CTC (Jeon et al., 2023)
baselines, with the additional training scheme of Jeon et al. (2023) applied to CTC.

Comparative Assessment
R-D curve comparison: We compare BLI with representative image compression methods:
JPEG2000 (Skodras et al., 2001), BPG444 (Bellard, 2014), and VTM 12.0 (Bross et al., 2021) as
traditional codecs; STF (Zou et al., 2022), LIC-TCM (Liu et al., 2023), and FTIC (Li et al., 2024) as
fixed-rate learned codecs; and PLONQ (Lu et al., 2021), DPICT (Lee et al., 2022), and CTC (Jeon
et al., 2023) as progressive codecs. Since earlier progressive methods were built on CNN-based
baselines (Minnen et al., 2018; Cheng et al., 2020), we re-implement DPICT and CTC on the
stronger FTIC backbone for fair comparison, denoted as FTIC-DPICT and FTIC-CTC. Figure 5
shows the PSNR and MS-SSIM R-D curves on the Kodak dataset, with MS-SSIM converted to dB.
BLI consistently outperforms existing progressive codecs over a wide bitrate range, with particularly
strong gains at low-to-medium bitrates, where limited decoded information makes bound-conditioned
inference especially important for reducing reconstruction distortions. Additional R-D curves and
BD-rate results on the CLIC and JPEG-AI datasets are provided in Appendix A.
Compared with fixed-rate learned codecs, BLI remains competitive despite the additional constraint of
progressive decoding. In particular, BLI approaches the performance of strong fixed-rate methods and
surpasses STF over most of the MS-SSIM range. This suggests that bound-conditioned refinement
helps preserve structural and perceptual details. Note that STF, LIC-TCM, and FTIC are individually
optimized for each target rate, whereas BLI generates all R-D points from a single progressive codec.
Among progressive approaches, BLI achieves the best R-D performance over most of the bitrate
range. Its consistent advantage over FTIC-DPICT reflects the benefit of bound-conditioned inference.
The gains over FTIC-CTC arise from finer step-wise autoregressive modeling within each trit-plane,
which enables more effective use of spatial context and bound information for entropy modeling and
latent reconstruction.

4.3
Limitations and Future Work
Although BLI supports flexible transmission schedules within each trit-plane, our current evaluation
adopts the R-D-prioritized transmission order of Lee et al. (2022). Designing transmission orders
better matched to bound-conditioned inference remains an important direction for future work. A
performance gap also remains between BLI and recent fixed-rate codecs, as expected for progressive
codecs that reconstruct from bitstream prefixes. Closing this gap is another important direction for
future work.
5
Conclusions
We introduced bound-conditioned latent inference (BLI), a progressive image compression framework
that reformulates trit-plane coding as sequential latent inference under shrinking interval constraints.
By jointly refining the Gaussian parameters for entropy modeling and the latent estimates for
reconstruction within progressively updated bounds, BLI enables flexible in-plane autoregressive
refinement and more effective use of the information shared by the encoder and decoder at each
coding step. Experiments show that BLI achieves state-of-the-art R-D performance among progressive
codecs and preserves high-frequency details more faithfully, while replacing CTC’s plane-specific
predictors with a shared BLI module that uses fewer parameters and achieves lower latency. Overall,
BLI provides an effective framework for progressive learned image compression.

Training Loss
In (6), the endpoint indices are given by Kend = {4, 8, 12, 16, 20}, where l = 4 and l = 20 represent
the MST and the LST, respectively. The weight αl is adopted to account for the different quantization
intervals across plane levels. Specifically, because the quantization interval increases by a factor
of 3 from the LST to the MST, we set αl proportional to the inverse squared quantization interval.
As a result, the weights decrease by a factor of 1/9 from the LST to the MST, corresponding to

B.3
Transmission Schedule
In BLI, the trits transmitted within each trit-plane are divided into multiple coding steps. The step
endpoints are represented by Kend, which gives the cumulative coding-step indices at the endpoints
of successive trit-planes. During training, each trit-plane is uniformly divided into four steps, yielding
Kend = {4, 8, 12, 16, 20}. To expose BLI to diverse partial-observation patterns, we randomly assign
trits within each trit-plane to the four steps during the initial training stage. We then fine-tune the
codec using the same R-D-prioritized transmission order (Lee et al., 2022) as in evaluation.
During evaluation, we use a nonuniform full BLI schedule, Kend = {4, 8, 14, 30, 54}, corresponding
to step allocations of (4, 4, 6, 16, 24) from the MST to the LST. This schedule allocates more
refinement steps to trit-planes closer to the LST, since these planes typically contain more high-
entropy trits and benefit more from finer in-plane subdivision. Within each trit-plane, the assignment
of trits to coding steps follows the R-D-prioritized order. Specifically, for each latent element, we
estimate the expected bit increase ∆R and the distortion change ∆D using the predicted distribution
parameters available at the beginning of the plane. Trits are then ranked in descending order of
−∆D/∆R, so that those expected to yield larger distortion reduction per bit are transmitted earlier.

C.2
Sensitivity to Transmission Ordering
We further analyze the effect of the within-plane transmission ordering on the progressive R-D
performance of BLI. Using the same trained BLI model, we compare the R-D-prioritized ordering
used in our main experiments with three alternative strategies: random ordering, raster scan, and
ascending order of the predicted scale parameter. Table 8 reports two types of BD-rate. The full
BD-rate is computed using all progressive reconstruction points, whereas the endpoint BD-rate is
computed using only the fully decoded trit-plane endpoints.
The transmission ordering has a substantial effect on the full progressive R-D performance, while its
effect on the trit-plane endpoints is relatively small. The results indicate that transmission ordering
mainly affects how efficiently information is delivered at intermediate prefixes within each trit-plane,
while the reconstruction quality at fully decoded trit-plane endpoints is less sensitive to the ordering.
The R-D-prioritized strategy therefore improves progressive efficiency primarily by transmitting
high-impact trits earlier within each plane.
