IsoVRBench: Video Reward Modeling
Without Human Feedback
Shuyue Stella Li1,2, Jacqueline He1,2, Jiahui Chen2, Sreya Dutta Roy2, Yulia Tsvetkov1, Luke Zettlemoyer1,2,
Marjan Ghazvininejad2,†, Xiaochuang Han1,2,†
1University of Washington, 2Meta Superintelligence Labs
†Equal last author
Video reward models are trained and evaluated on human preferences between videos that often
differ simultaneously on properties such as prompt alignment, visual fidelity, motion, and generator
identity. As a result, aggregate accuracy does not reveal which video properties a reward model
can reliably distinguish. We construct preference pairs by controlled interventions that change one
property of a generated video while holding the rest fixed. The resulting pairs, by construction,
provide preferences for how the videos differ along a specific dimension, without requiring any
human annotation. We propose IsoVRBench, a video generation reward modeling benchmark of 8,000
such pairs across eight interventions, and show that state-of-the-art models separate broad quality
differences between generators but miss important features such as temporal order. The best reward
model reaches only 60.8% on reordered frames against 100% for humans, and rankings on existing
benchmarks do not predict this failure. Using the synthetic isolated intervation pairs as training
data leads to 1) IsoVRM, a reward model outperforming its counterpart trained with a larger human
labeled preference dataset by 3.8 points on VBench-2.0, 2) IsoWan, the video generator post-trained
with IsoVRM, outperforming its human-data counterpart in human preferences, and 3) gains that
stack when combined with human data. Because intervention pairs are automatically generated
rather than collected, the benchmark and training data can be easily extended as generators evolve.

Introduction
Reinforcement learning post-trains video generators against reward models (Liu et al., 2025b; Rafailov et al.,
2023; Wallace et al., 2024), which rank candidates at sampling time and define the objective for preference
optimization. A generator therefore improves only along the properties its reward model perceives. Yet video
reward models (VRMs) are trained and evaluated on human preferences between independently generated
videos, which differ simultaneously in visual quality, content, motion, and generator identity. Neither the
preference labels nor a model’s accuracy on these comparisons reveals which properties the model perceives,
even when annotators are instructed to judge a particular dimension. Existing benchmarks also rely on
outputs from fixed generator sets and per-sample human labels (Liu et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2024; Tong et al.,
2025; Zheng et al., 2025), which limits their scale and freshness as generators and their failure modes evolve.
We construct comparisons by controlled intervention instead, and use them to evaluate reward models, train
them, and then post-train the video generators (Figure 1). Starting from an anchor video, an intervention
changes one property while holding the rest fixed, so both the preferred side and the property under test
follow from the construction. Temporal Shuffle, for example, preserves every frame of the anchor but changes
their order, testing whether a model uses frame order, while Frame Hold creates stuttering motion from
the anchor’s own frames. No pair needs a human label, and human validation is needed only once per
intervention to confirm that the intended change is perceptible. Because the same interventions apply to any
generator’s outputs, the pairs can be regenerated as generators evolve. We use this procedure to construct

Evaluating nine VRMs and two general-purpose vision-language judges on IsoVRBench, we find that models
are substantially more accurate on broad differences between generators than specific and easily perceivable
differences such as temporal changes that preserve visual content. On shuffled videos, the best reward model
reaches only 60.8% against 100% for humans, and no model exceeds 66.1%. Rankings on existing reward and
temporal-understanding benchmarks do not predict sensitivity to frame order.
The same construction yields a training corpus of 160,000 pairs. IsoVRM-Q2, trained on these intervention
pairs, outperforms its counterpart VideoReward (Liu et al., 2025b), which is trained from the same base
model on a larger human-annotated dataset by 3.8 points on VBench-2.0. When used as the reward for
post-training a video generator, IsoVRM-Q2 yields the generator preferred by human raters over the generator
trained with VideoReward. IsoVRM-VR, initialized from VideoReward and further trained on intervention
pairs, outperforms VideoReward, including 63.5% versus 56.3% on VBench-2.0 and 69.0% versus 66.4%
on MJ-Bench-Video. The generator post-trained with IsoVRM-VR reaches the highest VBench-2.0 score,
51.5 against 49.0 under VideoReward, with a human win frequency of 51.2%. Thus, intervention-derived
supervision can either replace human preference supervision or augment it, improving both reward-model
transfer and the generators trained against those rewards. We make three contributions.
• Controlled intervention pipeline for scalable VRM development, where changing one property at a time
determines both the preference label and the property under test without human labeling.
• IsoVRBench, a benchmark on which current VRMs and general-purpose judges perceive frame content but
not frame order, a failure that rankings on existing benchmarks do not predict.
• Intervention-derived supervision that trains competitive VRMs without human preference labels or in
addition to them, and improves downstream video-generator post-training.

Constructing IsoVRBench
2.1
Controlled intervention framework
Let 𝑥be a video generated from prompt 𝑝under sampling configuration 𝜃. An intervention ℐ𝐷produces a
counterpart 𝑥′ that differs from the anchor 𝑥along a set of dimensions 𝐷, either by transforming 𝑥itself or by
rendering a new video after changing 𝑝or 𝜃. Each intervention targets a set of dimensions 𝐷for which the
construction specifies the preferred side; for the interventions in this work, the anchor 𝑥is preferred, such
that 𝑥≻𝑑𝑥′ for each 𝑑∈𝐷. For an intervention to provide a controlled evaluation of a particular property,
we consider three conditions.
1. Perceptibility. The induced change should be detectable to human viewers, who should prefer the intended
side on the targeted dimension.
2. Confinement. The intervention should change only the targeted dimension, while holding other dimensions
fixed. This enables differences in reward to be attributed to the target property.

2.2
Intervention definitions
In this work, we employ eight distinct interventions to construct video pairs along different quality dimensions.
Table 1 summarizes the interventions by decreasing control over the anchor and Figure 2 shows one example
of each intervention.
Rearranging the anchor’s frames. The most controlled interventions modify only the temporal presentation of
frames from the anchor. Temporal Shuffle divides the anchor into six segments and permutes them so that no
segment remains in place, changing the order of events while preserving every frame. Because temporal order
is informative only when there is sufficient motion, we use the 1,000 anchors with the most motion (more
details in Appendix C). Frame Hold retains 8 to 12 anchor frames and repeats each until the next, preserving
the clip length while turning smooth motion into visible jumps. Because both interventions construct the
counterpart entirely from anchor frames, they strongly control for frame-level appearance and are labeled on
motion. We call them the frame-reuse interventions.
Regenerating part of the clip. The next three interventions introduce newly generated content by changing a
single input. Together with the three below, they are the re-rendering interventions, since their counterparts
contain newly rendered frames. Motion Re-gen retains 8 or 12 anchor frames as keyframes and regenerates each
interval using the anchor’s own generator, starting from one keyframe and returning to the next. This allows
motion to deviate from the anchor’s trajectory between fixed keyframes while preserving the surrounding
scene. Random keyframe spacing varies the duration over which this deviation can occur. Attribute Edit edits
or removes one attribute of the prompt and re-renders using the same generator and seed; the resulting
video is scored under the original prompt, making the edited attribute the targeted source of misalignment.
Reduced Steps re-renders the same prompt and seed using 8 denoising steps instead of 50 in the anchor. The
three interventions target motion, text alignment, and visual quality, respectively.
Substituting the generator. The last three interventions replace the anchor’s generator (Wan2.2-T2V-A14B) with
a weaker generator while preserving the prompt. Aligned Weak Gen renders with Wan2.2-TI2V-5B conditioned
on the anchor’s first frame, while Weak Gen (5B) and Weakest Gen (1.3B) render the prompt with Wan2.2-TI2V-
5B and Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, respectively. Because changing the generator can simultaneously affect visual
quality, motion and text alignment, these pairs receive an overall label and deliberately give up confinement.
They resemble the independently generated comparisons used in existing preference benchmarks, allowing
Section 4.1 to compare published rankings with performance on these interventions.

2.4
Corpus and splits
IsoVRBench contains 1,000 pairs per intervention. In total, the benchmark contains 8,000 pairs spanning
15,047 clips and 7,129 prompts, with chance accuracy at 50%. For the intervention-strength analysis in
Appendix B.2, we additionally construct a Reduced Steps split using 16 rather than 8 denoising steps. The
training corpus contains 164,000 pairs spanning 234,075 clips and 70,075 prompts, with 160,000 pairs for
training and 4,000 for validation. Prompts are disjoint across splits because multiple interventions can reuse
the same anchor clip, making a pair-level split susceptible to video leakage. Benchmark prompts are also
disjoint from the training corpus.
3
Benchmarking reward models on IsoVRBench
We evaluate nine reward models:
VideoReward (Liu et al., 2025b), VisionReward (Xu et al., 2024),
PickScore (Kirstain et al., 2023), UnifiedReward-Flex-9B and UnifiedReward-2.0-8B (Wang et al., 2025),
VideoScore v1 and v1.1 (He et al., 2024), VideoScore2 (He et al., 2025), and LiFT (Wang et al., 2024). For
models with property-specific reward heads, we use the head corresponding to the labeled property; for
single-score models, we use their overall score. To see whether weaknesses are specific to reward-model
training, we also evaluate two general-purpose vision-language judges that are not trained as video reward
models: Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) and GPT-4.1 (OpenAI, 2025). Each judge scores a clip its
prompt and eight uniformly sampled frames, using the expected value over the ten digit tokens, then compare
the two scores from the pair. Evaluation details are in Appendix E.
Humans outperform every reward model on every intervention. Table 2 reports performance on IsoVRBench on
each isolated dimension. The best reward model, VideoReward, averages 73.3%, and human raters average
96.4%, agreeing with the constructed label on 347 of 360 rated pairs (Appendix O). The best model differs
across interventions, and five of the nine reward models are best on at least one.

General-purpose VLM judges share the failure. GPT-4.1 and Qwen3-VL-8B, which are not trained as video

therefore not specific to reward-model training.
Average accuracy hides differences between models. The five models whose averages lie between 68.1% and
70.1% differ by up to 15.2 points on a single intervention, most on the three motion interventions (13.9
on Temporal Shuffle, 15.2 on Frame Hold, and 14.0 on Motion Re-gen). PickScore and UnifiedReward-Flex-9B
average 69.7% and 68.4%, yet PickScore scores 14.0 points higher on Motion Re-gen and 9.4 points lower on
Reduced Steps. Fine-grained scores in each isolated dimension therefore allow us to better understand the
gap in current models and enable targeted improvements.
4
Analysis
Models struggle the most on the frame-reuse interventions, specifically Temporal Shuffle and Frame Hold, the
two interventions where every frame is the anchor’s own, so failures in these dimensions come from the
models’ inability to identify temporal relations and smoothness. We show these failures are overlooked by
existing benchmarks and are not recovered by feeding more frames into the context.
4.1
No published benchmark predicts frame order sensitivity
We compare model rankings on Temporal Shuffle against two classes of benchmark, video generation reward
benchmarks that compare independently generated videos—VideoGen-RewardBench (Liu et al., 2025b),
GenAI-Bench (Li et al., 2024), and VBench-2.0 (Zheng et al., 2025)—and temporal video-language understand-
ing benchmarks—TemporalBench (Cai et al., 2025) and TempCompass (Liu et al., 2024b))—which present
a single video and evaluate its relationship to text. Figure 3 shows the performance correlations between
Temporal Shuffle and each of the existing benchmarks.

of its labels. TempCompass, built to test temporal understanding, instead tracks Attribute Edit (𝜌= +0.80), the
intervention that tests whether a video matches its prompt. Neither kind of benchmark therefore isolates
frame order, which IsoVRBench identifies as the prominent failure mode of current reward models.
4.2
More frames do not improve temporal judgments
The default frame count for each model is used in Table 2. We now vary the frame count for each model to
investigate whether seeing more frames improves the temporal judgments of VRMs. Figure 4 shows each
checkpoint re-evaluated on the same 2,000 pairs with 4, 8, 16, 32, and 48 frames.
More frames help slightly on Frame Hold but not on Temporal Shuffle . Averaged over the eleven models, moving
from four frames to the largest frame count each model accepts raises accuracy by 4.4 points on Frame Hold
and 1.5 points on Temporal Shuffle (Figure 4). VideoReward, GPT-4.1, and Qwen3-VL-8B gain, respectively,
24.6, 18.9, and 16.2 points on Frame Hold between four frames and their best frame count, with VideoReward
reaching 82.3. On Temporal Shuffle, only VideoReward improves substantially, by 16.9 points to 66.1%, while
GPT-4.1 and Qwen3-VL-8B lose 6.6 and 6.1 points, and VisionReward and LiFT stay below chance on both
interventions at every frame count. Seeing more frames therefore does not give these models access to
frame order. Denser sampling makes the repeated frames of Frame Hold easier to detect, consistent with

5
Intervention pairs as training data
5.1
Training reward models
We train two reward models on preference labels constructed by our intervention protocol. Both employ a
Qwen2-VL-2B backbone and follow the VideoReward architecture (Liu et al., 2025b), with a scalar reward
head at per-dimension marker tokens and a Bradley-Terry objective with ties (Bradley and Terry, 1952), and
Appendix G gives the training configuration.
We first ask whether intervention supervision can replace human preference supervision by training IsoVRM-
Q2 from a Qwen2-VL-2B initialization without prior reward-model training. We next ask whether intervention
supervision can augment human preference supervision, and train IsoVRM-VR by initializing from released
VideoReward weights, which have already been trained on human preference data.
Our intervention training data contains 20,000 pairs per intervention, or 160,000 in total, with another 500
prompt-disjoint pairs per intervention for validation. All IsoVRBench prompts are excluded, although the
benchmark uses the same eight interventions (Appendix H). We benchmark our reward models against
VideoReward, which shares the same backbone, model size, and reward architecture but receives solely
human preference supervision during reward-model training.
As Table 3 shows, IsoVRM-Q2, trained without human preference labels, outperforms VideoReward on
VBench-2.0 by 3.8 points, 60.1 against 56.3, and trails it on VideoGen-RewardBench (71.5 against 73.2),
GenAI-Bench (67.9 against 69.9), and MJ-Bench-Video (61.2 against 66.4). VBench-2.0 includes two generators
absent from VideoReward’s training data, and VideoReward rates both below their human win rates and
the two it trained on above them (Appendix J). This is the generator-identity shortcut of Section 6, since in
preference benchmarks the two generators’ identities alone predict 74.1% to 78.2% of the labels (Appendix I).
Intervention pairs carry little of this signal, because five of the eight interventions render both sides with
the same generator, and those five supply most of the transfer. Added to VideoReward’s supervision,
the intervention pairs raise VBench-2.0 from 56.3 to 63.5 in IsoVRM-VR, which also scores 73.3 against
73.2 on VideoGen-RewardBench and 69.0 against 66.4 on MJ-Bench-Video (Tong et al., 2025), and trails on
GenAI-Bench, scoring 68.2 against 69.9.
5.2
Training a video generator with intervention-supervised reward models
We fine-tune Wan2.2-TI2V-5B with group relative policy optimization (GRPO) for diffusion models (Liu et al.,
2025a) using each reward model in Table 3. Appendix R shows more training details.
Human annotators prefer the generator post-trained under IsoVRM-Q2, whose reward model saw no human
preference labels. Across every comparison in the three-way human study, it wins 266 of its 480 appearances,
the IsoVRM-VR generator wins 246, and the VideoReward-supervised generator wins 208, win frequencies
of 55.4%, 51.2%, and 43.3% (Appendix P reports the pairwise rates and agreement). On VBench-2.0 all
runs improve on the pretrained generator (Table 4), and the generators post-trained under IsoVRM-VR and
8


IsoVRM-Q2 reach 51.5 and 49.8, compared with 49.0 under VideoReward, the same order as their reward
models’ transfer accuracy on VBench-2.0 (Appendix Q gives every dimension). Both intervention-trained
reward models therefore yield generators that improve on the VideoReward-supervised one under human
judgments and under VBench-2.0.

Related work
Video reward models and their evaluation. Image and video reward models learn scores from human choices
between independently generated outputs, through scalar or multidimensional heads, decomposed questions,
reasoning-based scoring, or pairwise judgments (Kirstain et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Liu et al.,
2025b; He et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; He et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024, 2025), and general vision-language
models are also prompted as judges (Zheng et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024). Reward models are evaluated by
agreement with human preferences between generated videos in VideoGen-RewardBench, GenAI-Bench,
and MJ-Bench-Video (Liu et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2025), and generators by per-dimension
protocols in VBench, VBench-2.0, and EvalCrafter (Huang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024a).
Even when an annotation names the dimension to judge, the two videos differ in other dimensions and in
generator identity, so a model can succeed on correlated differences instead of the requested property, a
shortcut documented in other domains (Gururangan et al., 2018; Geirhos et al., 2020). Our pairs differ in one
property by construction.
Constructed supervision. Synthetic data transforms scarce source data into forms a model learns from more
readily (Yang et al., 2024). Image-quality assessment applies distortions of known severity but still collects
human quality labels (Ponomarenko et al., 2015), attribute-specific constructions support question generation
and latent preference models (Li et al., 2025a,b), and comparisons between stronger and weaker generators
give preferences without human labeling but only for overall quality (Geng et al., 2025). Our generator
substitutions reuse that signal, and the other interventions extend the construction to one named property
per video pair, with no human labeling.
Temporal order. Frame order has long served as self-supervision, for recognizing forward playback, detecting
shuffled frames, and sorting clips (Pickup et al., 2014; Wei et al., 2018; Misra et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2017; Xu et al.,
2019). Video-language benchmarks find models weak on temporal understanding beyond static content (Cai
et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024b), and TempCompass, the closest construction to ours, builds conflicting videos that
share static content and differ in one temporal property, by reversal or by spatial or temporal concatenation
(Table 12). These benchmarks test a single video against text, and Temporal Shuffle poses the same contrast as
a preference pair.
7
Conclusion
Controlled interventions make a reward model’s perception testable one property at a time, and they show
that current video reward models see frame content but not frame order, a failure that none of five existing
benchmarks reveals. Because the construction determines the preferred side, the resulting pairs need no
human labels, and human validation is required only once per intervention. Used as supervision, these pairs
train reward models that transfer to human-labeled benchmarks and yield generators people prefer, with
or without human preference data. Any property that can be written as an intervention can therefore be
measured in a reward model and taught to it, without collecting new human preferences.
Ethics Statement
IsoVRBench contains model-generated videos and does not require collecting videos of research participants.
Human participation is limited to perceptibility validation and preference judgments on generated clips.
Generated content and learned reward models may inherit biases, unsafe depictions, or representational
gaps from the source prompts and base generators. These artifacts are intended for research on video reward
modeling and should not be used as standalone measures of safety, factuality, or human values.

Additional validation
B.1
Confinement has to be judged frame by frame
Judging visual quality on whole videos mixes appearance with motion. Human raters preferred the Motion
Re-gen anchor on 17 of 20 decided video pairs but on none of 17 corresponding frame pairs. We therefore
test confinement on single frames.
For each of 45 clips, raters compare the anchor and Motion Re-gen frames at the index where the two differ
most, excluding the keyframes, which are identical. Raters judged 41 pairs equal, and the other four split
three to one (𝑝= 0.63, sign test). All eight identical controls were judged equal. The regenerated intervals use
30 denoising steps against the anchor’s 50, and the frame comparison shows no loss of per-frame quality
from it.

C
Temporal Shuffle anchor selection
Reordering is visible only in a clip that moves, so Temporal Shuffle draws on the anchors with the most motion.
We measure motion as the mean absolute difference between sampled frames in 8-bit pixel values, averaged
over pixels and color channels. For the benchmark, motion is computed on every third frame, and the 1,000
anchors with the most motion range from 18.3 to 56.1, against about 12 for a typical clip. The training corpus
instead keeps every anchor above 25, computed on every fourth frame, which admits 23% of them.
D
Tie handling
Tie rates range from zero to 61% and follow how finely each model scores. PickScore and VideoReward each
produce about 15,000 distinct scores and rarely tie. LiFT produces ten, assigns 0.9 to 40% of clips, and ties on
61% of pairs. VisionReward and VideoScore2 tie on 21% and 27%.
Paired clips share a prompt and most of their content, which raises the tie rate further for coarse scores.
Independent draws from LiFT’s score distribution would tie on 25.8% of pairs, against the observed 61.3%.
We count a tie as wrong because it gives no ordering for a difference people perceive.
E
Prompted judge protocol
Each judge answers with a digit from 0 to 9, and we rescale the expected digit under its token probabilities to
0–100. For Qwen3-VL the probabilities come from its logits. For GPT-4.1 they come from the ten most likely
tokens the API returns, which hold nearly all the probability of a single-digit answer.
Taking the decoded digit instead produces many ties. On samples of 200 pairs for Qwen3-VL and 60 for
GPT-4.1, Qwen3-VL ties on 47%–54% of pairs and GPT-4.1 on 17%–20%. The expected digit brings both below
1%. Asking Qwen3-VL for two decimals between 0 and 1 still yields only nine distinct values and a 54% tie

and 13 points above its 28.1 VBench-2.0 human win rate. With four generators, this pattern arises by chance
one time in eight.
Five of our interventions use the same generator on both sides and so carry no generator ranking. The
inherited ranking affects VideoReward on VBench-2.0, whose generators it partly never saw, and not on
VideoGen-RewardBench, whose generators it saw in training.
On VideoGen-RewardBench, GenAI-Bench, and MJ-Bench-Video, dropping the generator substitutions
costs 1.9, 1.6, and 0.5 points, 69.6, 66.3, and 60.7 against 71.5, 67.9, and 61.2 for IsoVRM-Q2, so the five
same-generator interventions supply most of the transfer.

Figure 4 omits three sets of points. LiFT stops at 16 frames because its VILA1.5-13B backbone has 4,096

Caption ranking on temporal-understanding benchmarks
A reward model gives one score per video and text, so we use the TemporalBench (Cai et al., 2025) and
TempCompass (Liu et al., 2024b) settings in which it picks among complete captions for a fixed video.
TemporalBench poses binary caption questions on short videos and reports Multiple Binary Accuracy, and
TempCompass poses binary caption selection and reports caption-matching accuracy.
We score each caption with the model’s text-alignment head, or its only output for a single-score model,
and select the unique maximum, counting exact ties as wrong. For UnifiedReward we use the continuous
alignment score of its pointwise video prompt.

R
Generator training
Each run draws from 12,303 training prompts and samples eight videos per prompt, with advantages
computed within each group. Videos contain 121 frames at 704 × 1280 and use 30 sampling steps. The reward
weights visual and motion quality by 0.5 and text alignment by 1.0. A KL penalty keeps the policy near a
reference policy that is updated every 10 steps. Each run uses 128 H200 GPUs and validates every 50 steps on
512 held-out prompts, with one video per prompt at 50 denoising steps.
