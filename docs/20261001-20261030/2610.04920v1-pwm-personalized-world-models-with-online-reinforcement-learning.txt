ABSTRACT
Pretrained world models can generate diverse environments, yet users often want
to explore a particular scene specified by their own video. This requires learn-
ing the scene’s visual identity while retaining the quality of action-conditioned
generation. We introduce Personalized World Models (PWM), a framework for
customizing interactive world models from short scene videos through online
reinforcement learning. In PWM, the support trajectory and its associated con-
trols provide reward feedback on continuations sampled from the current pol-
icy. In the GRPO instantiation, group-relative optimization updates a compact
LoRA adapter using a unified reward for scene appearance, visual continuity,
and motion, while base-policy anchoring regularizes changes to the pretrained
generation prior of a frozen Yume-5B backbone.
The same adaptation pro-
cedure is applied across real and rendered environments.
We also instantiate
PWM with DiffusionNFT as an alternative reward-guided optimization method
for learning the scene-specific adapter. We also introduce PWM-Bench, com-
prising 150 customization tasks across Indoor, Outdoor, and Gaming, with paired
evaluation on held-out continuations. The GRPO and DiffusionNFT instantia-
tions of PWM improve customization over native Yume in 71.3% and 65.3% of
the evaluated scenes, respectively, with positive mean gains across all three do-
mains. For the GRPO instantiation, matched SFT comparisons further demon-
strate higher mean customization gains and better mean image-quality scores
in every domain, while retaining frame-level visual quality close to the pre-
trained model. Code: https://github.com/AIGeeksGroup/PWM. Website:

INTRODUCTION
Interactive world models enable users to explore environments through action-conditioned video
generation. Recent systems combine pretrained visual generation priors with user controls and au-
toregressive rollout, supporting interactive exploration across diverse environments (Yu et al., 2025;
Mao et al., 2026). Personalizing this experience introduces a concrete requirement: the generated
world should retain the identity of the environment provided by the user. For a particular interior,
outdoor location, or game scene, its characteristic appearance and content should remain recogniz-
able as the generated trajectory progresses.
Personalization methods have demonstrated that pretrained generators can acquire visual identity
from limited reference observations (Ruiz et al., 2023). Interactive world generation extends this
objective to action-conditioned continuations. A short support video provides observations of a
scene’s materials, lighting, visual style, and characteristic content. Successful customization should
therefore strengthen fidelity to the target environment while retaining the visual quality of the un-
derlying generator.
Reward-based optimization provides a way to express preferences directly on generated images and
videos (Xue et al., 2025). For scene customization, the support trajectory can supply the reference
needed to evaluate those outputs: whether they match the environment’s appearance and whether
their visual evolution is consistent with the observed trajectory.
We introduce Personalized World Models (PWM), a framework for per-scene customization
through online reinforcement learning. Given a short support trajectory and its controls, PWM
learns a compact LoRA adapter on a frozen Yume-5B backbone. The current policy samples groups
of candidate continuations, and reward feedback derived from the support trajectory guides updates
to the scene-specific adapter. We instantiate this framework with GRPO and DiffusionNFT as alter-
native reward-guided optimization methods. Online learning here refers to collecting fresh rollouts
from the current policy during adaptation.
Both instantiations use native generation windows, the same adapter capacity, and a shared reward
for scene appearance, visual continuity, and motion. The GRPO instantiation additionally uses a
base-policy anchor. Optimization and checkpoint-averaging settings are fixed across scenes within
each instantiation.
To evaluate this capability, we introduce PWM-Bench, a suite of 150 per-scene customization tasks
spanning Indoor, Outdoor, and Gaming. Each task provides an 80-frame support segment and a
temporally later, non-overlapping 145-frame test continuation. The benchmark evaluates scene fi-
delity through appearance agreement and an entity-based hallucination penalty, complemented by
frame-level image-quality measurements. Comparisons use three paired random seeds per scene
under shared initial context, controls, and generation settings.
Across PWM-Bench, the GRPO instantiation of PWM improves customization over native Yume in
107 of 150 scenes, with a mean relative gain of 6.39%. The DiffusionNFT instantiation improves 98
of 150 scenes, with a mean relative gain of 5.22%. Both instantiations achieve positive mean gains
in every domain. Compared with matched SFT, the GRPO instantiation increases the fraction of
scenes improved over Native from 57.3% to 71.3% and achieves higher mean customization gains
across all three domains. It also obtains better domain-level means than SFT on MUSIQ, NIQE, and
BRISQUE, while remaining close to native Yume in these frame-level quality measures.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We introduce PWM, a per-scene customization framework that learns a compact adapter
from a short support trajectory through online reinforcement learning on a frozen interac-
tive world model, instantiated with GRPO and DiffusionNFT.
• We introduce PWM-Bench, comprising 150 customization tasks across three first-person
domains, with held-out continuations and a shared protocol for evaluating scene fidelity
and visual quality.
• We demonstrate positive average customization gains over native Yume across all three
domains with both optimization methods. Matched SFT comparisons show that the GRPO
instantiation benefits more scenes and achieves better mean image-quality scores.

RELATED WORK
Interactive world models.
Interactive world generation combines visual synthesis with action
control to produce trajectories that respond to user inputs. GameFactory (Yu et al., 2025) learns
action control from game videos and uses a domain adapter with multi-phase training to transfer
this capability to open-domain scenes. Yume (Mao et al., 2025) combines camera control with au-
toregressive video generation, while Yume-1.5 (Mao et al., 2026) develops context compression and
text-controlled interaction for continuous world exploration. These advances establish a foundation
for generating and navigating diverse environments. PWM studies how to specialize such a pre-
trained model to a particular observed scene. Given a short support trajectory, it learns a compact
scene adapter for subsequent action-conditioned generation in the same environment.
Personalization of generative models.
Personalization adapts a generative prior to visual content
specified by limited reference observations. DreamBooth (Ruiz et al., 2023) learns a subject from a
few images and synthesizes that subject in new contexts. Tune-A-Video (Wu et al., 2023) extends
adaptation from limited observations to video generation by tuning an image diffusion model on
a single text–video pair. Parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) support this
adaptation through trainable low-rank updates to frozen pretrained weights. PWM applies scene-
level personalization to an interactive world model: the support video provides observations of the
target environment, and the learned adapter is evaluated on a temporally held-out continuation under
action controls.
Reinforcement learning for visual generation.
Reward-based fine-tuning adapts visual gener-
ators to objectives defined on their outputs. DDPO (Black et al., 2024) treats iterative denoising
as a sequential decision process and applies policy gradients to optimize sample-level rewards.
VADER (Prabhudesai et al., 2024) optimizes video generation by propagating gradients from differ-
entiable reward models. DanceGRPO (Xue et al., 2025) applies group-relative policy optimization
to diffusion and rectified-flow models across image and video generation. DiffusionNFT (Zheng
et al., 2026) performs online reward-guided optimization through the forward noising process, in-
corporating positive and negative learning signals into a flow-matching objective without requiring
likelihood estimation. Building on this line of work, PWM obtains reward feedback from the target
scene’s support trajectory and updates a scene-specific adapter using online rollouts. We instan-
tiate PWM with GRPO and DiffusionNFT, using the same scene-level task, adapter capacity, and
reward definition. The reward combines visual agreement with initial-frame consistency and motion
magnitude matching, connecting reward-based optimization to the scene customization objective.
Weight averaging.
Weight averaging combines parameter states into a single model. Stochastic
Weight Averaging (Izmailov et al., 2018) averages weights along a training trajectory, while Model
Soups (Wortsman et al., 2022) averages models fine-tuned from a shared pretrained initialization.
PWM uses this idea within each scene’s adaptation run, averaging adapter checkpoints to retain a
single compact adapter for inference. Each optimization instantiation uses a checkpoint-averaging
rule that is fixed across scenes and does not use held-out test frames.

METHOD
We propose PWM, a per-scene personalization approach for interactive world models. Given a
short preceding trajectory, PWM trains a scene-specific LoRA on frozen Yume-5B to transfer scene
knowledge to a subsequent trajectory. The core objectives are visual identity tailored to the target
scene, persistent spatial layout, and action responsiveness, while retaining the general action and
control priors of the pretrained world model. For this reason, PWM combines scene-specific low-
rank parameterization, online reward-guided optimization with GRPO or DiffusionNFT based on
native generation windows, a unified reward that balances scene fidelity and motion, and a weight
averaging strategy that does not require selecting a single checkpoint.

Following DDPO and DanceGRPO (Black

a finite-horizon Markov decision process. Let ct = (ht, ut) denote the visual history and control

A complete latent trajectory τt = (zt,K, . . . , zt,0) produces one video segment and receives a termi-

sequence of autoregressive video windows.
We use online to mean that each data-collection round collects fresh trajectories from the latest
behavior-policy snapshot. It does not refer to continual adaptation after deployment.
DiffusionNFT.
DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al., 2026) performs online reward-guided optimization
through a forward-process flow-matching objective.
Generated samples are assigned reward-
dependent weights, which determine their contributions to positive and negative learning branches.
These branches are constructed from the current model’s velocity prediction and a fixed behavior-
policy prediction, allowing reward-guided updates without estimating sampling-trajectory likeli-
hoods. PWM instantiates this objective for scene-specific LoRA adaptation, as described in Sec-
tion 3.3.

We start from a frozen Yume-5B world model with parameters θ0. For each scene, PWM learns an

adapter capacity for both optimization methods, every scene, and every first-person domain.

3.4
A UNIFIED REWARD ACROSS DOMAINS
Both the GRPO and DiffusionNFT instantiations of PWM use one reward for all scenes and first-
person domains:
R = 0.50 Rgt + 0.30 Rlpips + 0.10 Rcross + 0.10 Rdyn.
(12)
The four terms give the optimizer distinct responsibilities. Rgt measures spatially aligned DINO
feature agreement between the generated segment and the real continuation of the support window.
Rlpips is one minus their LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) distance and therefore preserves perceptual
appearance. Both terms are computed per frame and aggregated as 0.7 times the temporal mean
plus 0.3 times the minimum, so a short failure cannot be hidden by otherwise strong frames. Rcross
measures DINO feature agreement between the first generated frame and the window’s ground-truth
first frame. Finally, Rdyn rewards agreement between generated and real optical-flow magnitudes,
so appearance fidelity cannot be obtained by suppressing motion. Together, the first two terms
specify what the scene should look like, while the latter two constrain how a valid continuation
should evolve.
All reward references are drawn exclusively from the support trajectory. We do not tune reward
weights per scene or per domain, and the evaluation metric is separate from this training reward
(Section 4). This separation makes PWM a single customization procedure rather than a collection
of scene-specific reward recipes.
GRPO combines component-wise normalized advantages through Eq. (5), while DiffusionNFT uses
the combined scalar reward through Eq. (8).

materials, and sense of place — on a continuation it has never observed. Our benchmark evaluates
this promise directly: each generated rollout is scored against the held-out ground-truth continuation
of its own scene.

4.2
SCORING PROTOCOL
Each scene is evaluated with three random seeds, shared across Yume and both PWM instantiations,
under deterministic inference. For each instantiation, a scene counts as improved if its mean paired
difference against Yume over the three seeds is negative. We report improvement rates and mean
and maximum scene-level relative gains by domain.
Adaptation uses only the support windows, and evaluation uses only the held-out test segment, so no
evaluated frame is seen during training. Both PWM instantiations are evaluated on the same fixed
set of scenes.

5.2
PER-SCENE TASK CONSTRUCTION
Each task contains an 80-frame support segment and a temporally later, non-overlapping 145-frame
test segment from the same scene. PWM adapts independently on each support segment and evalu-
ates the resulting adapter on its held-out continuation.
We use a shared control interface across all three domains. Each video window is conditioned on
translation commands (W/A/S/D), a camera-direction token, and three continuous motion magni-
tudes. For EgoVid-5M, these annotations are obtained from the associated VIO trajectory. For
YouTube videos, they are derived from estimated camera motion. For Minecraft, they are recorded
directly from the scripted agents.
The same source-side quality criteria are applied across domains. The retained tasks provide con-
tinuous and non-degenerate camera motion, consistent scene identity across the support and test

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
We introduced PWM, a framework for customizing a pretrained interactive world model to an in-
dividual scene through online reinforcement learning. PWM turns a short support trajectory into a
compact LoRA adapter using native-window generation, a shared reward, and a fixed checkpoint-
averaging rule. We also introduced PWM-Bench to evaluate per-scene customization across Indoor,
Outdoor, and Gaming. Across the evaluated suite, PWM improves customization over native Yume
in 107 of 150 scenes. The matched SFT comparison further demonstrates higher mean customiza-
tion gains and broader scene-level benefits, alongside visual quality close to the pretrained model.
Future work will extend this approach to longer interaction horizons and richer support trajectories,
enabling scene customization over more extensive exploration.

Section 5 specifies task construction and the shared experimental protocol. Appendices A–C provide
optimization settings, reward definitions, and scoring details. Evaluation uses three paired seeds on
held-out continuations, with no test frames used for adaptation or checkpoint construction.

A.1
ADAPTATION CONFIGURATION
For each scene, we train one LoRA adapter on the frozen Yume-5B backbone.
The adapter
is shared across six overlapping support windows of 29 frames, starting at zero-based indices
[0, 9, 18, 27, 36, 45]. Each window is conditioned on its own ground-truth first frame and corre-
sponding control caption. Windows are processed in this order, with adapter updates carried forward
within the scene.
For each window, the current policy generates a group of G = 4 trajectories using K = 4 stochastic
denoising steps. We compute the trajectory advantages and perform four optimization passes on
the collected group. Each pass accumulates gradients over all GK transitions before taking one
optimizer step. We train for eight epochs and save the adapter after each complete pass through the
six windows. This gives 48 rollout groups and 192 optimizer updates per scene.
Table 5 lists the shared configuration. Backbone forward passes use bfloat16, and trainable LoRA
parameters are stored in float32. Reward networks are frozen and evaluated without gradient propa-
gation through them.

EVALUATION DETAILS
Our evaluation measures the benefit of adapting a pretrained world model to an individual scene. We
compare each adapted model with native Yume on the same held-out continuation, then summarize
both the magnitude and the distribution of scene-level benefits. This appendix specifies the paired
evaluation, aggregation rules, and scoring implementation.

C.1
PAIRED SCENE CONTINUATIONS
Each scene provides a support trajectory for adaptation and a later, non-overlapping test trajectory
for evaluation. PWM and Native use the same initial visual context, control sequence, native gener-
ation geometry, decoding configuration, and 145-frame evaluation horizon. Native is evaluated with
the scene adapter disabled. In the SFT comparison, the SFT adapter is evaluated under the same
conditions.
We use the paired random seeds Q = {42, 123, 777} with deterministic inference for each seed.
Predictions are scored against the corresponding ground-truth test frames over the full horizon. The
scene is the unit of aggregation: seed-level scores are averaged within a scene before results are
combined across scenes.
