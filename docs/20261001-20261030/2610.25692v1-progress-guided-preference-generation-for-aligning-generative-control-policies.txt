Abstract: Generative policies have achieved strong performance in imitation
learning tasks, but their success is often constrained by the limited availability
of expert demonstrations. Although preference-based fine-tuning can leverage
suboptimal rollouts to improve performance, existing approaches heavily rely
on expensive human annotations. In this work, we introduce Progress-Guided
Preference Generation (PGPG), an unsupervised preference alignment framework
that eliminates the need for human-in-the-loop supervision. Our method leverages
ordered coverage alignment to measure sequential progress relative to a small set
of expert demonstrations, enabling the automatic extraction of pseudo-preference
labels from autonomously collected rollouts. Furthermore, we introduce key
enhancements to improve the reliability of these progress-guided preferences,
enabling effective fine-tuning of pretrained generative policies without requiring
manually designed reward functions. Extensive experiments across a suite of
challenging long-horizon manipulation tasks demonstrate that PGPG outperforms
standard supervised fine-tuning baselines. Moreover, our conceptually simple
framework is broadly applicable across multiple classes of generative policies,
demonstrating strong versatility when integrated with both diffusion and flow-
matching policies. Project site is available at: https://pgpg2026.github.io.

Introduction
Learning robotic policies that generalize beyond curated demonstrations remains a central challenge
in reinforcement learning and imitation learning [1, 2, 3]. Behavior cloning can produce strong initial
policies from expert data, but it is often brittle under distribution shift and highly sensitive to data
quality. This issue is particularly pronounced in real-world settings, where expert trajectories are
scarce, training coverage is limited, and the available demonstrations may themselves be suboptimal
[4, 5]. Reinforcement learning, by contrast, can improve robustness through trial-and-error interaction.
However, it typically requires carefully engineered reward functions, extensive environment interac-
tions, and challenging exploration processes, all of which may be inefficient or unsafe in real-world
control scenarios [6, 1, 7]. Recently, preference-based learning [8] has emerged as a promising middle
ground between imitation learning and reinforcement learning [9, 2]. Instead of relying solely on
expert demonstrations or manually engineered reward functions, preference-based methods replace
explicit reward design with relative comparisons between trajectory segments. This allows policies
to learn from simple preference queries rather than handcrafted scalar rewards [1, 10, 11, 2]. This
paradigm is particularly attractive for robotics, where designing reliable reward functions is difficult,
and where improving the performance and robustness of pretrained policies while minimizing costly
environment interaction is highly desirable [9, 12, 13, 14, 15].

A major obstacle to scaling preference-based learning is its heavy reliance on human feedback,
which is inherently costly and labor-intensive [16, 2]. This bottleneck is particularly restrictive for
modern generative control policies, such as diffusion [5] and flow-matching models [17]. While
these architectures are highly expressive and excel at capturing multimodal action distributions, they
currently lack scalable alignment mechanisms when labeled preferences are scarce [14]. Because
existing alignment methods typically assume access to abundant, high-quality human annotations,
their real-world applicability remains severely limited [9, 18, 14]. These limitations highlight an
open question: How can we automatically extract meaningful preference signals from autonomously
collected rollouts to effectively fine-tune generative policies without human-in-the-loop supervision?
To this end, we propose Progress-Guided Preference Generation (PGPG), an unsupervised framework
for fine-tuning generative control policies. The key idea is to use ordered coverage alignment to
measure the continuous progress of collected rollouts relative to a small set of expert demonstrations,
and then convert this progress into preference labels for segment pairs. Importantly, the resulting
signal does not merely evaluate whether a rollout reaches the final goal, but also whether it exhibits
ordered progress that is structurally consistent with expert behavior. To make this signal suitable
for preference ranking, we transform cumulative progress into marginal rewards, avoiding temporal
double-counting and enabling meaningful comparisons between trajectory segments. We further
aggregate progress across multiple demonstrations, which is particularly important in robotic ma-
nipulation tasks where multiple valid strategies may exist and a single demonstration should not
overly constrain learning. In this way, PGPG transforms limited demonstration coverage into a
scalable source of supervision for preference alignment. Extensive experiments across a suite of
challenging manipulation tasks from Robomimic [4] demonstrate that PGPG can effectively fine-tune
generative policies using only a small set of expert demonstrations together with a large collection
of autonomously collected rollouts. We further demonstrate the versatility of our framework by
seamlessly integrating it with multiple classes of generative policies, including Diffusion Policy [5]
and Rectified Flow Policy [19].

Related Work
Preference-based Reinforcement Learning.
Extensive research has explored aligning agent
behavior through pairwise trajectory ranking [1, 20, 21, 10, 11, 13, 12]. The traditional paradigm in
preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) typically operates in two stages: first fitting a proxy
reward model from preference data, and subsequently using the learned reward to guide downstream
policy optimization [1, 11, 22, 12, 23]. While this paradigm has demonstrated strong performance
across domains ranging from Atari [1, 20] to large language models [24], the intermediate reward-
modeling stage introduces additional optimization complexity. More recently, methods such as Direct
Preference Optimization (DPO) [25] and Contrastive Preference Learning (CPL) [2] have eliminated
the need for an explicit reward model. Instead, these approaches directly fine-tune policies to satisfy
preference constraints through a simplified classification objective. Owing to their simplicity and
efficiency, these methods have been adopted for fine-tuning generative control policies [9, 14, 26].
However, applying PbRL to robotic control remains heavily bottlenecked by the substantial human
effort required to label large amounts of interaction data [16, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. To eliminate this
human-in-the-loop burden, our framework automatically extracts preference labels from a limited set
of offline expert demonstrations. Furthermore, while recent automated labeling pipelines rely on large
language models to generate Python reward functions [9], PGPG derives preference labels directly
from expert demonstrations, naturally grounding the learning signal in the visual and kinematic
structure of the task.
Generative Control and Preference Alignment.
Recent advances in generative modeling, par-
ticularly diffusion and flow-matching frameworks [32, 33, 34, 35], have demonstrated a remarkable
capability to capture complex, multi-modal distributions. This high expressiveness makes them
uniquely suited for decision-making and robotic control, triggering broad adoption across offline RL
[36, 37], online RL [38, 39, 40], and manipulation tasks [5, 41, 42]. By framing policies as generative
2


processes, these approaches model multi-modal action trajectories more robustly than conventional
Gaussian or VAE-based baselines [7, 43]. Early foundational works like DQL [7] integrated diffu-
sion processes directly into Q-learning frameworks. Subsequent iterations focused on stabilizing
optimization and improving evaluation. For instance, SfBC [3] decoupled behavior cloning from
explicit action selection, while CEP [44] introduced generalized energy-guided sampling. To address
the computational overhead of iterative inference denoising, recent lightweight formulations such as
FQL [45] and OFQL [46] drastically minimize sampling complexity while preserving competitive
policy performance.
Concurrently, generative control models have emerged as competitive backbones for preference-
based alignment. While early methods like AlignDiff [47] and FTB [48] utilize complex, two-stage
diffusion planning loops, recent frameworks like FKPD [26] and UAPA [14] pivot toward highly
efficient, one-step direct policy optimization. While PGPG operates within this efficient one-step
alignment paradigm, it bypasses the core bottleneck of existing literature. Rather than relying on
costly, large-scale human annotations, PGPG synthesizes reliable preference labels directly from
autonomously collected rollouts by anchoring progress to a small expert dataset.

Preliminaries
Preference-based Reinforcement Learning.
Reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms [49] typi-
cally consider a Markov decision process (MDP), formally defined as a tuple M = (S, A, T , r, γ, p0),
where S and A denote the state and action spaces, T (s′|s, a) and r(s, a) represent the transition
dynamics and reward function, γ ∈[0, 1) is the discount factor, and p0 denotes the initial state
distribution. In our setting, the agent additionally receives a visual observation o ∈O, represented
as an image rendered from the underlying state s. In many real-world applications, the reward
function is not directly available, resulting in a reward-free MDP M \ r. Preference-based RL [1]
addresses this challenge by learning a reward function rϕ from preference data, typically provided by
human annotators. Given an unlabeled dataset of trajectory segments, annotators compare sampled
pairs and indicate which segment they prefer. These pairwise comparisons form a preference dataset
Dpref = {(σw, σl)}, where each trajectory segment of length L is defined as σ = (s1, a1, . . . , sL, aL),
and σw is preferred over σl. Recent methods [1, 20] typically employ the Bradley-Terry model [50]
to learn the reward function rϕ from these preference comparisons.

Sequence Matching.
Sequence-matching problems [52] have been widely explored in inverse
RL to align a learner’s trajectories with expert demonstrations. While many approaches rely on
optimal transport [53, 54, 55] to match expert trajectory distributions, these methods often struggle to
preserve temporal consistency and are prone to goal abandonment in long-horizon robotic tasks [56].
This limitation motivates the need for a structurally ordered metric. In this work, we adopt ordered
coverage alignment (ORCA) [56] to quantify the continuous progress of collected rollouts relative
to expert demonstrations. This measure is particularly well-suited for our framework because it
inherently handles temporal misalignments, such as variations in execution speed between the learner
and the expert. Concretely, given a learner trajectory τ of length H and an expert demonstration ˜τ of
length ˜H, the ORCA step-wise progress at timestep t in the learner’s trajectory is computed as:
R(t, τ, ˜τ) = Ct, ˜

This formulation tracks the probability that the learner has successfully completed all preceding
subgoals up to j in the correct order. Intuitively, Eq. (2) assigns a high score only when the agent
reaches and remains at the final target without skipping intermediate subgoals. Additionally, the
ordered coverage is non-decreasing over time: once the learner has covered a subgoal at a given
timestep, it remains at least equally successful at future timesteps. In this work, we leverage this
continuous progress measure to automatically derive preference labels for segment pairs, enabling
preference learning without a human-in-the-loop.

Method
While the preference learning objective in Eq. (1) provides a powerful mechanism for aligning
generative policies, existing approaches typically rely on human-provided preference labels. This
manual annotation process is expensive, difficult to scale, and introduces a major bottleneck for
robotic control tasks. To eliminate this dependence on human annotation, we propose Progress-
Guided Preference Generation (PGPG), a framework that automatically generates progress-guided
preferences to fine-tune policies toward expert behavior, as shown in Figure 1.

than an instantaneous, per-step reward. Directly summing these cumulative scores across a trajectory

segment and can lead to inaccurate preference rankings between segments.

While step-wise progress can also be estimated using recent progress reward models [57, 58, 59, 60],

dynamic programming-based metric that grounds the progress signal directly in domain-specific
demonstrations rather than pretrained VLM priors. As a result, PGPG can generate preference labels
significantly more efficiently while ensuring that the resulting supervision remains tightly aligned
with the underlying task dynamics.

progress signals without being forced to merely imitate a single global demonstration.
4.2
Implementation Details
Since ORCA evaluates high-dimensional visual observations within trajectories, we first project raw
image frames into a semantic latent space using a pretrained visual encoder before computing pairwise
distances. Specifically, we employ R3M [61] as the visual backbone, which in our preliminary
experiments provided more robust semantic alignment than the ResNet50 [62] used in the original
ORCA implementation [56]. By applying PGPG to score these trajectory segments, we construct a
preference dataset Dpref. This dataset is subsequently utilized to fine-tune the pre-trained policy via
the CPL objective defined in Eq. (1). The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.
5
Experiments
In this section, we conduct comprehensive experiments across a suite of complex manipulation
tasks to investigate the following questions: (1) Can PGPG synthesize reliable preference labels
to guide policy learning? (2) How effectively does our framework improve the performance of
pretrained generative policies compared to supervised fine-tuning (SFT) baselines? (3) How do
critical hyperparameters influence the overall performance of PGPG?
5.1
Experimental Setups
To evaluate the effectiveness of our method, we adopt the Robomimic benchmark [4], which consists
of five tabletop manipulation tasks: Lift, Can, Square, Transport, and Tool Hang. These tasks cover
diverse manipulation skills and long-horizon behaviors, ranging from simple pick-and-place tasks to
precise insertion tasks. To evaluate the generality of our framework, we conduct experiments using
both Diffusion Policy [5] and Rectified Flow policies [19]. Following prior work [14], for each task,
the reference policy πref is trained to achieve approximately 40% success rate, after which we collect
500 rollout trajectories to construct the rollout dataset. We further retain only 40% of the original
demonstration dataset, resulting in approximately 120 demonstrations per task (80 for Tool Hang).
We use the mh datasets from Robomimic for all tasks except Tool Hang, which only provides a ph
dataset. All methods are trained using low-dimensional state observations. Visual observations are
used solely by PGPG for preference generation. We evaluate the performance of the fine-tuned policy
after 1000 training epochs. Results are averaged over 50 episodes across three random seeds, as in
[63]. We compare PGPG against the following baselines:

5.2
Main Results
As shown in Figure 2, PGPG achieves the highest average success rate across all tasks, significantly
outperforming non-privileged baselines such as Mixed BC and DPPO. In fact, Mixed BC yields only
marginal improvements and occasionally degrades base policy performance. Among the baselines
that use privileged information, Filtered BC and CPLKL, the preference-based fine-tuning of CPLKL
achieves stronger results. This indicates that selectively ranking trajectory quality provides a more
effective learning signal than directly mixing successful rollouts with expert demonstrations.
Notably, on tasks with dense ground-truth rewards, PGPG matches the performance of the reward-
guided CPLKL baseline (with the sole exception of the Flow Policy on Square). This confirms that
our automatically generated preferences effectively replace manually designed reward functions.
Furthermore, PGPG consistently outperforms CPLKL on sparse-reward tasks (Transport and Tool-
Hang). We attribute this advantage to the limitations of sparse rewards, which assign nearly identical
supervision to distinct segment pairs, failing to capture subtle differences in progress. In contrast,
our progress-guided preferences deliver dense, temporally structured supervision, leading to superior
policy fine-tuning.

5.3
Ablation Studies
Unless otherwise stated, all ablation studies are conducted with the diffusion policy.
Choice of Alignment Metric.
We investigate the effectiveness of different sequence alignment
metrics for preference generation by applying our Marginal Progress Scoring (MPS) to Temporal OT
[55], DTW [66], and the ORCA baseline [56]. As shown in Table 1, MPS yields large, consistent
gains only when combined with ORCA. When applied to other metrics, MPS produces inconsistent
results: it generally improves Temporal OT (except on Square) but degrades DTW on three of five
tasks. This suggests that the gains arise from the interaction between marginal scoring and ORCA’s
ordered-coverage structure, rather than from MPS or ORCA alone. This finding is consistent with
ORCA’s analysis [56]: DTW and Temporal OT do not reliably capture full subgoal coverage, resulting
in an unreliable ranking signal. In contrast, ORCA’s score is provably monotone in the number of
subgoals covered.
Impact of the Number of Demonstrations.
We evaluate the data efficiency of PGPG by varying
the number of expert demonstrations used for label synthesis (Figure 3a). The results indicate rapid
performance improvements as the number of demonstrations increases from 1 to 10 across both the
Lift and Square tasks. Notably, the success rate saturates after 10–20 demonstrations, highlighting
that our framework can achieve near-optimal policy alignment without the burden of large-scale
expert data collection.
Quality of Pseudo-Preference Labels.
We evaluate the accuracy and retention rate of our synthe-
sized preference labels against ground-truth preferences under varying discrimination thresholds (ϵσ).
As illustrated in Figures 3b and 3c, adjusting this threshold reveals a critical trade-off between label
reliability and dataset scale. While larger thresholds improve label accuracy by filtering out ambigu-
ous segment pairs, they simultaneously reduce the amount of usable training data. Consequently,
downstream policy performance peaks at a moderate threshold (ϵσ = 0.6), which effectively balances
label quality and data retention. In contrast, excessively large thresholds (ϵσ = 0.95) severely reduce
the available training data, leading to a sharp performance degradation despite near-perfect label
accuracy. Further ablations are provided in the Appendix.

6
Conclusion
We presented Progress-Guided Preference Generation (PGPG), an unsupervised framework that
aligns generative control policies without relying on human annotations or explicit reward engineering.
By leveraging ordered coverage alignment, our method autonomously evaluates sequential progress
against a small set of expert demonstrations, translating suboptimal rollouts into robust pseudo-
preference labels. Empowered by key enhancements to ensure label reliability, PGPG significantly
improves the performance of pretrained generative policies on challenging, long-horizon manipulation
tasks, outperforming standard SFT baselines.
Limitations.
First, PGPG relies heavily on the robustness of the pre-trained visual encoder; future
work will explore online fine-tuning to support cross-embodiment alignment where temporal mis-
matches are severe. Second, visual sequence matching remains susceptible to task misspecification
under heavy occlusion. We plan to mitigate this by integrating richer modalities, such as depth or
tactile sensing. Finally, our Marginal Progress Scoring assumes a largely monotonic progression
toward the goal, which may struggle to accurately score long-horizon tasks that require strategic
backtracking or non-quasistatic behavior.

A.1.5
Multi-Demonstration Aggregation Operator
As shown in Table 6, the max operator consistently outperforms mean pooling across both tasks.
This validates our hypothesis: averaging progress scores across diverse demonstrations heavily
penalizes valid, multimodal behaviors by forcing segments to match a potentially non-existent
“blurry” trajectory. Conversely, the max operator explicitly preserves multimodality by rewarding a
segment if it aligns with any single valid expert mode, effectively mitigating spurious matches and
inconsistent preference rankings.

A.1.8
Applying PGPG with GR00T N1.7
The experiments in the main paper fine-tune task-specific diffusion and flow-based policies, each
trained on a single Robomimic task. To test whether PGPG generalizes beyond this setting, we apply
it to GR00T N1.7 [71], a large pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) model, on the multi-task
LIBERO-10 benchmark [72]. This evaluation further demonstrates the effectiveness of our method in
three aspects:
(i) Multi-task learning. A single language-conditioned policy is fine-tuned on all ten tasks at
once. Preferences are generated for each task against that task’s own demonstrations and
then pooled into one fine-tuning run, without task-specific models or hyperparameters.
(ii) Pretrained VLA. The policy is a 3B-parameter model in which a vision-language backbone
conditions a flow-matching action head. PGPG needs only the flow-matching loss of the
action head, which takes the place of the log-likelihood in CPLKL, so it applies to the VLA
without architectural changes.
(iii) Few-shot data. Only 5 demonstrations and 20 policy rollouts are available per task,
compared with about 120 demonstrations and 500 rollouts per task in the main experiments.
Benchmark and Dataset.
LIBERO-10 (also known as LIBERO-Long) consists of ten long-horizon,
language-conditioned manipulation tasks for a simulated Franka Panda arm, each chaining two sub-
goals (illustrated in Figure 5). The policy takes as input a third-person and a wrist-camera image
(256 × 256), the end-effector state, and the instruction. We study a few-shot learning setting in which
16


only five teleoperated demonstrations are available per task (50 in total, from the LIBERO-10 dataset
with no-op actions removed). The pretrained policy collects 20 rollouts per task (200 in total), of
which 75 are successful.
Pretrained Policy.
GR00T N1.7 combines a vision-language model (VLM) backbone (Cosmos-
Reason2-2B) with a Diffusion Transformer action head trained with flow matching, which predicts
action chunks of length Tp = 16. We obtain the pretrained policy πref by fine-tuning the released
GR00T N1.7 checkpoint on the 50 demonstrations for 5000 iterations, using a batch size of 16 and a
learning rate of 10−4, while keeping the VLM backbone frozen.
Implementation of PGPG.
Pseudo-preference labels are generated as in the main paper: ORCA
progress [56] is computed on R3M features [61] of the third-person image by comparing it with the
five demonstrations of the same task, and each segment is scored by its marginal progress, maximized
over the demonstrations. Segments span 160 environment steps (ten action chunks). For each task
we sample 5000 demonstration-vs-rollout queries and 5000 rollout-vs-rollout queries, require both
segments in each query to start at the same progress level (±0.05), and discard queries whose score
gap is below 0.1, leaving 47878 labeled pairs. The policy is fine-tuned with CPLKL [2] using πref
as the reference. Because the action head is a flow-matching model, the per-chunk log-likelihood
ratio is replaced by the difference between the velocity-prediction errors of πθ and πref, computed
with shared noise and flow time (α = 0.015, discount 0.999, weight 0.25 on the losing segment, with
per-step terms clipped at 0.2). Only the action head is updated using cached backbone features. We
train for 8000 steps with AdamW (learning rate 10−4, cosine schedule with 5% warm-up) and 48
preference pairs per batch on four NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPUs.
Baseline and Evaluation.
Filtered BC fine-tunes πref using the flow-matching objective on the
demonstrations and 75 successful rollouts, with the same learning rate, batch size, and number of
training steps. Unlike PGPG, it relies on the simulator’s success signal to select rollouts. All policies
are evaluated at their final checkpoints over 50 episodes per task for each of three seeds. For a given
seed, every method starts from the same initial states. The policies execute the first eight actions of
each predicted chunk, and an episode ends upon success or after 720 steps.
Results.
The results are shown in Table 8. PGPG improves the average success rate from 50.5% for
the pretrained policy to 72.1%, compared with 64.8% for Filtered BC. This corresponds to gains of
21.5 ± 1.8 percentage points over the pretrained policy and 7.3 ± 3.6 percentage points over Filtered
BC. PGPG achieves the best performance on seven of the ten tasks, with the largest gains on T1
(18.0% →64.7%), T8 (40.0% →84.0%) and T2 (58.0% →94.7%). Filtered BC performs better
on T5 and T6. Neither method solves T9 (success rates of at most 3.3%), where none of the 20
rollouts collected by the pretrained policy succeeds.

Base Policy
We adopt Diffusion Policy [5] and Rectified Flow Policy [73, 19] as the base policies in PGPG due to
their strong ability to model complex robot action distributions. Both policies employ Transformer-
based architectures, serving as the noise predictor and velocity predictor for diffusion and flow
matching, respectively. Additionally, following [5], we use two historical states as policy inputs and
predict Tp future actions, where Tp = 16 for Diffusion Policy and Tp = 10 for Rectified Flow Policy.
We denote the predicted action chunk by At = (at:t+Tp−1).

A.2.3
Environmental Details
We use Robomimic [4], a large-scale robotic manipulation benchmark designed for imitation learning
and offline reinforcement learning (RL). It consists of five manipulation tasks, each accompanied by a
proficient human (ph) teleoperated demonstration dataset. Four tasks (excluding Tool Hang) addition-
ally provide mixed proficient/non-proficient human (mh) demonstration datasets. In our experiments,
we use the mh datasets when applicable, as they are generally considered more challenging than the
ph datasets due to the inclusion of demonstrations from multiple non-proficient operators. The five
tasks considered in this paper are:
• Lift: The robot arm must lift a small cube.
• Can: The robot must place a coke can from a large bin into a smaller target bin.
• Square: The robot must pick a square nut and place it on a rod.
• Transport: Two robot arms must transfer a hammer from a closed container on a shelf to
a target bin on another shelf. One robot arm must retrieve the hammer from the container,
while the other arm must clear the target bin by moving a piece of trash to the nearby
receptacle. Finally, one arm must hand the hammer over to the other, which must place the
hammer in the target bin.
• Tool Hang: A robot arm must assemble a frame consisting of a base piece and hook piece
by inserting the hook into the base, and hang a wrench on the hook. This is the most
difficult task due to the multiple stages that each require precise, dexterous, rotation-heavy
movements.
A.2.4
Details of Baselines
For both Mixed BC and Filtered BC, we fine-tune the pretrained policies using a small set of expert
demonstrations combined with the exact same rollout trajectories used by PGPG. For Filtered BC,
any trajectories that fail to satisfy the task-specific success criteria are discarded. To ensure a fair
comparison, the reinforcement learning baseline, DPPO [39], is restricted to approximately the same
number of environment interactions (rollouts) as our method. Furthermore, both CPLKL and DPPO
utilize the same low-dimensional state inputs as all other methods. CPLKL relies on the ground-truth
environment rewards provided by the benchmark (dense when available), whereas DPPO uses the
sparse task rewards for policy optimization. All methods use low-dimensional state observations. For
PGPG, we additionally leverage the corresponding image observations (at a resolution of 224 × 224)
solely for pseudo-preference label generation.
A.2.5
Experimental Settings
For pseudo-preference label generation, we set the number of expert reference demonstrations to
M = 10 for Lift, M = 20 for Can, and M = 40 for the remaining tasks. In practice, we select the
M shortest demonstrations to form the reference set. To construct the initial preference dataset, we
uniformly sample N candidate segment pairs from the merged dataset—specifically, N = 10000
for Lift, N = 20000 for Can, and N = 30000 for the remaining tasks—and perform a search over
the discrimination threshold ϵσ ∈{0.3, 0.4, 0.6}. We select the highest threshold that successfully
retains at least 1,000 valid preference pairs for Lift and Can, and 5,000 pairs for the remaining tasks.
From a computational perspective, computing the ORCA metric in Eq. (2) between a rollout and an
expert demonstration incurs a dynamic programming cost of O(H · ˜H) [56]. To improve efficiency,
the entire labeling process is performed offline prior to policy training. Specifically, instead of
anchoring each rollout to a single demonstration, we compute and cache M independent progress
sequences per rollout trajectory, one for each expert demonstration. With these step-wise progress
20


traces precomputed, evaluating the aggregated score for an arbitrary segment reduces to an O(M)
operation. This requires only computing the marginal progress difference for each of the M expert
demonstrations and applying the max-pooling operation described previously. As a result, the
proposed caching strategy eliminates redundant sequence-matching computations and substantially
accelerates preference dataset construction. We follow the implementation of [56] using cosine
distance for the spatial cost matrix, while utilizing an adaptive temperature that scales dynamically
with the minimum spatial cost to ensure robust temporal alignment.
