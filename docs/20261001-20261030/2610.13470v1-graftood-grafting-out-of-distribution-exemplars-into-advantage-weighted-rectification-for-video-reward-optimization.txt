ABSTRACT
Video reward optimization can increase proxy rewards while reducing video dy-
namics, a mismatch we identify as motion collapse, including under a video-level
motion-quality reward. We propose GraftOOD, which replaces part of the on-
line rollouts in selected prompt groups with out-of-distribution video exemplars.
A shared velocity-regression objective combines group-relative reward rectifica-
tion with sequence-level exemplar supervision, requiring neither exemplar gen-
eration trajectories nor an additional supervised-loss coefficient. We character-
ize its pointwise optimal field as a source-posterior-weighted combination of the
two source-specific target fields. We construct GraftSet-Video, containing 9,588
format-matched exemplars for 2,397 prompts, to supply external supervision. On
Wan2.1-1.3B, GraftOOD raises Dynamic Degree from 4.86 to 44.41 relative to
reward-only FlowAWR, while HPSv3 decreases from 8.10 to 7.32 and Vision-
Reward remains close. At the main setting, GraftOOD leads nine of ten metrics
among three exemplar-augmented methods. Ablations of exemplar frequency and
within-group count reveal dynamics–reward trade-offs rather than uniform gains
from increasing exemplar usage. These results support external exemplar super-
vision as a complement to scalar reward feedback, without equating higher mea-
sured dynamics with coherent, visually plausible motion in the generated videos.
Code is available at GraftOOD.

INTRODUCTION
Reinforcement learning has become a central component of generative-model post-training, en-
abling learning from human preferences (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) and verifiable
outcomes (Shao et al., 2024). Unlike supervised imitation alone, it lets a model sample its own out-
puts, receive feedback, and concentrate updates on behaviors that satisfy the specified criteria. This
principle extends to visual generation, where learned evaluators guide improvements in aesthetic
quality and prompt correspondence (Black et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2025; Zheng
et al., 2025). These advances depend not only on the optimization algorithm, but also on how well
the reward represents the preferences that define the desired outputs.
For video generation, a proxy reward must account for both individual-frame quality and how con-
tent evolves over time. Optimizing this proxy favors high-scoring outputs, which need not satisfy
all of these preferences. In our experiments, optimizing frame-aggregated HPSv3 (Ma et al., 2025;
Team et al., 2025b) raises its score while reducing Dynamic Degree (Huang et al., 2024). Optimizing
VideoAlign’s Motion Quality (Liu et al., 2026b) likewise raises its reward while reducing Dynamic
Degree. We refer to this mismatch between rising rewards and declining video dynamics as motion
collapse. These observations are consistent with reward exploitation: a policy may increasingly
favor high-reward outputs whose temporal behavior is undesirable.

Existing methods improve reward optimization through policy gradients or regression objectives
(Liu et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2025; He et al., 2026), but improving a proxy objective does not by itself
resolve its mismatch with the desired outputs. Combining rewards is one response, yet it requires
choosing which attributes to score and how to balance them. In our experiments, adding a RAFT-
based motion reward (Teed & Deng, 2020) to HPSv3 mitigates but does not prevent the decline in
Dynamic Degree. Rather than relying solely on further reward design, external videos can provide
examples of outputs we want the policy to learn from. This raises the question: Can an external
exemplar distribution guide online reward optimization toward desired video attributes that
the reward does not adequately constrain?
DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al., 2025) optimizes rewards through velocity regression rather than like-
lihood ratios over generation trajectories. FlowAWR (Fu et al., 2026) further derives advantage-
weighted velocity rectification. This regression view provides a shared interface for online reward
optimization and external supervision: both update the same velocity network, but they differ in the
sample sources and regression targets that define each update.
We introduce GraftOOD, which replaces part of the online rollouts in selected prompt groups with
out-of-distribution (OOD) video exemplars. Online targets retain group-relative reward rectifica-
tion, while exemplars provide sequence-level supervision without their generating trajectories. Both
enter one regression objective without an additional supervised-loss coefficient. We characterize its
pointwise optimal field as a source-posterior-weighted combination of the online target field and the
exemplar reference field (Figure 2, top). This analysis describes the regression target, rather than
guaranteeing properties of generated videos. Figure 1 gives same-prompt qualitative comparisons
of video sequences generated with GraftOOD and reward-only optimization over multiple frames.
We construct GraftSet-Video from public video reward-model resources, supplementing selected
prompt–video pairs with outputs from external generators, including Seedance and Kling. The cor-
pus contains 9,588 videos for 2,397 prompts outside the original policy-training set, with four ex-
emplars per prompt and the policy’s resolution and frame count.
On Wan2.1-1.3B, GraftOOD uses an exemplar-prompt ratio of 0.167 and four exemplars per group
in the main comparison. Relative to FlowAWR, it raises Dynamic Degree from 4.86 to 44.41,
while HPSv3 decreases from 8.10 to 7.32 and VisionReward remains close. GraftOOD leads nine
of ten metrics in this comparison with FlashOOD and NFTOOD. Ablations of both exemplar fre-
quency and within-group count reveal dynamics–reward trade-offs, not uniform improvements from
increasing exemplar usage. Higher Dynamic Degree alone does not establish better motion quality.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We identify motion collapse in video reward optimization: increasing proxy rewards can
coincide with declining video dynamics, even with a video-level motion-quality reward.
These observations motivate sequence-level supervision beyond scalar reward feedback.

VIDEO REINFORCEMENT WITH OOD DISTRIBUTION GRAFTING
3.1
PROBLEM SETUP
Consider a video policy conditioned on a prompt c and updated using a terminal reward R(c, x1).
Online samples provide training experience, while the reward determines which outputs to encour-
age or suppress. As a proxy for the desired video attributes, the reward may also favor undesirable
outputs. Optimizing it can then concentrate the policy on a high-reward region that does not reflect
our preferences for visual appearance and temporal behavior.
Figure 2(a,b) illustrates this mismatch: optimizing HPSv3 raises its score while Dynamic Degree
declines. Optimizing VideoAlign Motion Quality likewise raises its reward while reducing Dynamic
Degree (Appendix B.3). Adding a RAFT-based motion reward mitigates but does not prevent the
decline observed with HPSv3 in the same panel. Meanwhile, Figure 2(c) shows that HPSv3 training
increases VisionReward (Xu et al., 2026), an evaluator not used for training. These results motivate
supplementing reward optimization rather than replacing it: improvement on another evaluator
coexists with loss of dynamics in the videos produced during reward optimization.
Additional reward terms revise how online samples are scored, requiring choices about the attributes
to measure and their relative weights. We instead introduce external videos as a distributional ref-
erence alongside reward feedback. They supply sequences for the policy to learn from without first
generating those examples itself. The selected exemplars provide direct sequence-level targets to
guide policy learning toward desired video attributes.
We construct the OOD exemplar distribution pood(x1 | c) from prompt–video pairs selected from
public video reward-model resources (Liu et al., 2026b; Xu et al., 2026; He et al., 2024; 2025; Tong
et al., 2025), supplemented with outputs from external generators such as Seedance and Kling. These
videos are selected to provide higher-quality references for the base policy. Their paired prompts lie
outside the original policy-training set, and the videos match the policy’s training format. OOD is
defined relative to this policy setting, not necessarily to the reward model’s training data. The policy
also generates fresh rollouts on these prompts for reward-relative updates. The exemplars provide
clean terminal latents, but not their generating trajectories. How can we incorporate these external
video references into online reward optimization, so that policy updates are guided by both reward
feedback and the sequence-level attributes of the selected exemplars?

3.3
PRACTICAL IMPLEMENTATION
GraftSet-Video.
We construct GraftSet-Video with exactly four video exemplars per prompt,
yielding 9,588 videos for 2,397 prompts. We first select videos from public video reward-model
training and evaluation resources, including those accompanying Liu et al. (2026b); Xu et al. (2026);
He et al. (2024; 2025); Tong et al. (2025), using HPSv3 scores (Ma et al., 2025). Keeping the
selected videos, we supplement prompts with fewer than four exemplars using additional outputs
from external generators, including Seedance (Seedance et al., 2025; 2026) and Kling (Team et al.,
2025a), to meet this four-exemplar requirement. These generators are chosen to provide higher-
quality video references for the base policy beyond its own online rollouts.
HPSv3-based selection is performed offline, inherits HPSv3’s limited motion sensitivity, and does
not enter training-time advantage estimation. Videos contain 81 frames at 832 × 480 and 16 fps, en-
coded with the policy’s VAE without further resampling. The prompts are disjoint from the original
19,700 training prompts and 300 evaluation prompts. Source resources may overlap reward-model
training data; OOD refers to the policy setting above. Figure 3 shows the source composition and
the mean HPSv3 score for each source; Appendix B.1 provides dataset details.

Exemplar scheduling and allocation.
We control exemplar usage through the OOD prompt ratio
ρ and the within-group exemplar count nood. The ratio ρ determines the fraction of training prompt
groups drawn from GraftSet-Video. Each such group combines nood of the prompt’s four stored
exemplars with G −nood fresh online rollouts. The remaining groups use original training prompts
with G online rollouts each and no external exemplar supervision.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Datasets, Models, and Baselines. Following DanceGRPO (Xue et al., 2025), we use 19,700 train-
ing prompts and add exemplars from GraftSet-Video. On Wan2.1-1.3B (Wan et al., 2025), we
compare the base model, Flash-GRPO (He et al., 2026), DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al., 2025), and
FlowAWR (Fu et al., 2026), alongside their exemplar-augmented variants FlashOOD, NFTOOD,
and GraftOOD, as defined by the three training objectives in Section 3.2.
Implementation Details. Training uses 20-step rollouts, classifier-free guidance (CFG) scale 4.5,
and group size G = 8. NFT/AWR variants use EMA sampling and CFG-free updates. We train
rank-32 attention LoRA adapters with AdamW at learning rate 10−4, freezing the backbone. The
main OOD comparison uses ρ = 0.167 and nood = 4. All methods use 50 sampling steps and
CFG scale 4.5 for evaluation, generating 81-frame videos at 832 × 480 and 16 fps. Further settings,
including LoRA scaling, numerical precision, and regularization, appear in Appendix B.2.
Rewards and Evaluation. We optimize HPSv3 (Ma et al., 2025), averaging the top 30% of per-
frame scores following LongCat-Video (Team et al., 2025b). We report VBench’s Aesthetic Quality,
Imaging Quality, Subject Consistency, and Object Class on its official prompt set (Huang et al.,
2024). On 300 VidProM prompts, we measure Dynamic Degree, VisionReward (Xu et al., 2026),
VideoAlign’s Video Quality, Motion Quality, and Text Alignment (Liu et al., 2026b), and HPSv3.
Appendix B.2 details the prompt sets, score aggregation, and evaluation protocol.

4.2
MAIN RESULTS
Relative to FlowAWR, GraftOOD raises Dynamic Degree (DD) from 4.86 to 44.41, while HPSv3
changes from 8.10 to 7.32 and VisionReward from 5.11 to 5.08 (Table 1). Although DD remains
below the base model’s 48.61, HPSv3 and VisionReward exceed its 4.70 and 1.26. GraftOOD
thus retains more dynamics than reward-only FlowAWR while preserving reward and visual-score
gains over the base model, despite the remaining gap in measured dynamics. All three exemplar-
augmented methods raise DD over their respective online baselines, but HPSv3 rises only for Flas-
hOOD and falls for NFTOOD and GraftOOD under the shared main-comparison configuration.
GraftOOD leads nine of ten metrics among OOD methods, including all four VBench metrics. Flas-
hOOD attains the highest DD (58.26), but the bottom-right basketball example in Figure 5 shows
character deformation. DD uses RAFT-estimated optical-flow magnitudes (Huang et al., 2024; Teed
& Deng, 2020) without distinguishing coherent motion from deformation. Deformation can there-
fore inflate DD, offering a possible explanation for FlashOOD’s score rather than evidence of better
motion. GraftOOD exceeds both OOD baselines on SC, VQ, and MQ at this setting.

RELATED WORK
Reward Alignment for Video Generation.
Preference alignment for video generation includes
pairwise objectives, such as VideoDPO (Liu et al., 2024), and online reward optimization. DDPO
and DPOK formulate diffusion fine-tuning through policy gradients (Black et al., 2024; Fan et al.,
2023). Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO extend group-relative optimization to flow-based visual gen-
eration using stochastic sampling (Liu et al., 2026a; Xue et al., 2025), while MixGRPO and Flash-
GRPO adopt mixed ODE–SDE sampling and single-timestep updates, respectively (Li et al., 2025;
He et al., 2026). Alternatively, DiffusionNFT optimizes reward-weighted regression on the forward
process (Zheng et al., 2025), and FlowAWR derives advantage-weighted velocity rectification (Fu
et al., 2026). GraftOOD builds on this regression formulation but addresses a different question:
how external video exemplars can supplement reward optimization when the proxy inadequately
constrains temporal behavior of the generated video sequences.
External Data for Reinforcement Learning.
External experience has been incorporated into on-
line RL through different learning signals. RLPD mixes offline transitions with online replay for
actor–critic learning (Ball et al., 2023), while LUFFY incorporates expert reasoning trajectories into
mixed-policy group-relative optimization (Yan et al., 2026). GraftOOD addresses a different super-
vision problem: external videos specify desired sequence-level behavior but provide no generating
denoising trajectories. This distinction concerns the form of external supervision, not merely its
source: each video provides a regression target without a recorded sequence of policy actions. We
use these terminal exemplars to construct supervised velocity targets and combine them with reward-
relative online targets within prompt groups, excluding exemplars from online reward normalization.
Beyond combining data sources, our analysis characterizes the resulting exemplar-guided velocity
field. This formulation supplements attributes inadequately constrained by the proxy reward without
an additional motion reward or training-time reward-model scoring of external exemplars.
6
CONCLUSION
We identified motion collapse in video reward optimization, where increasing proxy rewards can
coincide with declining video dynamics. We proposed GraftOOD to incorporate external sequence-
level supervision into group-relative velocity regression without an additional supervised-loss coef-
ficient. Our analysis characterizes its pointwise optimum as a source-posterior-weighted combina-
tion of online and exemplar target fields. With GraftSet-Video, experiments on Wan2.1-1.3B show
higher measured dynamics than reward-only FlowAWR, while exemplar-frequency and count abla-
tions reveal trade-offs with HPSv3 and VisionReward. These findings support external exemplars as
a complement to scalar reward feedback, rather than a replacement for reward optimization. Exem-
plar selection and motion-quality evaluation remain limitations: higher Dynamic Degree alone does
not establish coherent motion or improved temporal quality in generated videos.

Training and sampling configurations.
All experiments use Wan2.1-1.3B (Wan et al., 2025)
and 19,700 training prompts following DanceGRPO (Xue et al., 2025), with additional exemplar
prompts and videos from GraftSet-Video (Section 3.3). Videos contain 81 frames at 832 × 480 res-
olution and 16 frames per second. Online sampling uses classifier-free guidance (CFG); NFT/AWR
variants disable CFG for training updates. All reported evaluations, including the base model, use
50 sampling steps and a CFG scale of 4.5. Training rollouts use 20 sampling steps, a CFG scale of
4.5, and group size G = 8; NFT/AWR variants use EMA reference policies for sampling. We train
rank-32 LoRA adapters with scaling factor 64 on the attention projections, keeping the backbone
frozen. We use bfloat16 and AdamW with learning rate 10−4, weight decay 10−4, and gradient norm
clipping at 1.0. FlowAWR and GraftOOD set γ to the global standard deviation of online-rollout
rewards in the current batch, shared across prompt groups and excluding exemplars. FlashOOD uses
14


λood = 1 in all configurations. GraftOOD shares a regression timestep within each prompt group
and independently re-noises each video.
The main comparison uses ρ = 0.167 and nood = 4. The ablations separate two controls: the fre-
quency of exemplar prompt groups, ρ, and their within-group exemplar count, nood. The experimen-
tal grid uses ρ ∈{0.167, 0.33, 0.5} and nood ∈{4, 2, 1} with G = 8, retaining four, six, or seven
online rollouts per exemplar group, respectively. All configurations use the same GraftSet-Video
candidate pool, with four stored videos per prompt. Reducing nood changes how many candidates
are used in a group, not the corpus or prompt population.
Reward and evaluation protocols.
The main experiments optimize HPSv3 (Ma et al., 2025),
averaging the top 30% of per-frame scores following LongCat-Video (Team et al., 2025b). For
offline corpus construction, the initial selection from public resources retains videos with HPSv3
scores above 7, before supplementary generation fills the remaining exemplar slots. This initial
threshold does not impose a score floor on the supplemented corpus. External exemplars do not
enter training-time reward normalization. We report VBench’s Aesthetic Quality (AQ), Imaging
Quality (IQ), Subject Consistency (SC), and Object Class (OC) on its official prompt set (Huang
et al., 2024). On the 300-prompt VidProM evaluation set, we measure Dynamic Degree (DD),
VisionReward (VR) (Xu et al., 2026), VideoAlign’s Video Quality (VQ), Motion Quality (MQ), and
Text Alignment (TA) (Liu et al., 2026b), and HPSv3. Motion Quality serves as an evaluation metric
here; its use as a training reward in the motivation study is discussed in Section 3.1.
These evaluation sets remain unchanged across prompt ratios and exemplar counts. DD measures
detected dynamics rather than motion plausibility and should be read alongside the other metrics
and qualitative examples in Section 4.2.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Evaluation scope and exemplar sources.
Our experiments are limited to text-to-video generation
with Wan2.1-1.3B, and all exemplars in GraftSet-Video are generated videos. The nood = 1 ablation
evaluates a single-exemplar group configuration, but does not establish the effectiveness of real-
video supervision. The current results therefore leave generalization to other tasks, model scales,
and exemplar sources untested.
Real-video supervision and physical consistency.
Real-world recordings could provide comple-
mentary examples of object interactions and temporal behavior that generated exemplars may re-
produce imperfectly. Whether such supervision improves physical consistency remains an empirical
question, requiring suitable video–text pairing, data selection, and evaluation. GraftOOD offers a
regression interface for investigating this direction because exemplar supervision requires terminal
videos rather than their generating denoising trajectories; this does not by itself guarantee successful
transfer to real-video data.
Extension to audio–video generation.
A further direction is text-to-audio-video generation
(T2AV), where supervision must account for both visual dynamics and audio–visual correspon-
dence. Such an extension would require appropriate joint representations and reward models sen-
sitive to temporal synchronization and cross-modal consistency. More broadly, advances in re-
wards and evaluation remain necessary to determine whether exemplar-guided optimization im-
proves physical plausibility rather than merely increasing proxy scores.
