ABSTRACT
Latent diffusion models enable high-quality video generation, but video VAE en-
coding and decoding remain computationally expensive. Existing methods use
stage-specific lightweight operators, yet either constrain spatial and temporal com-
putation jointly or remove temporal computation altogether, without distinguish-
ing their compression sensitivities in pretrained Conv3D. To address these lim-
itations, we present LightVAE, a compression framework that exploits redun-
dancy in temporal modeling and network depth. LightVAE exactly reparame-
terizes eligible causal Conv3D layers into an aggregate spatial response and a
difference-driven temporal correction. Based on their distinct compression sensi-
tivities, temporal low-rank compression (TLR) keeps the aggregate spatial ker-
nel dense and applies layer-specific low-rank approximation only to the tem-
poral correction.
An algebraically equivalent folded implementation converts
the arithmetic savings into practical acceleration. LightVAE further uses width-
preserving block pruning to reduce depth and probe-guided feature alignment,
guided by feedback from a frozen teacher suffix, to recover quality after com-
pression. The framework also extends to video encoders. Across three video
VAE backbones and two GPU platforms, LightVAE consistently accelerates de-
coding. For the Wan VAE family, it achieves up to 2.24× and 1.11× the de-
coding throughput of state-of-the-art efficient VAE decoders on NVIDIA H100
and RTX 5090, respectively, with higher DAVIS PSNR and lower LPIPS. On
MiniMax-H3, it achieves up to 2.97× the original decoding throughput while re-
taining 99.51% of the original model’s overall VBench-2.0 score. Our code is
available at https://github.com/ModelTC/LightVAE.

INTRODUCTION
Recent video generation models have advanced in visual fidelity, motion coherence, and prompt
alignment (Yang et al., 2025; HaCohen et al., 2024; Team Wan et al., 2025). Many operate in a
latent space (Rombach et al., 2022), where a video variational autoencoder (VAE) maps between
pixels and compact spatiotemporal representations, while a diffusion Transformer (DiT) (Peebles &
Xie, 2023) generates video latents through iterative denoising. Although this design reduces gener-
ation cost, both denoising and VAE inference remain expensive for high-resolution or long videos.
Most acceleration methods target denoising. Diffusion distillation reduces the number of sampling
steps (Salimans & Ho, 2022; Yin et al., 2024b;a; 2025; Fan et al., 2026); caching reuses compu-
tation across steps (Liu et al., 2025); and efficient attention, quantization, and optimized kernels
reduce per-step cost (Zhang et al., 2025; LightX2V Contributors, 2025). As denoising becomes
faster, VAE inference can account for a larger fraction of end-to-end latency (Zou et al., 2026; Zhu
et al., 2026). In convolutional video VAEs, causal Conv3D becomes particularly expensive in high-
resolution decoder stages, where dense spatiotemporal kernels operate on large feature maps. We
therefore focus primarily on decoder compression.

A practical approach is to compress a pretrained decoder while preserving its latent interface and
recovering quality through teacher supervision. Turbo-VAED (Zou et al., 2026) replaces dense
Conv3D with depthwise-separable Conv3D in low-resolution stages while retaining dense Conv3D
at higher resolutions. Flash-VAED (Zhu et al., 2026) further combines depthwise-separable Conv3D
with channel-pruned Conv2D in high-resolution stages. Flash-VAED’s stage-wise replacement ex-
periments show that substituting Conv3D with Conv2D in later, higher-resolution stages yields
greater latency reductions with smaller quality losses, providing an empirical basis for reducing
temporal computation in these stages. However, the depthwise-separable Conv3D used by both
methods constrains joint spatial filtering and channel mixing, limiting its ability to reproduce pre-
trained dense-convolution responses. Flash-VAED’s pruned Conv2D removes local temporal inter-
actions while also narrowing spatial representations, restricting the spatial mappings inherited from
the teacher. Conv2D replacement fixes the affected layers to spatial-only processing rather than pro-
viding adjustable temporal capacity. These observations motivate examining whether the spatial and
temporal contributions of pretrained Conv3D have different compression sensitivities.
Motivated by this question, we introduce LightVAE, a compression framework that combines two
complementary forms of structural compression: temporal low-rank compression (TLR) within el-
igible convolutions and width-preserving pruning across network blocks (Figure 1). TLR reduces
temporal computation within retained layers, whereas block pruning reduces network depth without
narrowing the retained feature representations. LightVAE further uses an algebraically equivalent
folded implementation for efficient TLR execution and probe-guided feature alignment for recon-
struction recovery.
TLR begins with an exact reparameterization of pretrained causal Conv3D that expresses its out-
put as an aggregate spatial response plus a temporal correction driven by historical–current feature
differences. Under the same cumulative singular-value retention criterion, our branch-specific abla-
tions show that temporal-only factorization produces smaller decoder-output errors than spatial-only
or joint factorization, while joint-factorization errors remain close to those of spatial-only factoriza-
tion. These results lead to TLRConv2D, a stateful operator that keeps the aggregate spatial kernel
dense and applies truncated SVD only to the temporal correction. Layer-specific ranks control
temporal capacity and computational cost; Figure 2 illustrates the fidelity benefit of retaining the
correction. To translate the reduced arithmetic into wall-clock gains, we derive an algebraically
equivalent folded implementation that absorbs the current-frame correction into the spatial kernel
and caches low-dimensional history projections. This implementation avoids explicit online feature
differences, reduces convolution calls and intermediate tensors without additional rank truncation,
and improves measured throughput on both evaluated GPUs.
At the depth level, high shortcut–output cosine similarity suggests potential block redundancy (Fig-
ure 9). We therefore complement TLR with width-preserving block pruning, adapting TinyFusion’s
recoverability-aware search (Fang et al., 2025) to convolutional and Transformer blocks while re-
taining required shortcuts, projections, and resampling operations. This depth reduction is comple-
mentary to TLR because it acts across blocks rather than within retained temporal convolutions. For
reconstruction recovery, uniform feature MSE weights coordinates equally without accounting for
their downstream effects. We instead introduce probe-guided feature alignment, which combines
gradients from a frozen teacher suffix with signed student–teacher feature discrepancies to construct
detached weights. These weights suppress matching directions that locally increase the probe loss
in feature space, while the probe loss itself is excluded from the recovery objective.
LightVAE-Pro applies TLR at the original decoder depth, whereas LightVAE-Lite additionally ap-
plies width-preserving block pruning using a recoverability-aware search adapted from TinyFu-
sion (Fang et al., 2025). For Transformer-based autoencoders, the framework uses block pruning
without TLR. We summarize our contributions as follows:
▶We introduce TLR, exploiting the different compression sensitivities of spatial and temporal com-
putation in pretrained causal Conv3D. TLR preserves dense spatial modeling while compressing
only the temporal component; an equivalent folded implementation reduces convolution calls
and intermediate tensors for practical acceleration.
▶We introduce a general structural compression-and-recovery framework that works for both con-
volutional and Transformer autoencoders. Its probe-guided recovery uses a frozen teacher suffix
to weight feature alignment according to how feature discrepancies affect the final reconstruc-
tion.

RELATED WORK
Efficient video autoencoders and operator design.
Video autoencoder acceleration explores
lightweight architectures (Cheng & Yuan, 2025) and compression of pretrained decoders (Zou et al.,
2026; Zhu et al., 2026), the latter preserving the latent interface of an existing diffusion backbone.
Turbo-VAED (Zou et al., 2026) uses depthwise-separable Conv3D in low-resolution stages and
dense Conv3D at higher resolutions. Flash-VAED (Zhu et al., 2026) combines depthwise-separable
Conv3D with channel-pruned Conv2D, demonstrating that later stages tolerate reduced temporal
computation. Depthwise-separable Conv3D constrains joint spatial filtering and channel mixing,
while channel-pruned Conv2D removes local temporal interactions and narrows spatial representa-
tions. Prior work also factorizes spatiotemporal convolutions (Qiu et al., 2017; Tran et al., 2018)
or applies low-rank kernel approximation (Lebedev et al., 2015). TLR instead exactly separates an
aggregate spatial response from a difference-driven temporal correction, preserving the dense spatial
kernel at conversion while controlling temporal capacity through layer-specific ranks.
Structured pruning and knowledge distillation.
Channel pruning reduces network width (He
et al., 2017), whereas block pruning reduces depth while preserving the feature widths of retained
blocks. TinyFusion (Fang et al., 2025) jointly learns structural selection and parameter adaptation
to identify diffusion Transformers with strong post-pruning recoverability. LightVAE adapts this
search principle to convolutional and Transformer blocks in video autoencoders. Reconstruction re-
covery commonly uses output or intermediate-feature distillation (Hinton et al., 2015; Romero et al.,
2015). CrossKD (Wang et al., 2024) passes student features through a teacher detection head and
directly distills the resulting cross-head predictions. Gradient-Guided Knowledge Distillation (Lan
& Tian, 2024) uses task gradients to weight task-relevant features during distillation. LightVAE in-
stead uses gradients from a frozen teacher-suffix probe, together with signed student–teacher feature
discrepancies, to construct detached feature-alignment weights. These weights suppress matching
directions that locally increase the probe loss in feature space. Unlike direct cross-head prediction
distillation, the probe loss itself is excluded from the recovery objective.

4.3
BLOCK SELECTION AND RECOVERY
Block selection.
We adapt TinyFusion’s recoverability-aware search (Fang et al., 2025) to video
VAE blocks under fixed group-wise retention quotas. Required shortcuts, projections, and resam-
pling operations are retained. Selection logits and shared LoRA parameters (Hu et al., 2022) are
trained with frozen base weights using an L1 reconstruction term and LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018).
After search, LoRA updates are merged into the retained weights before TLRConv2D initialization.
The search applies to convolutional and compatible Transformer blocks. Appendix D summarizes
the grouped search, pruning units, and export procedure.
Probe-guided recovery.
At a compatible feature boundary, a frozen teacher suffix maps the stu-
dent’s feature to a reconstruction, which is compared with the detached teacher output. Let g denote

5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
For Wan2.1/2.2 (Team Wan et al., 2025; Wan Team, 2025), Pro retains all 14 residual blocks, while
Lite keeps one residual main branch per stage (14→5). MiniMax-H3 (MiniMax, 2026) uses block
pruning only, reducing decoder Transformer blocks (36 →12) and encoder residual main branches
(12 →6). Throughput is measured on NVIDIA H100 and GeForce RTX 5090 using 81-frame
480 × 832/704 × 1280 clips for Wan2.1/Wan2.2 and 124-frame 768 × 1344 clips for MiniMax-
H3.
Reconstruction uses 33 consecutive native-resolution frames on UCF-101 (Soomro et al.,
2012) and all frames at 720 × 1280 on the DAVIS 2019 Challenge test set (Caelles et al., 2019).
Where required, last-frame repetition yields 4n + 1 inputs; repeated frames are excluded from scor-
ing. Ablations use 33-frame 480 × 832 clips unless specified otherwise. Video-level RGB PSNR
and frame-averaged SSIM (Wang et al., 2004) and LPIPS-VGG (Zhang et al., 2018; Simonyan
& Zisserman, 2015) are averaged equally across videos. For VBench-2.0 (Zheng et al., 2025),
LightX2V (LightX2V Contributors, 2025) uses the throughput-test resolutions with 81/121/124
frames for Wan2.1/Wan2.2/MiniMax-H3, respectively. Within each backbone, only the decoder
varies; prompts, seeds, and sampling settings remain fixed. Appendix F provides compression hy-
perparameters, training settings, loss coefficients, and software versions.

MAIN RESULTS
Reconstruction and generation quality.
Tables 1 and 2 summarize LightVAE’s speed–
quality trade-offs.
On Wan2.1, Lite achieves 1.12×/1.11× the throughput of Flash-VAED on
H100/RTX 5090, with higher DAVIS PSNR and lower LPIPS, while Pro closely preserves the
original reconstruction quality. On Wan2.2, Pro surpasses Turbo-VAED on all reconstruction met-
rics across both datasets while achieving higher H100 throughput (38.84 vs. 29.24 FPS). Across
the Wan VAEs, Lite reaches up to 4.06×/4.20× the original throughput on H100/RTX 5090. On
RTX 5090, Wan2.2 Lite reaches 31.87 FPS, whereas Turbo-VAED runs out of memory under our
81-frame protocol, compared with the standard 17-frame evaluation in its original paper (Zou et al.,
2026). Both LightVAE variants use r = 0 in the converted up2/up3 residual convolutions, consis-
tent with evidence that later stages tolerate reduced temporal computation (Zhu et al., 2026). The
ablations in Section 5.3 separately show that TLR gives the lowest matched-MAC error among the
tested allocations, block pruning reaches higher recovered PSNR than channel pruning at similar
pre-recovery throughput, probe-guided recovery improves over reconstruction-only recovery, and
folding increases throughput on both GPUs. On MiniMax-H3, Lite achieves 2.95×/2.97× the orig-
inal decoding throughput on H100/RTX 5090 while retaining 99.51% of the original VBench-2.0
overall score. Reference encoding is accelerated by up to 2.68×, with jointly compressed encoders
and decoders producing visually similar outputs. Appendices G.1 and G.2 provide detailed scores
and reference-conditioned comparisons.

CONCLUSION
We presented LightVAE, combining temporal low-rank compression, folded execution, width-
preserving block pruning, and probe-guided recovery. It achieves up to 2.24×/1.11× the throughput
of evaluated efficient Wan decoders on H100/RTX 5090 with higher DAVIS PSNR and lower LPIPS.
On MiniMax-H3, block pruning provides up to 2.97× decoding throughput while retaining 99.51%
of the VBench-2.0 score, and extends to efficient reference encoding.

LIMITATIONS
The convolutional evaluation of TLR currently focuses on the Wan VAE family; the MiniMax-
H3 experiments evaluate block pruning rather than TLR. Temporal ranks are selected from weight
spectra, block retention follows predefined group-wise quotas, and measured latency depends on
hardware and implementation. In addition, the probe weighting provides a local feature-space align-
ment property rather than a parameter-space descent guarantee. Future work could learn ranks and
retention counts jointly from activation and latency statistics and evaluate TLR across additional
video-autoencoder families.

At each step, execution proceeds as follows:
1. Compute Rt, Q1,t, and Q2,t using the joint Conv2D.
2. Read Q1,t−1 and Q2,t−2 according to the source temporal-padding rule.
3. Apply A to their sum and add the result to Rt.

sequence start, zero padding creates zero virtual entries. Under replicated-first-frame padding, pro-
jections of the first input frame populate the virtual history before the first output is formed.

EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
F.1
TRAINING AND RECOVERY
Search and optimization.
Block search follows TinyFusion (Fang et al., 2025), using LoRA (Hu
et al., 2022) with rL = 16 and αL = 32. Search and recovery share AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter,
2019) with (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999), zero weight decay, and a per-GPU batch size of one without
gradient accumulation. The learning rate warms up to 10−5 over 50 steps and then follows cosine
decay to 10−6. We clip the gradient norm to 1.0 and use an exponential moving average decay of
0.999.
Recovery loss coefficients.
In Equation (12), we use fixed coefficients λperc = 0.8, λadv = 0.05,
and λF = 0.01 throughout recovery. Uniform feature MSE and probe-guided alignment use the
same feature coefficient and feature boundaries. The coordinate weights wi are computed from
probe gradients and signed feature discrepancies, as defined in Appendix E.

F.2
COMPRESSION AND ABLATION SETTINGS
Model configurations.
For the Wan VAEs, LightVAE-Pro retains all 14 residual blocks, while
LightVAE-Lite retains one residual main branch per stage (14→5). Both use r = 0 in the converted
residual convolutions of up2/up3; elsewhere, TLR selects layer-specific ranks using η = 0.6.
MiniMax-H3 uses block pruning only: 36 →12 decoder Transformer blocks and 12 →6 encoder
residual main branches. Required shortcuts, projections, and resampling operations are retained.
Operator comparisons.
Operator ablations preserve the original Wan2.1 decoder depth and
width. Conv2D uses summed temporal kernel slices. The DW3D+PW/Conv2D hybrids follow
Flash-VAED’s operator layout (Zhu et al., 2026), with random-DW or teacher-SVD initialization,
excluding its channel pruning and training recipe. For teacher-SVD initialization, we apply a rank-
one SVD to each input channel’s pretrained Conv3D kernel, extending the DW+PW decomposition
of Guo et al. (2018) to three dimensions. All compressed variants use the same recovery protocol;
Figure 6 reports reconstruction quality, throughput, and peak memory.

REFERENCE-CONDITIONED GENERATION
We evaluate joint encoder–decoder compression in MiniMax-H3 reference-conditioned generation.
LightVAE-Lite reduces the encoder from 12 to 6 residual main branches and the decoder from 36
to 12 Transformer blocks, while retaining required shortcuts, projections, and resampling opera-
tions. We compare the original encoder and decoder with their compressed counterparts under five
reference-input configurations: one, three, six, or nine reference images, and nine images together
with one reference video.
Table G.2 reports encoding and decoding latency separately. Encoding latency covers visual-VAE
encoding of all reference images and the reference video when present, excluding other reference-
conditioning modules. Decoding latency covers VAE decoding of the generated latents. The original
and compressed pipelines use identical reference inputs, prompts, random seeds, diffusion back-
bones, and sampling settings. Output resolution and video duration are fixed across configurations.
Speedup is computed as the original latency divided by the corresponding LightVAE-Lite latency.
Reference encoding is accelerated by 2.12×–2.68×, and decoding by 2.68×–2.75× in this pipeline.
