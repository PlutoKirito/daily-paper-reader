ABSTRACT
Video diffusion models can reconstruct visually compelling content from severely
compressed representations at ultra-low bitrates, but they impose a rigid deploy-
ment trade-off. Bidirectional (BI) models achieve high quality through dense full-
sequence attention, yet require the entire sequence before reconstruction and incur
substantial latency. Autoregressive (AR) models enable low-delay streaming, but
their restricted temporal context reduces compression efficiency. This tension is
particularly relevant when a video stream must be decoded in real time yet is
also stored for later, quality-oriented playback, making AR and BI models natural
choices for the live and replay stages, respectively. To support both stages, we
present UniGVC, a Unified Generative Video Codec that supports two decoding
modes from a single bitstream. UniGVC-BI employs a BI-DiT with dense at-
tention for quality-oriented random-access decoding, whereas UniGVC-AR uses
windowed AR-DiT for low-delay streaming. Both paths share a hierarchical latent
codec that recurrently produces the same conditions with ultra-low bitrates, al-
lowing UniGVC-BI to serve as a conditional teacher for single-step distillation of
UniGVC-AR. A tiny conditional video decoder further reduces streaming latency
while preserving most of the reconstruction quality. On standard 1080p bench-
marks, UniGVC-BI achieves the best overall rate-distortion-perception trade-off
among the evaluated methods and produces temporally stable video at rates as low
as 0.0005 bits per pixel. Meanwhile, UniGVC-AR remains competitive while de-
coding 1080p video at 36.85 FPS, over 250× faster than UniGVC-BI. Code and
models will be released later.

INTRODUCTION
Video compression expects to preserve convincing content at rates where accurate transmission of
pixels is impossible. Traditional standards such as H.266/VVC (Bross et al., 2021), as well as
distortion-optimized neural codecs (Li et al., 2021; 2024; Jia et al., 2025), are highly effective at
removing spatial and temporal redundancy. At low bitrates, however, minimizing pixel distortion
encourages averaging over details, producing blur and erasing fine structure. Generative codecs (Qi
et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Mao et al., 2026) instead accept synthesized but visually consistent
details and target the rate-distortion-perception trade-off (Blau & Michaeli, 2019).
Video diffusion transformer (DiT) can jointly reason about appearance and motion and is conse-
quently well suited to coherent perceptual reconstruction (Peebles & Xie, 2023; Wan et al., 2025).
Nevertheless, its advantages come at a substantial systems cost: dense bidirectional attention re-
quires the whole coded segment, iterative denoising invokes the large model many times, and the
accompanying 3D VAE decoder can itself dominate latency. Such a diffusion decoder is attractive
for quality-oriented, delay-tolerant operation, but unsuitable for streaming scenarios.
Autoregressive video diffusion removes future evidence, while causal attention permits a frame or
latent chunk to be emitted as soon as its condition arrives, and cached keys and values avoid recom-
puting the past (Yin et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026b). This distinction is important in applications
such as video conference and online teaching, where the same stream must be viewed in real time

Once stored, a BI decoder can exploit both past and future context for higher-quality replay. How-
ever, existing generative video codecs typically bind the bitstream to one synthesis path.
We explore whether one compressed representation can instead support both regimes. Unlike video
generation, the diffusion now receives a strong, temporally aligned codec signal reconstructed from
the bitstream. This signal can serve as the autoregressive context. Conditioning on codec features
rather than previously reconstructed pixels or clean latents permits parallel training with a shared
diffusion timestep while retaining causal, cached inference. The same condition remains compatible
with a bidirectional generator, allowing both diffusions to optimize jointly with one shared codec.
Based on this observation, we introduce UniGVC (Fig. 1), a unified generative video compression
framework at ultra-low bitrates supporting bidirectional and autoregressive diffusion decoding in
a continuous video latent space (Wan et al., 2025). We facilitate a recurrent hierarchical codec to
compress video latents with ultra-low bitrates. The bitstream reconstructs a sequence of shared
codec conditions. UniGVC-BI processes those conditions with dense bidirectional attention and
iterative denoising, maximizing perceptual quality and temporal consistency for buffered random-
access decoding. UniGVC-AR instead uses contextual autoregression and a windowed KV cache
for low-delay causal streaming. We distill it into one step with the bidirectional teacher through
distribution matching and adversarial supervision, then replace the costly 3D video VAE decoder
with a condition-aware tiny causal decoder. Inference consequently supports switching between
generative decoders for different decoding quality and latency with the same bits.
We summarize our contributions as:
• We present a unified generative video compression framework whose single bitstream sup-
ports both a quality-oriented bidirectional decoding mode and a real-time autoregressive
decoding mode, covering random-access and low-delay use cases at ultra-low bitrates.
• We develop a hierarchical latent video codec for efficient bits allocation at severe compres-
sion. To enable autoregressive streaming, we distill AR-DiT into one step exploiting BI-
DiT as the distribution teacher under the same codec condition. A conditional tiny decoder
further breaks the real-time decoding bottleneck while maintaining most of the quality.
• Extensive experiments on video benchmarks demonstrate that UniGVC-BI achieves supe-
rior rate-distortion-perception trade-off and temporal stability at bitrates as low as 0.0005
bpp, while UniGVC-AR remains competitive and reaches 36.85 decoding FPS at 1080p.

UNIFIED GENERATIVE VIDEO COMPRESSION
2.1
HIERARCHICAL LATENT VIDEO CODEC FOR ULTRA-LOW BITRATES
UniGVC facilitates the temporal compression of the video VAE (Wan et al., 2025). Given a video
x ∈RT ×H×W , the VAE encoder maps it to a continuous latent sequence l with 16 channels, 8×
spatial downsampling, and 4× temporal downsampling (with a separately represented first frame),
making latent coding substantially cheaper than pixel-domain coding. As overviewed in Fig. 2, we
construct a recurrent codec unifying intra and inter latent coding to meet the autoregressive stream-
ing. A hierarchical entropy model is introduced to refine bits allocation under ultra-low bitrates.
Unified Recurrent Latent Coding. For latent frame lt, the latent encoder produces a primary latent
yt using the previous decoded feature f l
t−1. The latent decoder reconstructs a feature ft, updates a
recurrent memory buffer (Guo et al., 2025), and projects it to the condition ct. For the first latent
frame, temporal buffer and inputs are zero initialized and a small set of intra-specific entropy layers
is activated. This unified intra/inter design keeps every ct in the same feature space and makes joint
optimization with both diffusion decoders possible.

2018) typically consumes a considerable part of the bit budget (Fig. 2 Right). We therefore decom-

2.2
JOINT TRAINING WITH CONTEXTUAL AUTOREGRESSIVE VIDEO DIFFUSION
Preliminaries: Autoregressive Video Generation. Autoregressive (AR) video diffusion predicts
one latent frame (or chunk) at a time based on previous generation, involving three typical training
paradigms. Teacher forcing (TF) (Gao et al., 2024) trains to denoise the next frame conditioned on
a clean ground-truth prefix. It supports parallel training with a causal mask, but replaces that prefix
with imperfect generated latents at inference, causing exposure bias. Diffusion forcing (DF) (Chen
et al., 2024) assigns different noise levels to frames in a training sequence, leaving a similar train-
test gap (Huang et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2026). Self forcing (SF) (Huang et al., 2026b) closes
this gap by performing an AR rollout during training and conditioning on previously generated
clean latents, but serializes training and typically truncates gradients through the rollout. These
paradigms were adopted primarily for video generation, where generated latents are the only strong
temporal observations. It remains unclear which AR paradigm is most effective when a learned
codec condition is jointly optimized.
Autoregressive Paradigms with a Video Codec. As shown in Fig. 3, a codec permits two AR
inference pipelines. AR with generated clean latents follows conventional AR video generation and
predicts ˆlt from the current codec condition ct and previously generated clean latents ˆl<t. It cor-
responds to conditional TF or SF training. TF is parallel but train-test inconsistent, whereas SF is
consistent but requires a sequential self-rollout, making joint optimization with a recurrent codec

expensive. The second, AR with contextual latent, is specific to video compression. Because the bit-
stream provides a frame-aligned condition at every time step, the model can instead use the causal
condition sequence as history without feeding reconstructed latents back into later predictions. We
train this path using a codec-conditioned variant of DF. Since c≤t carries the temporal history rather
than a differently noised latent prefix, all target latents can share a diffusion timestep and be opti-
mized in parallel under causal attention. The conditions are decoded from the same bitstream during
training and inference, so this formulation avoids conditioning on accumulated generation errors
and allows every frame to provide gradients to the codec in parallel.
Contextual autoregression also follows established practice in neural video coding, where learned
feature context is generally more effective for inter-frame prediction than a reconstructed frame
alone (Li et al., 2021; Sheng et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023). An RGB frame has only three channels,
and a clean latent in video VAE has only 16. By contrast, codec condition ct supports much more
learned channels (e.g., 256). It is not constrained to be directly reconstructible and can preserve
diverse predictive cues about motion, structure, and color. Guided by these insights, we adopt
contextual autoregression trained with conditional DF for UniGVC-AR. Fig. 4 validates this choice
visually. At similar bitrates, DF better preserves appearance over time and exhibits less accumulated
degradation than conditional TF and SF.
Attention and Joint Objective. Both diffusion branches are initialized from the Wan2.1-1.3B video
DiT (Wan et al., 2025). BI-DiT preserves dense spatial-temporal attention. AR-DiT instead applies
a causal temporal window of w latent frames. This masked attention admits streaming inference and
bounds the KV-cache length. We use w = 3 based on the ablation in Sec. 4.3. LoRA adapters (Hu
et al., 2021) are inserted into the two DiTs to preserve the generative priors. We jointly update the
adapters and codec using flow matching loss LFM. The objective for stage 1 is
LStage1 = λ · R + λFM · LFM.

Gradients from both decoding modes consequently shape the same bitstream representation. Af-
ter training, the dense branch supports sequence-level generation from a buffered coded segment,
whereas the causal branch processes newly available ct with bounded KV memory.

3.2
PIXEL FINETUNING WITH CONDITIONAL TINY DECODER
Once AR-DiT is reduced to one step, the original video VAE decoder becomes the dominant latency
term because of its deep 3D causal convolutions and attention. We replace it in UniGVC-AR with a
lightweight tiny decoder composed of 2D causal memory blocks and PixelShuffle upsampling (Shi
et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2026a). Each block fuses its current-frame activation with a cached feature
from the preceding frame, preserving causal temporal information without 3D convolution.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
SETTINGS
Datasets. Our training corpus contains 100K 512 × 512 images from MLIC-100K (Jiang et al.,
2025), 1M 720p clips sampled from Koala-36M (Wang et al., 2025), and 11K 1080p clips of at least
97 frames from UltraVideo (Xue et al., 2025). We resize and center-crop samples to the resolution
required by each training stage. Evaluation uses the first 96 frames of the 1080p sequences in
HEVC-B (Sullivan et al., 2012), UVG (Mercat et al., 2020), and MCL-JCV (Wang et al., 2016).
Neural codecs receive RGB frames converted from YUV420 with BT.709.
Implementation. Codec initialization takes 100K steps on images, 300K steps on 29-frame clips,
and 35K steps on 97-frame clips. The batch size is 8 and clip size is 256 × 320. Starting from
λ = 0.25, we finetune ultra-low-rate operating points with λ ∈{1, 2, 4, 8, 16}. Stage 1 takes 15K
steps on 256×320 clips and 2K steps on 512×832 with a batch size of 32. Each DiT is then adapted
independently for 600 steps on 1024 × 1920 clips using only flow matching. Stage 2 runs for 600
steps on 97-frame clips, while stage 3 takes 2.6K steps on 25-frame clips, both using 1024 × 1920
clips and a batch size of 32. All models are trained in BF16 precision.
Evaluation. We compare with H.266/VVC using VTM-17.0 in low-delay (VTM-LD) and random-
access (VTM-RA) configurations (Bross et al., 2021); distortion-oriented neural codecs DCVC-
FM (Li et al., 2024) and DCVC-RT (Jia et al., 2025); and generative codecs GLC-Video (Qi et al.,
2025), GLVC (Guo et al., 2025), GNVC-VD (Mao et al., 2026), GVC1D (Zheng et al., 2026), Free-
GVC (Ling et al., 2026), and S2VC (Xue et al., 2026b). We measure distortion with PSNR and
MS-SSIM (Wang et al., 2003), perceptual fidelity with LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), DISTS (Ding
et al., 2020), FID (Heusel et al., 2017) and KID (Bi´nkowski et al., 2018). Temporal behavior is

RELATED WORK
5.1
GENERATIVE VIDEO COMPRESSION
Early generative codecs introduced adversarial losses into image and video compression (Agustsson
et al., 2019; Mentzer et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022). Inspired by image codecs (Jia et al., 2024; Xue
et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2025; 2026), recent video methods witnessed a growing trend on exploit-
ing pretrained generative latent spaces or diffusion priors. GLC-Video compresses discrete genera-
tive latents (Qi et al., 2025), whereas GLVC uses a continuous video tokenizer and recurrent latent
coding (Guo et al., 2025). DiffVC applies image-diffusion for perceptual enhancement (Ma & Chen,
2025). Concurrent designs explore compact one-dimensional latents (Zheng et al., 2026) or a single-
step image diffusion decoder (Xue et al., 2026b). Video diffusion models display strong potential in
alleviating temporal flickering. To this end, GNVC-VD performs sequence-level latent refinement
with a video DiT (Mao et al., 2026); and Free-GVC codes trajectories of a pretrained video diffu-
sion model without task-specific training (Ling et al., 2026). These approaches advance perceptual
quality, but expose only one fixed decoding trade-off. UniGVC instead transmits a decoder-agnostic
condition that can drive either a multi-step bidirectional generator or a one-step causal generator.
5.2
AUTOREGRESSIVE VIDEO DIFFUSION AND ACCELERATION
Standard video diffusions use dense bidirectional attention and iterative sampling, which provide
strong sequence-level coherence but prevent online decoding. CausVid converts a bidirectional
DiT into a few-step causal generator and applies asymmetric distribution-matching distillation
(DMD) (Yin et al., 2025; 2024b;a). Self Forcing addresses exposure bias by training on autore-
gressively generated histories (Huang et al., 2026b), while Causal Forcing studies the architectural
mismatch between bidirectional teachers and causal students (Zhu et al., 2026). Adversarial post-
training provides another route to one-step video synthesis (Lin et al., 2025). These advances target
open-ended generation, where past generated frames are the principal temporal condition and train-
ing must carefully reproduce their inference-time distribution.
6
CONCLUSION
This work presented UniGVC, a unified generative video compression framework that decouples
the transmitted representation from the receiver’s quality-latency choice for ultra-low-rate scenar-
ios. A hierarchical latent codec produces one bitstream and one sequence of compression-aware
conditions, driving either a multi-step bidirectional DiT for high-quality random-access decoding or
a distilled one-step autoregressive DiT with a causal tiny decoder for low-delay streaming. Context-
based autoregression enables parallel, train-test-consistent optimization, while adversarial distribu-
tion matching and pixel fine-tuning preserve much of the quality after acceleration. Across 1080p
benchmarks, UniGVC-BI provides the strongest overall rate-distortion-perception trade-off among
the evaluated methods, and UniGVC-AR reaches 36.85 FPS with only a small perceptual gap.
Limitations. Although UniGVC avoids storing or transmitting duplicate bitstreams, the retaining
two decoding parameter sets increases the storage of decoder-side model. In addition, as with gen-
erative codecs, plausible detail may not reproduce the source exactly at extreme rates. Applications
requiring scientific fidelity should avoid aggressive operating points or transmit additional residual
information. Future work includes shared-weight multi-mode DiTs and region enhancement.

Test data. The source sequences of HEVC-B (Sullivan et al., 2012), UVG (Mercat et al., 2020),

convert the source frames to RGB using the BT.709 conversion matrix before encoding. The decoded
outputs of traditional codecs are converted to RGB with the same procedure, and all objective metrics
are computed between RGB reference and reconstruction frames.
Traditional codec.
We evaluate H.266/VVC (Bross et al., 2021) with the VTM-17.0 refer-
ence software. VTM-LD uses the Low-Delay-B configuration encoder lowdelay vtm.cfg
with QP ∈{37, 40, 43, 46, 49, 52, 55}. VTM-RA uses encoder randomaccess vtm.cfg

YUV 4:2:0 format; only the decoded sequence used for metric computation is converted to RGB.
Neural codecs. For the distortion-oriented baselines DCVC-FM (Li et al., 2024) and DCVC-RT (Jia
et al., 2025), and for GLC-Video (Qi et al., 2025), we run the official code and released checkpoints
locally. All frames are processed in RGB. The code or checkpoints of GLVC (Guo et al., 2025),
GNVC-VD (Mao et al., 2026), GVC1D (Zheng et al., 2026), Free-GVC (Ling et al., 2026), and
S2VC (Xue et al., 2026b) were not publicly available when our evaluation was completed. We
therefore use rate-distortion-perception points provided directly by the authors for GLVC, GVC1D,
and S2VC, and the available results reported in the papers for GNVC-VD and Free-GVC.
Metrics. We evaluate PSNR, MS-SSIM (Wang et al., 2003), and DISTS (Ding et al., 2020) with
PyIQA under its default settings.
LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), FID (Heusel et al., 2017) and
KID (Bi´nkowski et al., 2018) are implemented with TorchMetrics. Since benchmarks contain rel-
atively few high-resolution frames, FID and KID are computed from overlapping 256 × 256 RGB

for the flow warping error E∗
warp (Lai et al., 2018) and DOVER (Wu et al., 2023). Lower LPIPS,
DISTS, FID, KID, and E∗

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS OF UNIGVC
B.1
CODEC DESIGN
Table 3 summarizes the primary codec modules. Spatial resolutions are measured relative to the
input video: the Wan VAE first maps a frame to 1/8 spatial resolution, the primary latent yt is
represented at 1/16, and the hierarchical hyperlatents (rt, zt) are represented at 1/64. The primary
latent contains 256 channels. The hyper-analysis output contains 128 channels and is split into a
16-channel residual branch rt and a 112-channel context-modeled branch zt.
The codec is built mainly from depthwise convolution (DC) blocks (Li et al., 2026). Each block
contains a residual 3 × 3 depthwise convolution followed by two 1 × 1 pointwise projections and a
weighted SiLU activation; a second residual feed-forward branch expands the feature width by four.
Downsampling uses PixelUnshuffle followed by a 1 × 1 projection, while upsampling uses a 1 × 1
projection followed by PixelShuffle. The latent analysis and synthesis transforms share their main
parameters between intra and inter latent frames. Only entropy parameters are specialized for the
first intra latent frame, and the rest inter latent frames.

B.2
TRAINING PROCEDURE
Table 4 details the complete training curriculum. All resources are rented individually.
Codec initialization. We first train the codec independently of the diffusion models. The cur-
riculum uses (1, 29, 97, 97, 97) frames for (100K, 300K, 20K, 10K, 5K) steps, with learning rates
(10−4, 10−4, 5×10−5, 2×10−5, 10−5). The image stage uses 512 × 512 crops; all video stages use
256 × 320 crops and batch size 8. We first obtain an initialization with λ = 0.25 in Eq. 3, then
derive the ultra-low-rate models using λ ∈{1, 2, 4, 8, 16} and additional 97-frame training. The
hierarchical weights (Guo et al., 2025) for the first four VAE latent frames are (12.0, 1.2, 0.5, 0.9);
subsequent frames alternate between 0.5 and 0.9.
Stage 1: joint codec–diffusion training. Each rate point resumes from its codec checkpoint. We
insert rank-32 LoRA modules into BI-DiT and AR-DiT. For the first 15K steps on 97-frame 256 ×
320 clips, the codec is frozen and only the LoRAs are optimized. We then jointly optimize the
codec and both LoRAs for 5K steps at 256 × 320 and 2K steps at 512 × 832, using batch size
32. Independent LoRA adaptation uses learning rate 10−4, while joint optimization uses 10−5. We
set λFM = 0.35, the flow-time shift to 5.0, and classifier-free condition dropout to 0.1. The rate
weight of the first latent frame is multiplied by 0.1 during joint training to preserve a stronger causal
anchor. Finally, with the codec fixed, we adapt the two LoRAs independently for 600 steps on
97-frame 1024 × 1920 clips using only flow matching. UniGVC-BI is fixed after this stage.
Stage 2: adversarial distribution matching. We merge the stage-1 AR-DiT LoRA into its back-
bone and optimize the full one-step generator, online fake-score model, and discriminator head for
600 steps using 97-frame 1024 × 1920 clips and batch size 32. The codec and real-score BI-DiT
remain frozen. We use learning rate 10−5 and λlatent
GAN = 0.05. The discriminator pools fake-score
features after one-third, two-thirds, and all of the DiT depth. We update the discriminator head
and fake-score model five times for every generator update; adversarial gradients are stopped at
the fake-score backbone during discriminator optimization, while the fake-score backbone itself is
trained with standard flow matching to model the distribution of the one-step generator.
Stage 3: pixel fine-tuning. Pixel training uses 25-frame 1024 × 1920 clips, batch size 32, and 2.6K
steps. The four phases contain (1000, 1000, 500, 100) steps with learning rates (10−4, 5×10−5, 2×
10−5, 10−5). We train the entire tiny decoder and new rank-8 LoRA modules inserted into the one-
step AR-DiT, while keeping the codec fixed. The DINOv3 discriminator input is resized to 448 to
control memory consumption and follows the discriminator design of Zhang et al. (2026). In Eq. 9,
(λMSE, λp, λpixel
GAN, λwarp) = (0.5, 2.0, 0.1, 1.0); LPIPS-VGG and DISTS contribute equally within
the perceptual loss Lp.

D
TEXTUAL PROMPT
D.1
VIDEO CAPTION
The pretrained Wan DiT is text-conditioned. At the encoder, we therefore use Qwen3.5-397B-A17B
to generate one sequence-specific caption for each 96-frame test video. This caption is transmitted
as a losslessly compressed UTF-8 string and recovered exactly at the decoder. Both UniGVC modes
concatenate it with the same fixed general quality prompt. The frozen Wan T5 encoder then maps
the complete prompt to at most 512 text tokens; only the sequence-specific text, rather than its
embedding tensor, is signaled in the bitstream.
We use the following captioning instruction:
“Watch the video carefully and write one detailed, factual paragraph of 80–150 words.
When applicable, describe (i) the setting and location, (ii) the main subjects and their ap-
pearance, (iii) actions, motion, and event progression, (iv) camera movement and framing,
and (v) lighting, color tone, and atmosphere. Do not add a preamble, bullet points, head-
ings, or quotation marks. Be objective, specific, and dense in detail, and avoid vague filler.”
This instruction encourages the sequence caption to describe information useful for conditional re-
construction rather than subjective interpretation. We use the following 24-word general quality
prompt for every sequence:
“Photorealistic video, realistic motion, natural environmental dynamics, physically plausi-
ble interactions, consistent temporal behavior, cinematic composition, documentary-style
realism, high visual fidelity, fine-grained details, 4K quality.”
Because this general prompt is fixed and shared by all videos, it is stored as part of the codec
configuration and incurs no per-sequence rate. Table 7 summarizes the sequence-specific captions
used in our evaluation. They contain 4.7-5.3 sentences and 84.8-124.3 words on average. After
concatenating the fixed prompt, the complete prompts contain 108.8, 136.4, and 148.3 words on
HEVC-B, UVG, and MCL-JCV, respectively. The transmitted sequence-caption strings average
517.8-759.6 bytes before lossless compression.

D.3
DISCUSSION ON PROMPT
To isolate the value of sequence-specific semantic side information, we compare the complete
prompt against the fixed general quality prompt alone while keeping the codec and decoder un-
changed. Table 9 reports BD-rate relative to the corresponding general-only baseline on HEVC-
B. Adding the sequence caption improves all four perceptual criteria for both decoding modes.
UniGVC-BI reduces BD-rate by 1.88% in LPIPS, 11.19% in DISTS, 11.06% in FID, and 17.11%
in KID. UniGVC-AR yields corresponding reductions of 2.64%, 6.04%, 14.29%, and 16.11%. The
consistent improvements for both decoders indicate that the caption complements the shared codec
representation rather than benefiting only one synthesis path. The larger gains in FID and KID than
in LPIPS further suggest that sequence-level text primarily helps resolve semantic and distributional
ambiguity at these extremely low rates.
Fig. 10 visualize the influence. With only the fixed general prompt, the decoder is informed of the
desired visual style but receives no textual description of this particular scene. The sequence caption
supplies the setting, subjects, motion, and camera behavior that are difficult to communicate through
a 0.00052-bpp visual code alone. Adding it produces a reconstruction that more faithfully reflects
the source content, while its compressed rate is only 6.7 × 10−6 bpp for this example. Together
