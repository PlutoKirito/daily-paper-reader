ABSTRACT 

Quantization-aware training (QAT) leverages lower-precision arithmetic to reduce the cost of LLM deployment, but aggressive quantization degrades final model performance. A common remedy is mixed-precision training, in which high pre-cision is assigned to some of the layers to maintain performance while keeping the cost constrained. This approach then requires precision assignments for model layers during training. We provide a new approach, called Q-PACE, consisting of a second-order sensitivity model that predicts the loss increase as a sum of quantiza-tion noise MSE weighted by per-layer curvature coefficients. During training, we periodically re-compute these coefficients using perturbations across layers, and re-assign precision. Pretraining and supervised fine-tuning experiments on LLMs of up to 4B parameters show that Q-PACE consistently improves over existing mixed-precision training recipes, and achieves comparable loss at substantially lower total memory budgets. We further find that quantization sensitivity is highly predictable by depth and layer type, and its stability during training allows for infrequent, cheap recalibration. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Quantization-aware training (QAT) methods (Courbariaux et al., 2016; Jacob et al., 2018) use low-precision arithmetic to lower the cost of both deploying LLMs, as they result in lower-bitwidth accurate models, and training them, as the matrix multiplications during training can be mapped to lower precision. Recent recipes make end-to-end NVFP4 and MXFP4 pretraining viable by leveraging techniques such as block (micro) scaling, stochastic rounding, rotations, and unbiased gradient estimators (Castro et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; NVIDIA et al., 2026). However, there is still a clear loss gap between lower-precision approaches and BF16 and MXFP8 training, and uniform four-bit training can be lossy or even become unstable over long horizons (NVIDIA et al., 2026). It is known that not every network layer needs the same precision, e.g. Malinovskii et al. (2025). Moreover, modern microscaling formats allow matrix products across various precisions, or even mixed-precision operands (Rouhani et al., 2023), so the bitwidth of each linear layer can be chosen separately, assuming a global budget constraint. The key question, which we consider here, is how to choose the layer-wise bitwidths that maximize accuracy while satisfying a precision budget. Current approaches either keep a fixed set of layers, typically the last few, in higher precision (NVIDIA et al., 2026), or rank layers by a local proxy, such as the product of gradient norm and quantization error (Pan et al., 2026). It is not clear whether these heuristics can be improved. In this paper, we show that the second-order loss model as a sum of per-layer errors weighted by sensitivity coefficients accurately predicts loss increase due to quantization during training (Sec. 3). This gives a training-time analogue of the “linearity theorem” model of Malinovskii et al. (2025), previously used for one-shot, post-training quantization.

We call our approach Q-PACE , Precision Allocation via Curvature Estimation for Quantization-aware training, and apply it at training iterates. Estimated per-layer sensitivity coefficients depend only on the model weights and are reasonably stable across iterations, so Q-PACE re-estimates them periodically during training: once they are determined, we solve a knapsack problem to determine “optimal” per-layer bit-widths. This approach only requires forward passes for coefficient estimation, and has negligible memory overhead and marginal computational overhead. We evaluate Q-PACE on pretraining experiments for models between 350M and 4B parameters, and on supervised fine-tuning of Qwen3 models, against state-of-the-art baselines (NVIDIA et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026), at budgets between 5.0 and 6.5 bits per weight. Our findings are as follows: • Q-PACE consistently improves over existing mixed-precision training strategies across model scales, precision budgets, and format options. In pretraining, it achieves the lowest final loss in every setting we evaluate, recovering up to 81% of the loss gap between uniform NVFP4 and MXFP8 training; the same trend holds for supervised fine-tuning. • Q-PACE provides a substantially better quality–precision trade-off. At a fixed precision budget, Q-PACE produces better final loss than both SNIP and the NVIDIA heuristic, while at matched loss it requires fewer bits per model parameter (Figure 1). • A detailed analysis of the produced allocations shows that quantization sensitivity is highly structured across the network: it depends on both layer type and depth, and its profile evolves over the course of training. However, these changes occur much slower than parameter updates. As a result, only a small number of reallocations (on the order of 10 over a full training run) is sufficient, keeping the overhead of Q-PACE low in practice. All in all, our results show that layer-wise precision can be assigned from a sensitivity model estimated using only forward passes, rather than from more complex error proxies. Doing so can recover a large fraction of the accuracy gap to uniform higher-precision training, at a small fraction of the training cost.

Our goal is to choose the quantization levels of n layers in a network during quantization-aware training. We concentrate on linear layers, i.e. fully-connected layers or attention projections. For each of the full-precision weight tensors W = ( Wi)ni=1 , we want to select a corresponding bitwidth ,

bi, from a set of candidate bitwidths, denoted as β.We use the term bitwidth as it is common in the literature. Technically, the selection is among formats

We denote the quantization of a tensor Wi by Q(Wi, b i). Any choice of quantization bitwidths will influence the model’s overall quality, i.e. the achieved loss , as well as its resource cost , such as storage and compute. Bitwidth assignment. Once we have determined sensitivities αi for all i = 1 , . . . , n , we can solve Eq. (1) with Eq. (5) as a proxy for the intractable L(W, B ). As a discrete selection problem with linear objective and linear constraints, this constitutes a multiple choice knapsack problem (MCKP) .For our implementation we use pseudo-polynomial dynamic programming (Pisinger, 1995): the budget C0 is discretized into M bins, and each layer cost dibi is rounded up to the whole number of bins. The discrete problem is then solved via a dynamic program that tracks an allocation that gives minimal loss degradation for each cost level. The time complexity of the algorithm is O(nM |β|),and in practice, for the models used, we measure ≲ 1 s of knapsack solving time, which is negligible compared to multiple forward passes needed for estimating αi.4Preprint 

Q-PACE. Ideally, the estimates αi and [MSE should stay up-to-date at any time during training. However, given that they require multiple forward passes to be updated, it would be unreasonable to change them at every optimization step. In practice, we observed that sensitivities demonstrate a stable pattern during training and infrequent re-allocations suffice. Therefore, we propose to update the estimates only at regular intervals of τ steps. In our experiments, re-allocation periods lower than 5B training tokens did not yield measurable improvement. Summarizing the discussion above, we obtain the Q-PACE procedure which is our main contribution in this work, see Algorithm 1 for the pseudo-code. 

Computational and memory overhead. Q-PACE calibration requires only forward passes. To perturb layer i we add δW i to the weights and evaluate the loss on D. The only extra memory is a single noise tensor the size of the largest layer, which is negligible compared to the training state. In contrast, gradient-based methods (Pan et al., 2026) require storing the activations and gradients needed for the backward pass, resulting in a substantially larger memory overhead. One recalibration costs mn + 1 forward passes over D: one unperturbed pass for Lhigh and one per (layer, noise level) pair. It is followed by a knapsack solve with negligible cost. 4 EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We train a decoder-only Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), with each block following an OLMo-2-style (OLMo: et al., 2025): multi-head self-attention and a two-layer MLP, with RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) applied to each sub-layer output before the residual. Attention uses rotary position embeddings (Su et al., 2023), and Qwen3-style (Yang et al., 2025) QK-RMSNorm. We use model sizes of 350M, 500M, 800M, and 4B. We start our experiments with a 350M model on 18B tokens and evaluate all the strategies, then select the best-performing ones for larger up to 4B model runs. The training data is ClimbMix dataset (Diao et al., 2025). We use a subset pre-tokenized with TokenMonster (vocab size 32 000). We used AdamW (Kingma & Ba, 2017; Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) and Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) optimizers. The learning rate (LR) schedule used is WSD (Hu et al., 2024). The optimal LR is found via a grid search. Context length range is from 1024 for 350M model to 4096 for 4B model, more details are provided in App. D. 

Quantization-aware training. Linear layers in the blocks (Q, K, V, O, and MLP projections) are the only quantized modules; embeddings and the LM head remain in high precision. We follow the e2e QAT setup by Panferov et al. (2026), which performs all of the weights-activations matmul and gradients-weight/activations matmuls in FP4. In our experiments, the candidate low precision formats for matmix multiplications are NVFP4, MXFP6, MXFP8. For mixed-precision runs, a global bitwidth budget is defined as the total memory cost of the quantized weights divided by the number of quantized parameters, i.e., bits per parameter. Unless stated otherwise, all matmuls for a layer, forward and backward, are performed in the same precision. The weight master copy and optimizer state are stored in FP32. 

Baselines. We compare Q-PACE against uniform NVFP4, MXFP8, the last-layers strategy of NVIDIA et al. (2026), and SNIP (Pan et al., 2026). NVIDIA keeps the last layers of the model at high precision, with the cutoff chosen to satisfy the budget. SNIP solves a similar knapsack problem, but estimates layer costs using gradient-based forward sensitivity and backward-update divergence. We also consider a version of SNIP, named SNIP weight-gradient-only, as it performs better than the original SNIP recipe in pretraining. Next, we study the trade-off between model quality and precision budget. Figure 3 shows test loss improvement over the FP4 baseline 800M model across bitwidths. For both AdamW and Muon, Q-PACE forms an empirical Pareto frontier: at a fixed precision budget, it achieves a larger loss improvement than the NVIDIA, while at a matched loss it requires fewer bits per weight. For Adam, our method outperforms NVIDIA strategy while using 1 bit per parameter less, that is 16% less model size in memory. With Muon, the corresponding saving is approximately 0.5 bits per weight. Altogether, this indicates that the benefit of curvature-aware sensitivity is not restricted to a single precision budget or optimizer. Since a model can be deployed under various weight and activation formats during inference, we additionally measure the loss–memory trade-off for different inference formats . 5 ANALYSIS 

Next we analyze the properties and evolution of bitwidth allocations produced by our method. 

Sensitivity is structured by layer type and depth. Figure 6a shows the final precision allocation produced by Q-PACE and SNIP for the 4B model. The allocation is highly non-uniform: the assigned precision depends both on the layer type and its position in the model. The depth dependence is particularly pronounced in the final block, where Q-PACE promotes five of the six quantized linear layers to the full precision. This observation helps to explain why the NVIDIA heuristic is effective relative to uniform FP4 training: later layers are indeed highly sensitive to quantization. However, sensitivity is not solely a function of depth. Layers of different types within the one block receive different precisions, while sensitive layers can also occur substantially earlier in the network. In particular, K and V projection layers receive a substantial fraction of the higher-precision budget under both Q-PACE and SNIP. At the same time, SNIP does not promote MLP projections at all, while Q-PACE assigns high precision to MLP projections toward the end of the model. 

Sensitivity evolves during training. Figure 6b shows the fraction of each layer type assigned to BF16 throughout training. While the number of layers of each type, selected by SNIP, remains nearly constant over time, the allocation produced by Q-PACE evolves. The largest changes occur between the early and middle stages of training, after which the allocation becomes substantially more stable. K and V projections remain repeatedly promoted to BF16, while the share of promoted Q projections decreases over time, and the share of MLP grows. Therefore, the sensitivity pattern has both a constant factor, determined by layer type and block number, as well as a time-dependent factor. The former explains why simple static heuristics can provide some benefit, whereas the latter motivates periodic reallocation during training. Additional analysis results can be found in App. H

6 RELATED WORK 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) (Dettmers et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2023; Frantar et al., 2023) has become a standard approach for reducing the memory and inference cost of LLMs. Most PTQ methods apply the same bitwidth to every layer. 

Non-uniform compression. A parallel line of work distributes compression non-uniformly across layers. AMC (He et al., 2018) and HAQ (Wang et al., 2019) used reinforcement learning with hardware feedback for this, while DNAS (Wu et al., 2018) relaxed the search into a differentiable one. HAWQ (Dong et al., 2019; 2020; Yao et al., 2021) instead scores layers by second-order information, and selects bitwidths by integer programming. SPDY (Frantar & Alistarh, 2022) and OBC (Frantar et al., 2022) cast the allocation as a dynamic program over per-layer compression levels. EvoPress (Sieberling et al., 2025) showed that such additive (“error-monotone”) models can fail, and replaced them with an evolutionary search. For LLMs, mixed-precision schemes range from keeping salient weights or groups in higher precision (Dettmers et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025), to layer-wise allocation driven by per-layer sensitivity estimates and knapsack solvers (Malinovskii et al., 2025; Helcig et al., 2026), or even more complex manifold optimization (Helcig & Alistarh, 2026). Other work accounts for interactions between layers (Schaefer et al., 2023) or for perturbations (Hu et al., 2025). The closest work to ours is HIGGS (Malinovskii et al., 2025), whose linearity theorem underlies our sensitivity model. Q-PACE differs from it in three main ways: 1. HIGGS allocates bitwidths once; we allocate during training; 2. HIGGS compresses only the weights, whereas we compress weights, activations, and gradient multiplications. 3., We investigate hardware-supported microscaling formats (NVFP4, MXFP6, MXFP8, BF16) and use an analytic 4−b distortion model, so no per-layer, per-format errors are needed. 

Mixed precision during training. Non-uniform precision during training has received less attention. Low-precision training recipes typically keep a hand-picked set of components in higher precision, e.g. DeepSeek-V3 keeps the embeddings, output head, MoE gating, normalization, and attention in higher precision (DeepSeek-AI, 2024), and NVFP4 pretraining keeps the last blocks in BF16 (NVIDIA et al., 2026). Earlier work on convolutional networks learned per-layer bitwidths jointly with the weights during QAT (Wu et al., 2018; Uhlich et al., 2020; van Baalen et al., 2020). However, these methods target the precision of the final inference model whereas we periodically re-allocate precision during training under a fixed global budget. The work closest to ours is SNIP (Pan et al., 2026), which periodically re-assigns per-layer precision during LLM pretraining by solving a knapsack problem. Its scores combine a first-order Taylor estimate of the forward loss change with an optimizer-dependent estimate of the divergence in the weight updates. Q-PACE replaces these with a second-order, loss-based sensitivity model, estimated from forward passes alone. We show that this yields substantially better accuracy. 9Preprint 

7 CONCLUSION 

We propose Q-PACE, a new method for adaptive mixed-precision LLM training, which probes linear layers and estimates their sensitivity. This information is then used for periodic reassignment of precision across layers during training. With no memory overhead, our method consistently improves over existing baselines and allows for substantial memory savings at a matched performance. Across pretraining experiments from 350M to 4B parameters and supervised fine-tuning of Qwen3-4B, it outperforms other methods and recovers up to 81% of the NVFP4-to-MXFP8 loss gap at 6.5 bit/parameter. Our study is limited to a restricted range of model scales and precision formats, and primarily considers settings where the same layer-wise precision is used in the forward and backward passes. Extending dynamic allocation to larger models, broader format choices, and independently optimized forward and backward precisions are promising directions for future work. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

D TRAINING SETUP 

Pretraining. The pretraining process employs a 32,000-token TokenMonster vocabulary, untied embeddings (tied in the 800M model), and Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) with θ = 10 4.Following RoPE, queries and keys are normalized, and a learned temperature parameter is applied to each attention head. Each attention and multilayer perceptron (MLP) branch undergoes RMS normalization prior to residual addition, and a final RMSNorm is applied before the output head. Table 6 provides a summary of each model training configuration. The 350M, 500M, and 4B models are trained with Muon on the weight matrices of the transformer blocks and AdamW on all other parameters. The Muon optimizer utilizes a momentum of 0.95 and scales each orthogonalized m × n

update by 0.2pmax( m, n ). The AdamW optimizer employs β = (0 .9, 0.95) and ϵ = 10 −6 (Jordan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a). The 800M model is trained with AdamW alone, with β = (0 .9, 0.95) ,

ϵ = 10 −8, and a weight decay of 0.1 on every parameter, including the embeddings. 

Supervised-Fine-tuning (SFT). Qwen3-4B-Base (Yang et al., 2025), which includes 252 switchable projections, is fine-tuned on 10% of the Tülu-3 dataset (Lambert et al., 2025) using TRL (von Werra et al., 2020). The Fused AdamW optimizer is configured with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −6, no weight decay, a linear-decay learning-rate scheduler with 3% warmup, and gradient clipping set to 1.0. Each epoch consists of 706 steps, with a global batch size of 128, a maximum sequence length of 4096, and training conducted in BF16 precision.
