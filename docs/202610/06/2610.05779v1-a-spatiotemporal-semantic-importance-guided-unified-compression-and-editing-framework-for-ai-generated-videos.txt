# A Spatiotemporal Semantic Importance-Guided Unified Compression and Editing Framework for AI-Generated Videos 

Xihua Sheng, Member, IEEE , Dong Liu, Senior Member, IEEE , Chang Wen Chen, Life Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —AI-generated videos are rapidly increasing in vol-ume, duration, and resolution, creating growing demands for efficient storage and transmission. Unlike natural videos captured from the physical world, AI-generated videos are samples from a learned generative distribution, where semantic structures are critical to content consistency, while many local textures and stochastic details can be plausibly regenerated. This distinction suggests that compression should preserve semantically impor-tant spatiotemporal information rather than reconstruct every pixel of a particular generative sample. Beyond reconstruction, AI-generated videos also create a practical need for prompt-based editing, where users expect to modify generated content while preserving its original spatiotemporal semantics. Motivated by these observations, we propose a unified compression and editing framework for AI-generated videos that incorporates a frozen video generator as a reusable generative prior. Within this framework, we design three spatiotemporal semantic importance-guided techniques that respectively address what to transmit, how much to transmit, and how to use the transmitted side information. First, an innovation selection method projects the latent discrepancy using spatiotemporal semantic importance, so that the selected innovations prioritize semantic invariants over replaceable generative variations. Second, a frame-adaptive bit allocation method estimates the nonuniform semantic demands of latent frames and allocates more innovations to frames requiring stronger semantic preservation. Third, a unified reconstruction and editing method continuously adjusts the influence of the transmitted side information, enabling the same compressed rep-resentation to provide strong guidance for faithful reconstruction or serve as a flexible semantic anchor for structure-preserving prompt-driven editing. Extensive experiments on videos gener-ated by multiple video generation models show that our codec achieves superior rate-perception performance over traditional, neural, and generative video codecs, while enabling structure-preserving prompt-based editing from the same bitstream. 

Index Terms —AI-generated videos, spatiotemporal semantic importance, video coding, video editing.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) video generation is emerging as a new paradigm for content creation. It is becoming increasingly popular across a wide range of applications such as short-form video creation and advertising. substantial storage and transmission demands. This raises a direct but underexplored problem: how should AI-generated videos be compressed? 

Existing video coding methods [1]–[3] mostly assume that videos are natural signals captured by cameras. A natural video records the physical world: object boundaries, textures, motion trajectories, and illumination changes all come from a real scene, and the goal of compression is to preserve this external signal under a bitrate constraint. AI-generated videos are fundamentally different. They are not measurements of a real scene, but samples drawn from the distribution learned by a generative model [4]. In such videos, identity, layout, object relations, and motion trends usually define the spatiotemporal semantics of the content, while many local textures, small ap-pearance changes, and stochastic details are only one possible realization of the generation process. In other words, not all pixel-level differences in an AI-generated video are equally meaningful: some differences break identity, silhouette, or motion consistency, while others can be regenerated differently and still remain plausible. Directly compressing AI-generated videos as natural videos therefore ignores the statistical origin and semantic structure of generated content. This distinction changes the coding objective. For AI-generated videos, the goal should not be to reconstruct every pixel, but to preserve the information that determines semantic consistency. Beyond reconstruction, this semantic view also changes how a compressed bitstream can be used at the receiver side. In conventional video coding, the bitstream is primarily designed for decoded playback. When further modifications are desired, such as transferring the style, changing object attributes, removing the watermarks of the generated video while preserving its original structure and motion, a separate editing model is commonly applied to the reconstructed video. Such a model needs to analyze the video again, for example by encoding it into a latent space to extract the structural and motion information required for prompt-based editing [5]–[9]. However, this spatiotemporal semantic information has already been conveyed by the compressed bitstream. Re-extracting it from the reconstructed video therefore introduces redundant processing and additional computational complexity. In this setting, the compressed bitstream should not merely describe a one-time reconstruction. It should also carry reusable semantic constraints that support controlled editing. Video generation models [10]–[12] provide a new opportu-nity to realize these goals. A video generator [13]–[15] has learned to transform noisy latent variables into video content arXiv:2610.05779v1 [cs.CV] 5 Oct 2026 JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 2

with plausible appearance, structure, and motion. When a frozen video generator is integrated into a codec, it can serve as a reusable video prior: the generator restores replaceable details, while the bitstream only needs to transmit compact side information that constrains the generation process toward the source video. In this sense, compression no longer has to independently describe the complete video signal. Meanwhile, because the generator is prompt-conditioned, the same side information can be combined with a user-defined prompt to produce an edited video that follows the editing inten-tion while preserving the source spatiotemporal semantics. This forms a unified AI-generated video coding framework, where one compressed representation supports both high-quality reconstruction and prompt-based editing, without train-ing a new compression model or introducing a separate post-reconstruction editing model. However, turning this opportunity into a practical codec faces three key problems. First, side-information selection needs to account for spatiotemporal semantic importance. If information is selected only according to latent errors, the bitstream may spend bits on replaceable stochastic textures in-stead of protecting the semantic structures that determine con-tent consistency. Second, the side-information budget needs to reflect the nonuniform spatiotemporal semantic importance of latent frames. Uniform allocation may waste bits on se-mantically stable frames with substantial temporal redundancy, while providing insufficient information for frames that re-quire stronger semantic preservation. Third, reconstruction and editing impose different requirements on the same side in-formation. Reconstruction needs strong constraints to recover the source video, while editing needs sufficient flexibility to follow a new prompt. Overly rigid constraints suppress editing, whereas overly weak constraints cause structural drift. Therefore, the codec needs a controllable mechanism for using the same bitstream under different reconstruction and editing requirements. To address these problems, we propose a spatiotemporal semantic importance-guided unified compression and editing framework for AI-generated videos. First, we propose a spa-tiotemporal semantic importance-guided innovation selection method to determine what information should be transmitted. It projects the latent discrepancy according to spatiotemporal semantic importance and selects innovations that prioritize semantic invariants over replaceable generative variations. Second, we propose a spatiotemporal semantic importance-guided bit allocation method to determine how much infor-mation should be assigned to each latent frame. It evaluates frame-level semantic demand and allocates more innovations to frames requiring stronger semantic preservation. Third, we propose a spatiotemporal semantic importance-constrained unified reconstruction and editing method to determine how the transmitted side information should be used at the re-ceiver. It continuously adjusts the influence of the bitstream constraint, allowing the same compressed representation to provide strong guidance for source reconstruction or serve as a flexible semantic anchor for prompt-driven editing. To summarize, our main contributions are threefold:  

> •

We propose, to the best of our knowledge, the first unified compression and editing framework specifically designed for AI-generated videos, where one compact bitstream supports both high-quality reconstruction and structure-preserving prompt-based editing.  

> •

We propose three spatiotemporal semantic importance-guided techniques: an innovation selection method that prioritizes semantically critical latent information, aframe-adaptive bit allocation method that adapts the in-novation budget to frame-level semantic demand, and a unified reconstruction and editing method that bal-ances transmitted spatiotemporal semantics with prompt-induced editing semantics.  

> •

Extensive experiments on AI-generated videos from mul-tiple generation models demonstrate that our framework achieves superior rate-perception performance against tra-ditional, neural, and generative video codecs, while fur-ther enabling structure-preserving prompt-based editing from the same compressed bitstream. A. Generative Video Compression 

Generative video compression reconstructs perceptually re-alistic videos at low bitrates by incorporating generative priors into the codec. Existing work can be grouped into generative adversarial network (GAN) [16]-based, vector-quantization-based, visual autoregressive-based, diffusion-based, and training-free methods. GAN-based methods introduce adversarial learning into learned video compression to improve perceptual realism at low bitrates. Mentzer et al. [17] proposed the first GAN-based neural video compression system, where detail synthesis is conditioned on the warped previous reconstruction and then propagated through optical flow, showing that adversar-ial learning is crucial for subjective visual quality. Yang et al. [18] proposed a recurrent conditional GAN-based video codec, in which the discriminator is conditioned on spatial-temporal features, encouraging reconstructions to be both photo-realistic and temporally coherent. Li et al. [19] proposed to improve visual fidelity by enhancing newly emerged regions with confidence-based feature reconstruction and suppressing checkerboard artifacts with a periodic compensation loss. Du et al. [20] proposed to combine contextual coding, transformer-convolution autoencoders, entropy modeling, and GAN-based perceptual enhancement for generative video compression. Liang et al. [21] proposed a rank-chain GAN loss function to improve perceptual quality across variable bitrates. These methods demonstrate that adversarial learning can effectively improve perceptual quality. Vector-quantization-based methods shift video compression from the pixel domain to a generative latent or token space. Qi et al. [22] proposed a method that performs image and video compression in the latent space of a generative VQ-VAE, showing that such a space is sparser, more semantic, JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 3

and better aligned with perceptual quality than pixel space at ultra-low bitrates. Guo et al. [23] further explored continu-ous generative tokenization for perceptual video compression, where a pretrained tokenizer projects video frames into a perceptually aligned latent domain and the codec is redesigned around this representation. Zheng et al. [24] proposed to represent videos with compact one-dimensional latent tokens, allowing the codec to reduce spatial redundancy and model long-term temporal context through a lightweight memory mechanism. Dang et al. [25] pursued real-time generative video compression by designing a token-based codec with efficient generative reconstruction. These methods show that generative token spaces can improve perceptual compression by representing videos with more compact and semantically meaningful units. Visual autoregressive-based methods formulate generative video compression as coarse-to-fine token modeling. Li et al. [26] proposed to encode videos into hierarchical residual to-ken maps, where coarse tokens are transmitted for progressive reconstruction and fine-scale tokens can be predicted by an autoregressive context model at the decoder. This design links progressive transmission, entropy modeling, and generative detail synthesis within a token prediction framework. Diffusion-based methods exploit diffusion or flow-based pri-ors to synthesize perceptually plausible details at low bitrates. Mao et al. [27] used a video diffusion prior for sequence-level generative refinement, improving temporal stability over frame-wise generative enhancement. Gao et al. [28] incor-porated diffusion sampling into implicit neural representa-tions for coarse-to-fine spatiotemporal reconstruction. Zhang et al. [29] designed a spatio-temporal diffusion framework to re-duce error accumulation and handle semantic mutation during low-bitrate reconstruction. Xue et al. [30] developed a single-step diffusion codec with semantic-temporal guidance to im-prove reconstruction efficiency and temporal coherence. Ma et al. [31]–[33] studied real-time and one-step diffusion-based perceptual video compression, reducing the sampling cost of diffusion decoders while maintaining perceptual quality. Li et al. [34] embedded temporal reference information into a one-step diffusion compressor to better exploit spatiotempo-ral correlations. Other methods improve controllability by transmitting compact semantic or structural cues. Wang et al. [35] used textual, appearance, and motion cues to condition diffusion reconstruction, and Wang et al. [36] introduced sparse semantic motion trajectories to guide generative video coding. These methods show the strong potential of diffusion priors for perceptual video compression, especially at ultra-low bitrates. Training-free methods reuse pretrained generative priors for video compression without updating model parameters. Ling et al. [37] used a video diffusion model to formulate video coding as latent trajectory compression, where reverse-channel coding transmits stochastic choices and adaptive inter-group mechanisms improve temporal coherence. Zeng et al. [38] transformed a deterministic rectified-flow sampler into a stochastic coding process, allowing compact codebook-selected innovations to steer a frozen video generator toward the source video. Gao et al. [39] used pretrained autore-gressive diffusion priors and represented predictive frames through compact combinations of reproducible codebook vec-tors. These methods show that frozen generative models can be used as powerful decoders and can be steered by compact side information without codec training. Overall, existing generative video compression methods have substantially advanced low-bitrate video reconstruction by introducing powerful generative priors. However, they are mainly developed for natural videos, where the source is treated as an observed signal to be approximated under a bitrate constraint. Even with advanced video generation models, the selection and allocation of transmitted information are often guided by latent errors of the observed signal. This differs from AI-generated videos, where the source itself is a sample from a learned generative distribution and many local stochastic details are replaceable rather than essen-tial. Therefore, AI-generated video compression requires a semantic-aware coding principle that prioritizes spatiotemporal information critical to content consistency. 

B. AI-Generated Video Compression 

Recently, AI-generated content has begun to receive atten-tion in image compression. Duan et al. [40] studied neural compression for AI foundation model generated images and constructed PKU-AIGI-500K, a large-scale benchmark con-taining diverse prompts and images generated by multiple foundation models. Their benchmark shows that conventional codecs and learned codecs trained on natural images do not necessarily generalize well to AI-generated images, revealing a clear domain gap in generated-content compression. Duan et al. [41] further extended this benchmark study by analyzing the characteristics of AI-generated images and proposing a cross-attention transformer codec that exploits the relationship between generated images and their text prompts. Chen et al. [42] explored a different direction by using Stable Diffusion as a cross-modal decoder for layered AI-generated image compression. Their method represents an image with semantic, structural, and texture layers, showing that a generative model can act as a decoder when compact multimodal priors are transmitted. These works provide important evidence that AI-generated content deserves specialized compression designs. However, compression specifically designed for AI-generated videos remains largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first unified compression and editing framework for AI-generated videos.

A negative BD-rate indicates bitrate savings relative to the anchor codec, whereas a positive value indicates an increase in bitrate.

VI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

In this paper, we presented the first unified compression and editing framework specifically designed for AI-generated videos. Rather than treating an AI-generated video as afixed natural signal whose every detail must be reproduced, our framework exploits its generative nature by using afrozen video generator to restore replaceable variations while transmitting compact side information that preserves source-specific spatiotemporal semantics. Within this framework, we developed three spatiotemporal semantic importance-guided techniques to determine what information should be trans-mitted, how the coding budget should be allocated across latent frames, and how the transmitted information should be used for reconstruction and editing. Experiments on videos produced by five representative generation models demonstrate favorable rate–perception performance over traditional, neural, and generative video codecs. They further show that the same compressed bitstream can support both faithful reconstruction and structure-preserving prompt-based editing. These benefits currently come at the cost of relatively high computational complexity. For 720 p videos, the average encoding and decoding times are approximately 5.4 and 4.6

seconds per frame on a single NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 GPU, respectively, mainly because both processes require iterative inference through the frozen video generator. This cost currently limits real-time deployment. However, recent advances in lightweight, on-device, and real-time video gen-eration models [65]–[69] provide promising opportunities to reduce generative inference costs. Future work will adapt the proposed framework to more efficient video generators and optimize its sampling and innovation-search processes for resource-constrained and real-time platforms.
